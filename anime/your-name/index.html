The Sacred Cartography of Your Name

君の名は。聖地巡礼

A Cultural and Topographical Dossier of the Suwa and Hida Regions

Home › Anime Pilgrimage › Your Name

The 2016 release of Makoto Shinkai’s animated masterpiece, Your Name (Kimi no Na wa), irrevocably altered the landscape of Japanese cultural tourism. By grounding a cosmic, time-bending narrative in hyper-detailed, real-world topography, the film ignited an unprecedented wave of seichi junrei (anime pilgrimage). The fictional town of Itomori is a brilliant composite: its sweeping, crater-lake topography is unequivocally modeled after Lake Suwa in Nagano Prefecture, while its architectural vernacular and municipal layout are meticulously drawn from Hida Furukawa in Gifu Prefecture.

💡 3 Core Highlights for International Travelers

  • The Atmospheric Reality of "Kataware-doki": Stand at Tateishi Park overlooking Lake Suwa at twilight—the exact visual blueprint for the film's climax.
  • Accessing Prehistoric Animism: Witness the 1,200-year-old cosmology of Suwa Taisha, a shrine complex that worships mountains and ancient trees without a traditional main hall.
  • Immersive "Seichi Junrei": Walk the preserved Edo-period streets of Hida Furukawa, matching exact anime frames from the train station to the local library.

Lake Suwa: The Cosmic Lake and "Kataware-doki"

The visual anchor of Itomori is its serene, circular lake. The undeniable physical model for this geography is Lake Suwa, Nagano's largest lake. The optimal vantage point to observe this cinematic panorama is Tateishi Park (立石公園), perched on the eastern slopes.

It is here during twilight that the concept of kataware-doki manifests. This fictional term is a brilliant linguistic evolution of the historical Japanese term for dusk: tasogare-doki (originating from "Who is that?"). In pre-modern Japan, fading light rendered faces indistinguishable—a dangerous boundary hour known as Omagatoki when spirits could cross into the living world. Shinkai transforms this ancient cultural anxiety into a moment of profound romantic and spiritual connection.

The Omiwatari (God's Crossing)

During severe winters, the surface of Lake Suwa freezes completely. Temperature fluctuations cause a massive, snaking ridge of fractured ice to thrust upward with a thunderous crack. According to local Shinto lore, this is the physical footprint left by the male deity of the Upper Shrine crossing the ice to visit the female deity of the Lower Shrine. This phenomenon perfectly echoes the film's central theme of divine pathways spanning impossible divides.

Suwa Taisha & Prehistoric Shinto Roots

To understand the religious mechanics of the fictional Miyamizu Shrine, one must analyze its spiritual ancestor: Suwa Taisha. Comprising four distinct complexes, it is one of the oldest Shinto sites in Japan, with roots extending well over 1,200 years into the prehistoric Jomon period.

Most major Shinto shrines feature a Honden (central sanctuary). Suwa Taisha, remarkably, lacks a Honden in three of its four complexes. Instead, the architecture is designed to frame and worship the raw natural environment—Mount Moriya, ancient cedar trees, and yews. This is the essence of pre-Shinto nature worship: the belief that Kami saturate the rocks and trees.

The Onbashira Festival

The spiritual perimeter of the shrines is defined by the Onbashira—sixteen colossal wooden pillars (four per shrine). Every six years, sixteen massive fir trees (over 150 years old, 17 meters long, weighing up to 12 tons) are manually felled and ridden by local men down steep mountain slopes during the perilous Yamadashi (mountain descent) phase. This staggering communal effort perfectly mirrors the film's theme of collective community action required to stave off a celestial disaster.

🍶 Kuchikamizake & The Miyamizu Rituals

In the film, Mitsuha ferments Kuchikamizake (mouth-chewed sake). This is a highly accurate depiction of one of the world's oldest fermentation methods. Historical texts like the Osumi no Kuni Fudoki (713 CE) document how salivary amylase breaks down rice starches for wild yeast fermentation. When Taki later drinks this aged sake, he ingests her biological and spiritual essence, bridging the flow of time.

Hida Furukawa: The Physical Blueprint of Itomori

While Suwa provides the cosmic topography, the physical streets and infrastructure of Itomori are painstakingly replicated from Hida Furukawa in Gifu Prefecture. Moving through the town is a surreal experience of walking inside a cinematic frame.

Film Scene Real-World Location
Taki realizes Itomori is in Hida. Hida-Furukawa Station: Travelers wait on the overpass footbridge before 9:57 AM to capture the exact alignment of a passing train.
Taki researches the comet disaster. Hida City Library: The exact reading desks seen in the film. The library welcomes fans with a dedicated guestbook (request a photo permit).
Taki visits local shrines. Keta Wakamiya Shrine: Its steep stone stairway and imposing red torii gate serve as the visual model.
The group eats local street food. Ajidokoro Furukawa: The exact eatery where the characters consume Gohei-mochi.

The Seto River Canal & Koi Migration: The visual identity of Hida Furukawa is defined by the Setogawa Canal, inhabited by over 1,000 massive koi carp. In late November, local volunteers physically net and transport all 1,000 carp to warmer winter holding waters, ceremoniously returning them in early April.

Kumihimo Weaving: At the Hida Furukawa Sakura Bussan-kan, international visitors can sit at traditional wooden stands and weave their own Kumihimo (braided cords), crafting a tangible replica of the "red thread of fate" that binds the protagonists.
