ANIME PILGRIMAGE ／ アニメ聖地巡礼

The Real Ryomen Sukuna: Jujutsu Kaisen's "King of Curses" vs. Japanese Mythology in Hida Takayama

Within the fictional narrative of the global hit anime Jujutsu Kaisen, Ryomen Sukuna is feared as the "King of Curses." He is depicted as a sadistic, four-armed, two-faced sorcerer whose overwhelming cursed energy drives the core conflict of the story. For millions of international fans, this terrifying image is the definitive Ryomen Sukuna.

However, if you travel to the mountainous Hida region in Gifu Prefecture, asking locals about the "King of Curses" will likely be met with confusion. In this quiet, historical pocket of Japan, Ryomen Sukuna is not a demon, but a beloved divine protector. The historical reality of Sukuna is a fascinating, complex tapestry woven from imperial political propaganda, profound regional pride, agricultural reverence, and—surprisingly—a 21st-century digital urban legend.

The Meaning of "Ryomen": The Symbolism of Absolute Vigilance

To understand the true Sukuna, we must first look at his name. "Ryomen Sukuna" (両面宿儺) contains the characters for "both" or "two" (Ryo) and "face" (Men), directly translating to "Two-Faced." While modern Western interpretations might associate "two-faced" with deceit, in the context of ancient Japanese animism and leadership, it was a powerful symbol of absolute vigilance.

It symbolized a chieftain who possessed superhuman situational awareness—someone who could "look in all directions" simultaneously, ensuring no enemy could launch a surprise attack against his people. Furthermore, his legendary four arms represented the ultimate form of localized multi-tasking: wielding martial weapons (like a bow and sword) to defend the province from invaders, while simultaneously using agricultural tools to clear land and ensure bountiful harvests for his followers.

History Written by the Victors: The Nihon Shoki Propaganda

The stark difference between Hida's hero and the monstrous villain stems directly from who was writing the history books. The earliest official historical mention of Ryomen Sukuna is found in the Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan), compiled by the Yamato imperial court in 720 AD.

During this era, the Yamato court was aggressively expanding its territory, subduing independent regional leaders. The imperial government systematically rewrote history to justify their military conquests. Human political rivals who refused to bow to the Emperor were often dehumanized and recorded in official texts as grotesque monsters, demons, or "earth spiders" (Tsuchigumo).

The Nihon Shoki frames Sukuna as an irredeemable, multi-limbed monster who oppressed the people and rebelled against the Emperor, requiring the heroic imperial general Takefurukuma to slay him. In stark contrast, local Hida legends passed down through generations credit Sukuna as a benevolent leader, an introducer of advanced agriculture, and a hero who successfully banished a toxic dragon (the poisonous serpent-deity Shichina) to save the local populace.

The 2005 Internet Urban Legend: The True Inspiration for Jujutsu Kaisen

If the historical Ryomen Sukuna was a noble chieftain, why does the Jujutsu Kaisen version resemble a mummified, cannibalistic curse? The answer lies not in ancient mythology, but in modern digital folklore.

In 2005, a highly detailed and deeply unsettling horror story was posted on 2chan, Japan's largest anonymous imageboard. The post detailed the discovery of a sealed wooden box in a demolished Iwate Prefecture temple. Inside was an ancient, horrific mummy of conjoined twins with two faces and four arms, supposedly created through a dark, cannibalistic ritual (Kodoku) during the Taisho era to be used as a cursed object to curse a rival nation.

The anime's depiction of a mummified entity with a gruesome origin perfectly maps onto this 21st-century internet creepypasta. Jujutsu Kaisen creator Gege Akutami masterfully lifted the aesthetic and the "cursed object" concept from this viral urban legend, adopting the name of the ancient Hida chieftain to give it historical weight. Therefore, the anime Sukuna is a hybrid: the name of a 4th-century hero attached to a 2005 internet horror story.

Real-Life Sacred Spots: Anime Pilgrimage Locations in Hida

For international fans seeking the true roots of the legend, the Hida region offers a profound cultural pilgrimage that contrasts sharply with the bloody world of the anime.

Senko-ji Temple (Takayama)

Located at an altitude of 900 meters on Mount Kesayama, Senko-ji is widely considered the epicenter of Ryomen Sukuna worship in Japan. According to temple legends, Sukuna himself founded the temple approximately 1,600 years ago.

The most famous artifact housed here is the wooden Ryomen Sukuna Seated Statue, carved around 1685 by the famous itinerant monk Enku. Enku took creative liberty with the mythology. Instead of a front-and-back face, he carved Sukuna's two faces side-by-side. This brilliant artistic choice allows viewers to see both a compassionate expression (representing the merciful leader) and a fierce expression (representing the fierce protector) simultaneously. Enku also carved Sukuna holding a large broadaxe—a deliberate and deeply respectful homage to the legendary master carpenters of Hida, whom Sukuna is said to have patronized.

Zendou-ji Temple & Nyukawa-cho

Nearby, Zendou-ji temple also honors Sukuna, claiming he introduced Buddhism to the area long before it officially reached the Yamato court. The entire Nyukawa-cho district in Takayama proudly embraces their ancient hero, occasionally featuring him in local festivals not as a King of Curses, but as the founding father of their regional identity.

Visiting these sites offers a rare opportunity to see how history, propaganda, and pop culture intersect, transforming a regional protector into a mythical monster, and eventually, into a global anime icon.
