01. Introduction: The King of Curses vs. The Hida Protector

Within the fictional narrative of the global mega-hit anime Jujutsu Kaisen, Ryomen Sukuna (両面宿儺) is universally feared as the "King of Curses" (呪いの王). He is depicted as a sadistic, four-armed, two-faced sorcerer whose overwhelming, cataclysmic cursed energy drives the core conflict of the series. For millions of international fans worldwide, this terrifying, bloodthirsty image is the definitive definition of Ryomen Sukuna.

However, if you take a journey to the misty, mountainous Hida region in Gifu Prefecture—specifically around Takayama—and ask local residents or temple priests about the "King of Curses," your inquiry will be met with polite confusion, or perhaps gentle correction. In this quiet, historical pocket of Japan, Ryomen Sukuna is not remembered as a demonic tyrant, but as a revered divine protector, an agricultural pioneer, and a local hero.

"History is a set of lies agreed upon." — The divergence between the imperial records of the Yamato court and the oral traditions of Hida encapsulates how ancient politics weaponized mythology.

The historical reality of Ryomen Sukuna is a fascinating, multifaceted tapestry woven from imperial political propaganda, profound regional pride, ancient agricultural animism, and—most surprisingly—a 21st-century digital urban legend born on an anonymous Japanese imageboard.

02. The Meaning of "Ryomen": Symbolism of Two Faces and Four Arms

To untangle the myth, we must first examine the literal meaning of his name. "Ryomen Sukuna" (両面宿儺) translates directly to "Two-Faced Sukuna." While modern Western and contemporary horror tropes automatically associate "two-faced" with deceit, hypocrisy, or treachery, in the context of ancient Japanese animism and tribal leadership, possessing multiple faces and limbs carried an entirely different symbolic weight.

In ancient folklore, a leader with two faces represented absolute vigilance and all-encompassing awareness. It symbolized a chieftain or regional ruler who could "look in all directions" simultaneously, ensuring that no surprise attack from rival clans or foreign invaders could catch his people off guard. Furthermore, his legendary four arms represented the ultimate embodiment of multi-tasking leadership: holding martial weapons (such as a bow and sword) in two hands to defend the province, while simultaneously wielding agricultural tools (like a hoe and sickle) with the other two hands to clear wilderness, irrigate fields, and guarantee bountiful harvests for his followers.

03. History Written by Victors: The Nihon Shoki Propaganda

Why is there such an astronomical chasm between Hida's benevolent guardian and the imperial monster? The answer lies in the oldest rule of historiography: history is written by the victors. The earliest official written mention of Ryomen Sukuna appears in the Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan), compiled under imperial commission in 720 AD.

During the Asuka and Nara periods, the central Yamato imperial court was aggressively expanding its military and political hegemony across the Japanese archipelago. Independent regional leaders who refused to submit to the Emperor's authority were systematically vilified, dehumanized, and recorded in official state chronicles as grotesque monsters, demons, or rebel chieftains.

The Nihon Shoki (specifically under the reign of the legendary Emperor Nintoku) records that in the year 65 AD, a strange man appeared in Hida province. He possessed a single torso with two distinct faces facing opposite directions, each head having its own set of eyes and mouth. He had no crown or heels, but possessed four arms and four legs, each pair operating independently. He was exceptionally swift, wielding swords and bows with deadly proficiency. Crucially, the imperial text states that he "refused to obey the Imperial Court and plundered the people," prompting the Emperor to dispatch the brave general Takefurukuma-no-mikoto to subjugate and slay him.

In stark contrast, local Hida oral traditions and temple records tell an entirely opposite story. According to the people of Hida, Sukuna was a wise local chieftain who introduced advanced irrigation, established trade routes, and successfully banished a poisonous, dragon-like serpent terrorizing the valleys. The "rebellion" was nothing more than Hida's stubborn resistance against imperial annexation, punished by having their hero branded as a multi-limbed freak in government archives.

04. The 2005 2chan Urban Legend: The True Blueprint for Akutami's Creation

If the historical Ryomen Sukuna was a noble tribal leader, how did he transform into the mummified, cannibalistic curse seen in Jujutsu Kaisen? The chilling aesthetic of Akutami's Sukuna—complete with conjoined mummified bodies, four arms, two faces, and cursed object finger-sealing—did not come from the 8th-century Nihon Shoki at all. It was born in the digital underground of 2005.

In 2005, a remarkably detailed, spine-chilling horror story was published anonymously on 2chan (Futaba Channel), Japan's massive imageboard network. The post claimed that workers demolishing an old, abandoned temple in Iwate Prefecture discovered a sealed wooden box. Inside was a horrifying mummy of conjoined twins possessing two faces and four arms, purportedly created via a dark, forbidden cannibalistic ritual (known as Kodoku) during the Taisho era to serve as an ultimate cursed object.

Gege Akutami masterfully lifted the core aesthetic, body horror, and "sealed cursed object" mechanic directly from this 21st-century digital creepypasta, affixing the historical name of "Ryomen Sukuna" to give the narrative immediate gravitas and mythical resonance. Thus, the anime Sukuna is a brilliant modern hybrid: an 8th-century imperial political scapegoat fused with a 2005 internet urban legend.

05. Real-Life Pilgrimage Spots: Senko-ji and Zendou-ji in Hida Takayama

For international fans and cultural travelers wishing to witness the true roots of the legend, the Hida region offers an enriching, peaceful pilgrimage that stands in poetic contrast to the carnage of the anime.

Senko-ji Temple (Takayama, Gifu)

Perched dramatically at an altitude of 900 meters on Mount Kesayama, Senko-ji is universally recognized as the epicenter of Ryomen Sukuna worship. Temple lore maintains that Sukuna himself founded the sanctuary approximately 1,600 years ago.

The temple's crown jewel is the sacred wooden Ryomen Sukuna Seated Statue, sculpted around 1685 by the legendary itinerant monk-sculptor Enku. Eschewing the terrifying imperial description, Enku carved Sukuna's two faces side-by-side: one side radiates deep compassion and wisdom (representing the benevolent ruler), while the other displays fierce, unyielding protection (representing the defender of the province). Enku also carved Sukuna grasping a heavy carpenter's broadaxe—a profound, deliberate tribute to the master woodworkers and carpenters of Hida, whose guilds revered Sukuna as their patron deity.

Zendou-ji Temple

Located nearby in Hida, Zendou-ji further preserves the legacy of Sukuna, maintaining records that he introduced early spiritual teachings and agricultural innovations to the isolated mountain basin long before Yamato imperial influence penetrated the Alps.

06. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is Ryomen Sukuna a real historical person?

Historians generally agree that Ryomen Sukuna was likely a composite archetype representing powerful local chieftains or tribal leaders in the Hida region who resisted the centralization and taxation policies of the early Yamato imperial state.

Can you visit Ryomen Sukuna's grave or statues in Japan?

Yes. The primary pilgrimage site is Senko-ji Temple in Takayama, Gifu Prefecture, where Enku's famous double-faced statue is housed. Several local shrines and caves in the Hida mountains are also associated with his legends.

Does Jujutsu Kaisen accurately portray Japanese mythology?

No. While the name "Ryomen Sukuna" and his physical attributes (two faces, four arms) are borrowed from Japanese folklore and the Nihon Shoki, his personality as a murderous king of curses and his specific backstory in the manga are largely fictional creations by Gege Akutami, heavily influenced by 21st-century internet horror folklore.
