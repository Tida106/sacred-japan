Demon Slayer Pilgrimage: What's Real, What's Fan-Made

Asakusa, Mount Kumotori, two Kamado shrines and a wisteria park

Here is what I tell Demon Slayer fans at Kamado Shrine in Dazaifu: it is a real, beautiful shrine, and it is not officially connected to the series. The shared name is a coincidence that fans have happily adopted. A good pilgrimage works best when you know which stops are on screen and which are fan-made, so I have sorted them for you.

💡 Quick Visitor Snapshot

  • Easiest: Asakusa in Tokyo, the Taisho-era city where Tanjiro meets Muzan.
  • Most famous fan stop: Homan-gu Kamado Shrine in Dazaifu, Fukuoka. Fan-linked, not official.
  • For hikers: Mount Kumotori, widely cited as the area of Tanjiro's home.

Asakusa: The Taisho-Era Metropolis

In the anime, Tanjiro is overwhelmed by Asakusa's lights and towering buildings, and the history behind it is real. Taisho-era Asakusa was Tokyo's entertainment capital, and the tower in the background of those scenes is the Ryounkaku. When he seeks refuge at a small yatai food cart and orders yamakake udon, he is stepping into a street-food culture that still exists in a different form around Senso-ji today.

Asakusa’s best-known landmark, and a good place to start the walk. The giant red lantern hangs in the center, and the two guardian statues stand in the side bays: the wind god Fujin on one side and the thunder god Raijin on the other. The green plaque above reads Kinryuzan, the mountain name of Senso-ji. Everyone stops here for a photo, so the wide square in front of the gate is the place to stand.

Walk through the gate and Nakamise-dori begins, a shopping street of about 250 meters lined with snack and souvenir stalls. This photo shows the lantern from the street side, framed by autumn-leaf decorations and rows of white lanterns, and it also shows how crowded it gets. For a street-food scene like Tanjiro’s udon stall, the early morning is much calmer.

Look up and the gate becomes a piece of carpentry: row upon row of rafters under the red-painted roof, with blue-and-pink cloud-patterned brackets at the corners. The gate was lost in a fire in 1865 and the current one dates from 1960, a reconstruction in concrete built to look like the wooden original, so the Taisho-era Asakusa of the anime had no Kaminarimon at all. It is a good detail to point out if you only have a minute.

🗼 The Ryounkaku (Asakusa Twelve Stories)

The towering octagonal brick building visible in the anime background is the Ryounkaku. Completed on November 11, 1890, by Scottish engineer W.K. Burton, it was Japan's first skyscraper and featured the country's first electric elevators. Though it survived the 1894 earthquake, it suffered catastrophic damage during the Great Kanto Earthquake on September 1, 1923, and was demolished shortly after.

Kamado Shrine in Dazaifu: A Real Shrine, an Unofficial Connection

Homan-gu Kamado Shrine sits at the foot of Mount Homan, and fans link it to the story through the shared name, mountain-ascetic imagery and the author's Fukuoka roots. Neither Kyushu Kamado shrine is an officially confirmed model, so go for the place itself, which is lovely.

These two photos show the same hall on a quiet moment and a busy one. The roof sweeps down in a deep curve, with a thick straw rope hung with white paper streamers across the front, and a stone komainu guards the steps. The paper lanterns carry what looks like a red plum-blossom crest, a nod to the Dazaifu area’s plum tradition. Photos tend to be easiest in the morning, before the lines form.

A short walk from the main hall, a line of vermilion torii runs through the trees toward a small Inari shrine, and the plaque on the first gate reads Shikibu Inari Okami. It is only a few steps long, but under the summer leaves it is the most photogenic corner of the grounds, and a good place to slow down and look at the forest that the shrine sits in.

The Yamabushi and the Ichimatsu Pattern

Mount Homan was a major training ground for Yamabushi (mountain ascetics practicing Shugendo). Their traditional ceremonial robes, called suzukake, feature the distinctive Ichimatsu (checkerboard) pattern. This is widely considered the direct historical precursor to Tanjiro's iconic green and black checkered haori.

Both shrines are active places of worship. Bow at the torii, keep your voice down and follow the photography signs. Dazaifu Tenmangu is nearby, so you can combine them; I have a full Dazaifu Tenmangu guide.

Planning the Trip

💡 Essential Travel Tip: Stay Connected
Navigating from central Fukuoka out to Mount Homan requires taking local trains and community buses. We highly recommend getting a travel eSIM by Simsima before your flight to access live Google Maps transit times. It activates instantly upon landing.

For wisteria at Ashikaga, check the bloom forecast before you commit, since timing varies from year to year. Asakusa is a half-day stop in Tokyo, Dazaifu is a half day from Fukuoka, and Mount Kumotori is a full day for prepared hikers only.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Kamado Shrine the official model for Demon Slayer?
No. Neither Homan-gu Kamado Shrine in Dazaifu nor the one in Beppu is an officially confirmed model. Fans link them through the shared Kamado name.
Where is Tanjiro's home in real life?
Mount Kumotori, on the Tokyo, Saitama and Yamanashi border, is widely cited as the area. It is a real mountain hike, not a casual visit.
Which Demon Slayer location is easiest to visit?
Asakusa in Tokyo. It is directly reachable by subway, and it matches the Taisho-era city in the series.
Can I combine Kamado Shrine with Dazaifu Tenmangu?
Yes. They are both in Dazaifu, and the Kamado shrine is about 10 minutes by bus from Nishitetsu Dazaifu.
