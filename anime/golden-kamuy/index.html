Golden Kamuy Pilgrimage: Otaru, Abashiri and the Ainu Culture Behind the Story

ゴールデンカムイ 聖地巡礼

The Hokkaido places behind the gold hunt: an Otaru canal, a pioneer village, a prison museum, and where to meet the real Ainu culture

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

Most anime pilgrimages are about one shrine or one street. Golden Kamuy is different: it is a map of an entire island. Satoru Noda's manga, serialized in Weekly Young Jump from 2014 to 2022, follows a veteran of the Russo-Japanese War and an Ainu girl across Hokkaido in search of hidden gold. As a guide, I like it because every stop is a real place with real history, and because it has sent a lot of visitors toward the Ainu culture, which is the part of Hokkaido I most want you to meet.

💡 Quick Visitor Snapshot

  • Easiest pairing: Otaru and the Historical Village of Hokkaido, both within reach of Sapporo.
  • Furthest stop: Abashiri Prison Museum in northeastern Hokkaido, facing the Sea of Okhotsk. Plan it as its own trip.
  • For the Ainu culture: Upopoy in Shiraoi, near Chitose Airport.
  • Best season: Summer for horse-drawn carriages at the village, winter for the snow scenes and horse-drawn sleighs.

Otaru: The Canal, the Bank and the Herring Mansion

Otaru is where I would start. It has a history of Ainu settlements and a 19th-century herring boom that built grand fishermen's houses called banya. SoraNews24 describes Otaru as close to Asirpa's village and as Lieutenant Tsurumi's base of operations in the story, and says the author modeled many locations on real Otaru buildings.

The canal at blue hour, with the gas-style lamps along the bank already lit and the warehouses reflected in still water. The stone warehouses on the right are the reason Otaru looks the way it does: this was a trading port, and the canal is where goods were moved between ships and storehouses. Snow is still piled along the left bank, so this was taken in the cold season.

A summer night on the canal. The brick warehouse on the right, partly covered in ivy, is lit from below, and the lamps along the left bank are doubled in the water. The photo has been processed to bring out the colors, so the real scene is a little calmer, but the mood of a warm evening walk along the water is accurate.

Walk the canal area first, then go five minutes to the former bank that is now the Otaru Romankan glass shop. Finish with a sweet dumpling at Otaru Niikuraya. The Former Aoyama Villa, the Herring Mansion, has an attached restaurant that serves herring soba, but photography is not allowed inside, so do not plan your photo stop there. For opening hours and fees, check each facility's official page, since the SoraNews24 article does not give them.

The same canal in February, gray and quiet, with people strolling the snowy bank on the left. If you are following a story set in the Hokkaido cold, winter is when Otaru feels closest to it. Dress for it: the wind off the water is sharp.

A winter night with strings of blue lights along the snow-covered bank, which looks like a seasonal lighting display. Icicles hang from the eaves of the warehouse on the right. Lighting events change from year to year, so check the Otaru tourism site before you plan around one.

Historical Village of Hokkaido

This open-air museum on the edge of Sapporo holds relocated and restored buildings from Hokkaido's Meiji to early Showa years. Many buildings appear in the manga, including a soba shop, a hotel and a barber shop, and a winter night scene with a horse-drawn sleigh in the live-action film was shot here. Horse-drawn carriages run in summer and sleighs in winter. The village is about 30 minutes by car from central Sapporo, according to one local travel site; check the official page for the bus route and tickets.

The large cream building with a red roof that you meet first, as far as I can tell the village's main building near the entrance. Its decorative half-timbering and iron rooftop railing give a first hint of the Western-influenced style of Hokkaido's Meiji era, when the island was being developed quickly. Everything beyond this point is a relocated or restored building from that period.

A photo studio with a tall glass skylight in the roof, where daylight lit the sitter. The white sign on the right advertises photography services, including on-location and night shooting, and reads as a photographer's name, which I read as Hirose (廣瀬). It is a small reminder that the village is not only farms and shops but the whole fabric of a pioneer-era town.

A dairy-style barn with a blue roof and a stone silo, showing the farming side of the village. Hokkaido was opened to settlement partly to farm it, and the American-style barns that appeared here are one of the island's visual signatures. I cannot tell you whether this exact barn appears in the manga, so treat it as an example of the setting, not a confirmed scene.

If you only have a day near Sapporo, this is the stop that feels most like walking into a page of the manga.

A white horse pulling a tram along the rails on the village's main street, with a small brick building on the left and bare trees in early spring. This is what I mean by walking into a page of the story. Operating days and seasons change, so check the village's official page before you count on a ride.

The same cream-and-red building from the side on an overcast day. Weather in Hokkaido can turn quickly even in summer, so bring a layer.

Abashiri Prison Museum

The story begins with tattoos on prisoners' bodies that, together, show where the gold is hidden. The real Abashiri Prison in northeastern Hokkaido is now open as a museum about the prisoners who built roads and other facilities that helped develop the island. Treat it as a history museum first. The story is fiction; the labor was real.

The wooden bridge on the approach, with the museum's entrance buildings ahead. Walking across it on a clear summer morning feels cheerful, which is a strange contrast with what the museum is about.

The same bridge in February, with a worker shoveling a path through the snow. Abashiri is in northeastern Hokkaido and winters are long and cold, which is the weather the story's early chapters imagine.

The gate pillars with two wooden signboards: one for the preservation foundation that runs the site and one reading Museum Abashiri Prison (博物館 網走監獄). The red-brick building with two domed towers is visible through the bars.

The red-brick entrance building with its central arch and two gray domes, with the brick path leading straight to it. The signs beside the arch read Museum Abashiri Prison (博物館 網走監獄) on the left and Abashiri Prison (網走刑務所) on the right. Figures dressed as a guard and a worker stand by the entrance, which are display figures, not real people.

A statue of a bent figure in a straw hat in the courtyard in front of the entrance. It stands for the labor that this place is known for: prisoners cleared land and built roads. The story is fiction, but this is the labor it points to.

The entrance in winter, with snow on the domes. This is a small, low-resolution photo, so I show it at its natural size.

Abashiri is far from Sapporo, so I would not squeeze it into a day trip. Plan a night or two in the area.

A long wooden cell block lit from skylights above, with cell doors down both sides. A plate on the right appears to give a cell number. This is the museum's best-known interior, and it is a quiet, slightly heavy place to walk through.

Another view of a cell block, with a brick floor and warm light from the skylights. The color is warmer than most photos of this corridor, so editing may have enhanced it, but the layout is the same.

The octagonal central watch post (中央見張). The cell blocks radiate from this point, so a guard standing here could look down several wings at once. Signs on the walls mark the wings by number.

A museum display with figures of prisoners in orange robes eating a meal while an officer in a white uniform watches. They are mannequins, and the scene gives you a sense of daily life more than any sign could.

Hakodate and Goryokaku

The final chapters are tied to Hakodate, and the star-shaped Goryokaku fort, Japan's first Western-style fortress, is shown in the story. Hijikata Toshizo, who in history was a real commander of the last battle of the Boshin War, appears here as a living character. I would not try to match specific scenes; go for the fort itself. Our full Goryokaku guide covers the real history, the tower and the cherry blossoms.

Meeting the Real Ainu Culture

Kamuy is the Ainu word for deities, and the Ainu are the indigenous people of Hokkaido. The manga's Ainu language was supervised by Hiroshi Nakagawa, an Ainu-language linguist at Chiba University, and the series has been recognized for its social impact: it won the Social Impact Award in the Manga Division at the 24th Japan Media Arts Festival.

A manga is still a manga, so if you want to learn about the Ainu people and their culture, go to a place built for that. Upopoy, the National Ainu Museum and Park in Shiraoi, opened in 2020 as a national center for the revival and promotion of Ainu culture. A tourism campaign tied to the series ran there from September 2025 to February 2026, but check the official site for current exhibitions, hours and tickets. Please go as a guest and listen first.

The National Ainu Museum seen from the approach path at dusk, with its long, angular gray roof over a stone base. The pattern of lighter tiles on the end wall looks like an Ainu-style design, but I could not confirm that from the file. The photo is from 2020, the year Upopoy opened, and the surroundings may look different now.

A reconstructed chise, the traditional Ainu house, with walls and roof thatched in reeds and tied down with horizontal bands. The board in front is titled 「チセの建築」 (Building a Cise), and a raised storehouse stands behind it on the right. Photographed in late autumn, which is why the grass is dry and the trees are bare.

The inside of a chise, as Commons titles the file. The walls are lined with woven mats in black, red and natural reed, and bundles hang from the roof beams; the Commons description calls them inau. Imagine this lit by the fire and you have the room the manga's Ainu characters sit in. The light is dim, so expect something like this rather than a bright exhibition hall.

A reed-walled room with a woven panel in a bold diamond-and-zigzag pattern, a white garment with curling embroidered lines hung on the wall, and a bent-wood frame on the floor. The photo is slightly blurred, so I am using it for the atmosphere rather than the detail. The labels were not legible, so I will not name the objects.

A square hearth sunk into the floor of a large reed-walled hall, with hanging wooden fixtures above and benches along the far wall. Commons describes the hearth in this series as an irori, the Japanese word for it. In an Ainu house the hearth is the center of the room, and I would treat it with that respect. This photo is also slightly blurred.

The same kind of hearth with live charcoal glowing in the ash and an iron kettle hanging above it. The white curled shavings standing by the rim look like inau, the ritual wood shavings, but that is my reading and the file does not say so. Visitors stand in the background in socks, so shoes come off in this room.

A ritual sword in the museum, with the label reading エムシ (emushi) and Ritual Sword in four languages. Its scabbard is dark and finely worked with pierced metal panels. The label calls it a ritual sword, not a weapon, so I would not read it as one.

An Ainu knife, makiri, photographed at Upopoy according to Commons. The label reads マキリ (makiri) and 小刀 (small sword), and appears to name the maker as ハムアキ (牟田章秋), so this is a modern work, not an old heirloom. The handle and sheath are carved with scrolls and fine cross-hatching, and a red cord ties the sheath to a pale antler toggle. I have not checked how this knife relates to any scene in the story, so take it as an example of the craft.

A wide dark basin with four long handles tipped with metal, as Commons titles it. It is carved with fine patterns on the sides. I cannot tell you its use or age from the photo, so check the label in the museum.

A bark fiber garment, as Commons titles it, in a warm brown woven cloth with dark blue bands and blue-green patches stitched with fine white lines. The cloth looks rough and strong, which is the point: this is clothing for daily work. Photographed through glass, so there are some reflections.

A grass fiber garment, as Commons titles it, in a pale woven cloth with black bands and thread in green, yellow and pink in stepped, curling patterns. Compare it with the brown bark one above: the patterns differ from garment to garment. A visitor is reflected in the glass.

A seasonal ohaw set at the Upopoy restaurant Umizora no Haru (海空のハル), according to Commons. Ohaw is the Ainu word for a soup. Here it comes in a carved wooden bowl with leek and root vegetables, next to rice and a few slices of roast meat. The menu changes by season and I have not checked what is served now.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where is Golden Kamuy set in real life?
It is set in Hokkaido in the years after the Russo-Japanese War, and the story also travels to Karafuto (Sakhalin). Fans visit Otaru, the Historical Village of Hokkaido in Sapporo, Abashiri Prison Museum and Goryokaku in Hakodate.
Which Golden Kamuy location is best for a first visit?
If you have one day near Sapporo, the Historical Village of Hokkaido and Otaru are the easiest pairing. Abashiri is far to the northeast, so it needs its own trip.
Where can I learn about the Ainu culture shown in the series?
Upopoy (National Ainu Museum and Park) in Shiraoi opened in 2020 as a national center for the revival and promotion of Ainu culture. Check the official site for current hours and tickets.
Are the filming locations of the live-action film the same as the manga locations?
Only partly. Several buildings at the Historical Village of Hokkaido appear in the live-action film and are also described as models for places in the manga, but the two should not be treated as the same list.
Do I need to know the story before visiting?
No. Most of these places are worth a visit on their own, from the Otaru canal district to a prison museum with real history behind it.
⛩️

The gold in the story is fiction, but the roads, the canal and the culture behind it are real. Follow the map, and then spend an hour at Upopoy listening to the people the story borrows from.
