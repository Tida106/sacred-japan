The Shrine Most People Walk Past

The Arashiyama bamboo grove is one of the most photographed places in all of Japan. Millions of visitors walk the tall, rustling corridor every year, phones raised, moving quickly toward the light at the far end. Most of them do not notice the small black torii gate set into the bamboo on the left side of the path.

They should stop. Nonomiya Shrine sits on a site that has been sacred for over 1,200 years — used in the Heian period as a ritual purification ground for imperial princesses destined to serve as the High Priestess of Ise Grand Shrine. It appears by name in The Tale of Genji, Japan's eleventh-century novel and the world's first full-length work of fiction. Its torii gate is the oldest style in Japan, made from unstripped oak bark in a form that predates the red-lacquered gates that appear in every tourist photograph. And its moss garden, enclosing a small stone shrine at the back, is one of the quietest places in Arashiyama.

Most visitors spend twelve minutes here. Give it forty, and you arrive at something different.

📖 Why This Matters Globally

The Tale of Genji, written by court noblewoman Murasaki Shikibu around 1008 CE, is recognized by UNESCO and literary historians as the world's first novel. Nonomiya Shrine appears in Chapter 10, "The Sacred Tree" (Sakaki), making it one of the few surviving physical locations from the world's oldest novel. Walking through the kuroki torii is, in a literal sense, walking through a scene in human literary history.

The Tale of Genji & Lady Rokujō

In Chapter 10 of The Tale of Genji, Prince Genji visits Nonomiya Shrine to see Lady Rokujō, one of his great loves, who is staying at the shrine with her daughter while they prepare for the daughter's departure to Ise as the new Saiō — the Imperial High Priestess.

The scene is one of the most melancholy in the novel. Genji arrives in the autumn, in the evening, just as the maples are turning. Lady Rokujō knows their relationship is ending — she is about to leave Kyoto for years, perhaps permanently, to serve at Ise. Genji has been spending time with other women. The conversation between them at the shrine gate, surrounded by the smell of chrysanthemums and the sound of the sutras being read inside, is shot through with the kind of suppressed grief that the Japanese aesthetic of mono no aware — the poignancy of passing things — is built to describe.

What makes this remarkable, historically, is that the shrine still exists exactly where Murasaki Shikibu placed it. The bamboo grove has changed in composition but not in location. The black torii gate that Genji passed through is the same style of gate that stands here today. You can walk the precise ground of this scene from a thousand years ago.

Every October, Nonomiya Shrine holds the Saigu Gyoretsu procession, a re-enactment of the Saiō's departure from the shrine toward Ise, with participants in full Heian court dress processing from the shrine through Arashiyama to the Togetsukyo Bridge. It is one of the quieter and more genuinely moving festivals in Kyoto's autumn calendar.

The Kuroki Torii: Japan's Oldest Torii Style

Most people recognize the torii gate as a red lacquered structure — the iconic vermillion form that appears in the thousands of gates at Fushimi Inari and at the approach to major shrines across Japan. That style, while ancient, is not the oldest.

Nonomiya Shrine's gate is a kuroki torii (黒木鳥居) — a torii built from sawtooth oak with the bark left fully intact. No lacquer, no shaping, no colour treatment: just the wood as it grew, fitted into a simple post-and-lintel form. The word kuroki means "black wood," referring to the natural dark colour of the stripped-and-dried oak bark.

This is the form of torii believed to have been used at Japanese shrines before lacquering and decorative refinement were introduced from the continent. It predates the vermillion torii in the historical record. There are very few kuroki torii still standing in Japan — Nonomiya's is the most famous, and its preservation within a shrine of active ritual use, rather than a museum, makes it genuinely rare.

The gate is replaced periodically with fresh oak — traditionally every twenty years, mirroring the cycle of rebuilding at Ise Grand Shrine — so what you see is not the original gate from Genji's time, but a faithful replica of the exact same form, maintained as a living tradition rather than a relic.

Imperial Purification & the Saigu Tradition

From the late eighth century through the fourteenth century, whenever a new Saiō (斎王) — the Imperial High Priestess destined to serve at Ise Grand Shrine — was appointed from among the unmarried daughters of the Emperor, she was required to undergo a three-year purification process before departing for Ise.

Nonomiya, which translates as "field shrine" or "shrine in the wild," was used as the principal purification site during the Heian period. The princess destined for Ise would stay here with her attendants, maintaining ritual purity, avoiding contact with death, childbirth, meat, and any other source of kegare (spiritual pollution), until she was deemed ready to serve as the physical intermediary between the imperial family and the sun goddess Amaterasu at Ise.

The practice of the Saiō was suspended in the fourteenth century, but the site's association with female ritual power, with royal purification, and with the connection between the imperial lineage and the most important shrine in Japan has never been severed. The shrine continues to be visited primarily by women — for prayers related to marriage, childbirth, and safe passage through transitions — and the autumn Saigu Gyoretsu festival is one of the only annual events that re-enacts Heian-period royal female religious practice in its original setting.

The Moss Garden & the Grounds

Nonomiya Shrine's grounds are small — you can walk the perimeter in five minutes — but they are carefully maintained in a way that rewards slowing down. The principal element is the moss garden that fills the interior of the enclosure, surrounding the small subsidiary shrines on the far side of the main hall.

Kyoto moss gardens, of which there are several famous examples, depend on the city's humid climate and careful tending. Nonomiya's is modest in scale but exceptionally well-kept. The deep green carpet extends up to the bases of the small stone shrines and around the roots of the old maple and cedar trees at the back of the grounds. In autumn, when the maples turn, the combination of orange-red above and green below, against the dark wood of the shrine structures, is the kind of visual arrangement that appears in Heian-period paintings of this very place.

There is also a small stone wishing shrine (神石) near the entrance — a smooth, rounded stone that visitors rub while making a prayer. It is said that wishes made here, particularly those related to love and marriage, are granted within a year. The stone is worn smooth by centuries of hands.

The deities enshrined here cover an unusually practical range for a small shrine: marriage and childbirth, the arts, academic success, and business prosperity. The combination reflects the shrine's history as a site of royal female life — a princess who spent years in ritual preparation here would have prayed for all of these things.

Autumn at Nonomiya: Festival & Foliage

Arashiyama's autumn colour typically peaks in mid to late November, and Nonomiya's grounds participate fully. The small garden's maple trees are modest in number but perfectly positioned: they are visible through the kuroki torii gate when viewed from the bamboo-grove path, creating a frame-within-a-frame composition that has been photographed from approximately the same angle for a very long time.

The Saigu Gyoretsu festival takes place in mid-October, slightly before peak autumn colour — usually the third Sunday of the month, though the exact date varies annually. The procession assembles at Nonomiya Shrine and moves through Arashiyama to Togetsukyo Bridge, with participants in the court dress of the Heian period — layered kimonos, ox-carts, musicians with traditional instruments, and the central figure of a woman portraying the Saiō surrounded by her female attendants. It is one of the more genuinely immersive historical recreations in Kyoto's festival calendar and, being smaller than the Jidai Matsuri, is accessible without fighting for viewing space.

If you are visiting Arashiyama in November for autumn colour, the best approach is: arrive at Saga-Arashiyama Station early, walk directly to Nonomiya while the bamboo grove is empty (before 9:00 AM if possible), spend 30–40 minutes in the shrine grounds, then move to Tenryu-ji's garden when it opens at 8:30. The bamboo grove itself is best experienced in both directions — the morning light falls differently returning than approaching.

💡 The Overlooked Morning Window

The bamboo grove receives its peak crowds between 10:00 AM and 3:00 PM. Nonomiya Shrine opens at 9:00, and the path through the grove is often nearly empty before 8:30. The light in the bamboo at 7:00–8:00 AM — when shafts of morning sun angle through the stalks — is the photography moment that most visitors, arriving later by bus and taxi, never see. Take the first JR train from Kyoto Station and you will have the grove to yourself.

Getting There, What to Pray For & What to Buy

Getting there: The simplest route is JR Sagano/San'in Line from Kyoto Station to Saga-Arashiyama Station — about 15 minutes, ¥240. From the station, follow the signs toward the bamboo grove (10 minutes on foot). Nonomiya Shrine's kuroki torii gate is on the left as you enter the main bamboo-grove path. Alternatively, take the Keifuku Arashiyama Line from Shijo-Omiya to Arashiyama Station (20 minutes), or the Hankyu Arashiyama Line to Arashiyama and walk 15 minutes.

Combining with Arashiyama: Nonomiya pairs naturally with Tenryu-ji (a UNESCO World Heritage garden, 2-minute walk, ¥500 garden entry), the bamboo grove itself, and the Togetsukyo Bridge riverside walk. A half-day circuit from Saga-Arashiyama Station — shrine, bamboo, garden, bridge, lunch — is the standard and is genuinely excellent.

What to pray for: Nonomiya is particularly effective (by tradition) for marriage and relationship prayers — the same category of prayer that has been made here since the Heian period. The shrine also receives many visitors praying for success in childbirth and for academic or artistic achievement. Rub the wishing stone by the entrance for a specific desire; pray at the main hall for the broader blessings.

What to buy:

  • Enmusubi-mamori (love/marriage amulet) — ¥800: The primary purchase at Nonomiya. Available in several colours; pink is the most popular.
  • Kodakara-mamori (childbirth/safe delivery amulet) — ¥800: A longtime specialty of this shrine given its connection to imperial female life.
  • Goshuin — ¥500: The stamp includes the shrine's name in elegant calligraphy and the bamboo-grove imagery.
  • Omikuji (fortune) — ¥200: Standard paper-strip format tied to a branch or pine on the grounds.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Nonomiya Shrine famous for?
Nonomiya is famous for three things: its kuroki torii gate (Japan's oldest torii style, made from unstripped oak bark), its association with the Tale of Genji (it appears by name in Chapter 10), and its role as the Heian-period purification site for imperial princesses before their departure to serve as High Priestess of Ise. Today it is also visited for marriage and relationship prayers.
How long does a visit to Nonomiya Shrine take?
The shrine grounds are small — the perimeter takes five minutes to walk. A thorough visit, including the moss garden, the subsidiary shrines, the wishing stone, and time for prayer and goshuin collection, takes 30–40 minutes. Most visitors spend less time than this and miss the back garden.
Is the Tale of Genji connection real, or a tourist invention?
It is documented. Chapter 10 of the Tale of Genji, titled "The Sacred Tree" (Sakaki), explicitly names Nonomiya Shrine as the location where Genji visits Lady Rokujō in the autumn before her departure for Ise. The shrine's site in the Saga-no field area of western Kyoto matches the location described in the novel. This is not a retroactive marketing claim.
When is the Saigu Gyoretsu festival?
The Saigu Gyoretsu typically takes place on the third Sunday of October, though the date is confirmed annually by the shrine. The procession departs from Nonomiya Shrine and moves through Arashiyama to Togetsukyo Bridge. Check the shrine's official announcement each year for the exact date.
Can I combine Nonomiya with Fushimi Inari in one day?
Possible but rushed. Fushimi Inari requires at least 2–3 hours for a meaningful hike; Arashiyama and Nonomiya benefit from at least a half-day. The two are on opposite sides of central Kyoto, requiring about 40 minutes by train between them. If you want to do both in one day, start at Arashiyama early (Nonomiya + bamboo + Tenryu-ji), then take the train to Fushimi Inari for the late afternoon light on the torii gates.
Is Nonomiya Shrine free to enter?
Yes, admission to the shrine grounds is free. There is no entry charge. The adjacent Tenryu-ji garden charges ¥500 for garden access and ¥800 including the main hall. The bamboo grove itself is free and always open.
