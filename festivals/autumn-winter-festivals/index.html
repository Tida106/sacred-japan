Beyond the Leaves: Japan's Autumn & Winter Festivals

秋と冬の祭りを訪ねる

Most travelers plan Japan around cherry blossoms and summer fireworks, and miss the festival season that follows. Autumn is when shrine communities give thanks for the harvest, and winter is when some of the country's most spectacular night festivals light up the cold. The four below are not the first matsuri most visitors hear about, but each is a genuine shrine festival with deep history, and each offers something you cannot see anywhere else.

You will find lacquered lion and dragon floats hauled through a castle town, Heian-style boats drifting under red maples, heavy lantern-lit floats pulled up a hill beneath fireworks, and a midnight ritual that has been held in Nara since the twelfth century. If you are building an autumn trip around the leaves, our guide to the true sanctuaries of autumn covers the temples and shrines. This guide adds the festivals that make the season come alive.

Karatsu Kunchi (Saga) — Fourteen Floats Through the Castle Town唐津くんち

Official guide: Karatsu Tourism Association →

The Story

Karatsu Kunchi is the autumn festival of Karatsu Shrine, held every year on November 2, 3 and 4 in Karatsu, on the northwest coast of Kyushu. Its stars are fourteen hikiyama (曳山), enormous floats of wood and lacquer that neighborhoods have kept and pulled for generations. They take the form of lions, a dragon, a sea bream and other auspicious creatures. The floats you will see were built between 1819 and 1876. Among them are the Red Lion of 1819, the Sea Bream of 1845 and the Flying Dragon of 1846. The festival was designated an Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property of Japan in 1980.

What to See

The festival unfolds over three days. On the evening of November 2, the Yoiyama, the floats gather and march by lantern light toward the shrine, starting around 7:30 pm. On November 3, young men from the Koda district perform a lion dance at the shrine altar in the morning, and at around 9:30 am the floats line up and march from the oldest to the newest to the otabisho, the resting place of the deity, in Nishinohama. Karatsu's own tourism association calls this the highlight. On November 4, the floats make an eight-kilometer procession through the old castle town, and the participants shout "enya, enya!" as they push the floats with their last strength.

💡 Visitor Tip: Karatsu Kunchi draws crowds in the hundreds of thousands, and the town is small. Book accommodation well ahead, and check the official page for the day-by-day schedule before you plan your viewing spots.

Arashiyama Momiji Festival (Kyoto) — Court Boats on the Oi River嵐山もみじ祭

Festival guide: Magical Trip →

The Story

The Arashiyama Momiji Festival is held on the second Sunday of November, which is November 8 in 2026, around the Oi River near Togetsukyo Bridge. It celebrates the autumn color of Arashiyama and Mount Ogura, a view loved by aristocrats in the Heian period, and it expresses gratitude to the local guardian deities.

What to See

Decorated boats move slowly down the river while performers in colorful costumes recreate the elegant boat excursions of the Heian court. There are koto performances and dances, Imayo songs and dances, and Heian Kangen court music. The event runs from around 10:00 to 15:00. Watching is free around Togetsukyo Bridge or along the riverside at Kameyama Park, and paid seating is also available but must be reserved in advance and is very popular.

💡 Visitor Tip: The festival falls slightly before peak foliage in Arashiyama, which usually arrives from late November into early December. Pair it with Tenryu-ji for the garden and the hills, and expect the festival day itself to be very crowded.

Chichibu Night Festival (Saitama) — Lanterns, Floats and Fireworks秩父夜祭

Festival guide: Web Japan →

The Story

The Chichibu Night Festival is the great winter festival of Chichibu Shrine, held on December 2 and 3. It has a history of about 300 years and is counted among Japan's three great float festivals, alongside Kyoto's Gion Festival and Takayama's festival in Hida. It was inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2016.

What to See

Floats from each town gather near Chichibu Shrine, where the deity rests at the otabisho during the festival. They are decorated with rows of lanterns, and each weighs more than ten tons. On December 3, the floats are hauled up a steep slope called Dango-zaka, which is the moment most visitors come to see. Fireworks light up the night: on December 2 from around 7:00 to 8:00 pm, and on December 3 from about 7:30 to 10:00 pm. Good viewing spots include the Chichibu City Hall parking lot and the area in front of Seibu-Chichibu Station. The second day is the busier one.

💡 Visitor Tip: Chichibu is roughly an hour and a half from Ikebukuro by limited express, but the fireworks on December 3 run until about 10 pm. Check your return train in advance, and dress warmly, because December nights in a mountain valley are cold. Schedules can change, so check the official site a week before you go.

Kasuga Wakamiya On-matsuri (Nara) — A Ritual Since 1136春日若宮おん祭

Official guide: Nara City Tourism Association →

The Story

The On-matsuri is the festival of Wakamiya Shrine, within the grounds of Kasuga Taisha in Nara. It began in 1136, when Regent Fujiwara no Tadamichi held a ritual at Wakamiya for peace, a good harvest and harmony in Yamato Province, and it has been held every year since. The 2025 festival was the 890th, which makes the 2026 festival the 891st. It is designated a National Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property.

What to See

The festival runs from December 15 to 18. The public highlight is the Owatari-shiki procession on December 17, which begins at noon and winds through the streets of Nara with participants in costumes from the Heian to the Edo period. Shinto performing arts are presented as offerings, and solemn rituals are held in the middle of the night. The procession can be watched from the roadside, and reserved seating at three points along the route is sold by the Nara City Tourism Association.

💡 Visitor Tip: From JR Nara or Kintetsu Nara Station, a bus bound for Kasuga Taisha takes about 11 to 15 minutes. Combine the festival with our guides to Kasuga Taisha and a day trip to Nara.

Planning Your Visit訪れる前に

Dates, Weather and Booking

Festival dates are traditionally fixed to a day or a weekday, but the exact program can change from year to year, so check the official page of each festival a week or two before you travel. The smaller host towns, Karatsu and Chichibu especially, have limited accommodation, and rooms fill up early. Late autumn and December can be cold, so bring warm layers, and for the evening events, gloves and a hat.

Festival Etiquette

  • Give ritual participants room. Pullers, priests and performers are working; do not step into a procession or block its path.
  • Do not touch floats or sacred objects unless you have been invited to.
  • Respect closed ceremonies. Some rites, such as the midnight rituals of the On-matsuri, are not open to the public.
  • Take your trash with you and follow local rules on food, alcohol and photography.

If you also plan to visit shrines on the way, our Shrine Etiquette guide covers the customs that apply on shrine grounds. For the summer counterpart of this guide, see Japan's great summer festivals.

Conclusion結び

The autumn and winter festivals are quieter on the world stage than Gion or Nebuta, and that is part of their appeal. They stay closely tied to their shrines and their towns, which is why the floats, the boats and the processions feel like events that belong to a place and not to a tourist schedule. Pick one, check the dates, and build a trip around it. You will see the season from the inside.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

When are Japan's major autumn and winter festivals?
Typical dates are: Karatsu Kunchi in Saga on November 2 to 4; the Arashiyama Momiji Festival in Kyoto on the second Sunday of November (November 8 in 2026); the Chichibu Night Festival in Saitama on December 2 and 3; and the Kasuga Wakamiya On-matsuri in Nara from December 15 to 18, with the costume procession on December 17. Always confirm the current schedule with the official tourism pages before you book.
What is the Karatsu Kunchi?
Karatsu Kunchi is an autumn festival of Karatsu Shrine in Saga Prefecture, held every year on November 2, 3 and 4. Fourteen huge lacquered hikiyama floats, shaped as lions, a dragon, a sea bream and more, are pulled through the old castle town by local neighborhoods. The festival was designated an Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property of Japan in 1980.
Is the Arashiyama Momiji Festival held at peak autumn color?
Usually slightly before it. The festival is held on the second Sunday of November, while peak foliage in Arashiyama typically arrives in late November to early December. The boats and the color on the Oi River are still beautiful, but if the leaves are your priority, plan a second visit later in the month.
Can I see the Chichibu Night Festival as a day trip from Tokyo?
It is possible, because Chichibu is roughly an hour and a half from Ikebukuro by limited express. Keep in mind that the largest fireworks on December 3 run until about 10 pm and trains are crowded, so check the return schedule in advance. Many visitors stay overnight in Chichibu or nearby.
Can I watch the Kasuga Wakamiya On-matsuri for free?
Yes. The costume procession on December 17 can be watched from the roadside. Reserved seating is also sold at three points along the route by the Nara City Tourism Association, and it is worth considering if you want a guaranteed view.
