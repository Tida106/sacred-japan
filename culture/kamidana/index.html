What Is a Kamidana?

神棚とは何か

Japan's household Shinto altar, explained — what's inside it, where it goes, and how it differs from a butsudan

Walk into a traditional Japanese home, a small family-run shop, or even a construction site office, and look up near the ceiling. More often than you'd expect, you'll find a tiny wooden shrine no bigger than a bread box, tucked onto a high shelf: a kamidana (神棚), literally a "god shelf." It's one of the most common pieces of Shinto practice in Japan, and one of the least explained to visitors.

As a licensed guide, I get asked about it constantly once travelers notice one — usually in a ryokan lobby or a shopkeeper's back wall. This is the explanation I give them: what it actually is, what's inside it, and the handful of rules that govern where it can go.

What Is a Kamidana, Exactly?神棚とは何か

A kamidana is a miniature Shinto altar, usually a small wooden shrine structure (miyagata, 宮形) that houses an ofuda — a talisman obtained from a shrine representing a kami, one of Shinto's countless spirits or deities. Unlike a shrine's inner sanctuary, nothing physically divine is "inside" the kamidana itself; the ofuda is treated as a point of connection to the kami it represents, allowing worship to happen at home rather than only at the shrine.

Kamidana are found in private homes, but just as commonly in shops, offices, factories, and dojos — anywhere someone wants a daily, physical reminder to express gratitude and request protection. It is simple, unassuming, and completely ordinary in Japanese daily life, which is exactly why first-time visitors so rarely notice it until it's pointed out.

What's Inside: The Ofuda and How They're Arrangedお札の配置

In a full three-chambered miyagata, up to three ofuda are enshrined together, each in a specific position of honor:

CENTER

Jingu Taima

神宮大麻

The ofuda of Amaterasu from Ise Grand Shrine, Shinto's most sacred site. Given the most honored position, front and center.

RIGHT

Ujigami-sama

氏神様

The ofuda of the local tutelary shrine — the kami who watches over the neighborhood or region where the household lives.

LEFT

A Favored Shrine

崇敬神社

An ofuda from a shrine the family has a personal connection to or admires, held in third place. Most households avoid collecting too many.

Offerings: Rice, Sake, Water, Salt, and Sakakiお供え物

Offerings sit on a small tray in front of the miyagata, arranged by importance: rice nearest the kami as the most important offering, sake (traditionally two small bottles) next, and water and salt placed furthest out. Two sprigs of sakaki (榊), a sacred evergreen, stand in small vases on either side of the shrine itself, traditionally replaced on the 1st and 15th of the month.

Older guidance called for changing offerings daily; many households today instead set food and drink out only just before paying respects, then remove it afterward — a practical adaptation that is now widely considered acceptable rather than careless.

Nothing about a kamidana is ornate. A shelf, a tiny shrine, a few grains of rice, a sprig of evergreen — and a family's daily gratitude made small enough to fit on a wall.

Where It Goes: Height, Direction, and What to Avoid祀る場所

Placement follows a short but firm set of rules, whether in a home, a shop, or a dojo:

01

Always Above Eye Level

A kamidana is never placed at eye level or on the ground. It sits high on a wall or shelf, looking down on the room rather than being looked down upon.

02

Never Over a Doorway or Walked-Under Space

People should never pass underneath it. A spot over a door, hallway, or frequently walked path is considered disrespectful, whatever the height.

03

Facing East or South

The ideal orientation faces east, toward the rising sun, or south, toward the midday sun. A clean, quiet room is preferred over a cluttered or noisy one.

Kamidana vs Butsudan: Not the Same Altar神棚と仏壇の違い

Visitors sometimes confuse the kamidana with the butsudan (仏壇), the household Buddhist altar — understandably, since many older Japanese homes have traditionally kept both. They serve different purposes entirely.

  • Kamidana: Shinto. Enshrines an ofuda representing a kami. Centers on gratitude, protection, and connection to shrines. Placed high, facing east or south.
  • Butsudan: Buddhist. Holds a Buddha image or mandala alongside ihai (memorial tablets) bearing the names of deceased family members. Centers on ancestor veneration, maintained with incense and daily offerings of food and tea.

Where a household keeps both, they are traditionally placed in separate rooms, or at minimum positioned so that neither directly faces or looks down on the other — two distinct forms of devotion kept respectfully apart.

Renewal: Why the Ofuda Changes Every Yearお札の更新

Unlike the kamidana structure itself, which can be used for years, the ofuda inside is treated as a fresh charm each year. Most households replace it around the New Year: the old ofuda is returned to a shrine, often burned in a purification ceremony called otakiage, and a new one is enshrined to watch over the year ahead — the same logic that governs how omamori charms are renewed.

Can a Visitor Have One?旅行者にも持てるか

Yes. As with omamori, a kamidana sits comfortably in the space between religious object and cultural artifact, and visitors are welcome to bring one home. Small decorative kamidana are sold at shrine gift counters and craft shops, and an ofuda itself can usually be received at any shrine's office for a small offering.

If you do bring one home, the placement rules above still apply wherever you put it — above eye level, not under a walked path, ideally facing east or south — which is really the whole guide in miniature.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is a kamidana?
A kamidana (神棚, literally "god shelf") is a small household Shinto altar that enshrines an ofuda, a talisman from a shrine representing a kami. It is found in homes, businesses, and even martial arts dojos across Japan.
What's the difference between a kamidana and a butsudan?
A kamidana is Shinto and enshrines an ofuda from a shrine, usually placed up high facing east or south. A butsudan is Buddhist and holds a Buddha image plus memorial tablets (ihai) for deceased family members, used for ancestor veneration with incense. Many Japanese homes have had both, historically, side by side.
Where should a kamidana be placed?
Above ordinary eye level, ideally in a clean, quiet room, facing east toward the sunrise or south toward midday sun. It should never be placed where people walk underneath it, such as over a doorway, or in a low or cluttered spot.
What offerings go on a kamidana?
Traditionally rice (the most important offering), sake, water, and salt, arranged on a small tray, plus a sprig of sakaki (a sacred evergreen) in a stand on each side. Many households today set offerings out just before paying respects rather than leaving them permanently.
Can a foreigner or non-religious person have a kamidana?
Yes. Like omamori, a kamidana is widely treated as a cultural object as much as a religious one, and visitors can purchase a small decorative kamidana or an ofuda from many shrines as a respectful souvenir.
How often is the ofuda inside a kamidana replaced?
Traditionally once a year, usually around the New Year, when the old ofuda is returned to a shrine (often burned in a purification ceremony) and a fresh one is enshrined for the year ahead.

A Small, Everyday Kind of Sacred日常の中の信仰

Of everything Shinto asks of a household, a kamidana may be the gentlest: no sermon, no congregation, just a shelf, a shrine the size of a shoebox, and a quiet moment of gratitude built into the architecture of daily life. If this is your first encounter with it, our introduction to Shinto is the natural next read.

Sources verified at time of writing. Household customs vary by region, sect, and family tradition; this guide describes common practice rather than a single fixed rule.
