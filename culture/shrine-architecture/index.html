Shinto Shrine Architecture Guide

神社建築ガイド

Styles, Roofs, Parts and How Shrines Are Built

Stand in front of almost any shrine hall in Japan and look up. At the very top of the roof you will often see two timbers crossing like a pair of horns, and lying along the ridge a row of short, fat logs. They look like decoration, and they are, but they are also a code. According to Nippon.com, the way the horns are cut and how many logs there are can tell you whether the kami inside is male or female.

As a licensed guide, I find that visitors who learn four or five terms start to see shrines instead of just walking through them. This guide gives you those terms: how a shrine compound is laid out, the two ancient models that every style grows from, the main styles you will meet, and two famous buildings that show the extremes, Ise, which is rebuilt every twenty years, and Izumo, which is the tallest. Then it explains how the buildings are made, and how to put it all to use on your next visit.

The Layout: From Torii to Honden社殿の配置

A shrine is a sequence, and the buildings are arranged along a path that moves you from the ordinary world toward the kami. The Wikipedia overview of Shinto architecture and the World History Encyclopedia article give the same basic order.

  • Torii: the gate that marks the entrance to a sacred area. Wikipedia notes that a torii can be made of wood, stone, metal or concrete, and that Fushimi Inari has thousands.
  • Sando: the approach path that joins the torii to the worship hall.
  • Haiden: the hall of worship, where you stand to pray. World History Encyclopedia calls it usually the most impressive building at the shrine.
  • Heiden: the offering hall between the haiden and the honden. It is sometimes a separate building and sometimes just a covered link, and some shrines do not have one.
  • Honden: the main sanctuary, where the kami is enshrined. It is normally at the rear, smaller and plainer than the haiden, and closed to the public.
  • Tamagaki and kairo: the fence that surrounds the honden, and the covered, colonnaded walkway that can run around the inner sacred area.

The honden itself is usually divided into two parts, an inner sanctuary (naijin) and an outer one (gejin), according to World History Encyclopedia. The object that holds the spirit, the goshintai, is kept inside, which is one reason you will almost never see it.

There is one important exception. Nippon.com points out that some shrines have only a haiden, because the kami is a natural feature such as a sacred mountain or tree. It names Omiwa Shrine at Mount Miwa and Suwa Taisha. If you want to see how that works on the ground, our Omiwa Shrine guide and Suwa Taisha guide walks through it.

Two Ancient Models: The Granary and the House神明造と大社造

Nippon.com says that all honden styles trace back to one of two models: shinmei-zukuri, which is modeled on the ancient Japanese granary and best known at Ise, and taisha-zukuri, which is thought to reflect ancient residential architecture and takes its name from Izumo Taisha. The Wikipedia overview adds a third ancient form, sumiyoshi-zukuri, represented by Sumiyoshi Taisha.

That is a useful picture to hold on to. The oldest shrines did not copy a temple. They copied a storehouse for rice, which was the wealth of the country, and a house for a chief. Wikipedia also notes that before Buddhism arrived, most shrines were temporary structures built for a particular purpose, and that from around the mid-6th century Buddhism brought new elements. Later shrine halls borrowed features from temple architecture such as stone column bases, brackets, painted surfaces and metal ornaments. The Encyclopedia of Shinto adds that shrine architecture did not evolve in a straight line the way temples and houses did, and that there were periodic movements to return to ancient forms.

So when a shrine is plain, unpainted wood, you are probably looking at the oldest tradition. When it is red, gilded and carved, you are looking at a later layer that borrowed from the temple world. If you want to compare the two worlds on the ground, our shrine vs temple guide sets them side by side.

Reading the Roof: Chigi and Katsuogi千木と鰹木

Back to the code on the roof. Nippon.com describes chigi as paired slender timbers at the roof ends that fork upward and outward from the ridge, and katsuogi as short logs laid across the ridge. It calls them probably the most distinctive features of Shinto architecture, found on the most orthodox honden. World History Encyclopedia says they once did real work, holding down the roof in early wooden buildings, and are now mostly decorative.

The rule of thumb, as Nippon.com gives it:

  • Chigi cut horizontally (uchi-sogi) suggests a female deity, and chigi cut vertically (soto-sogi) a male one.
  • An even number of katsuogi suggests a female deity, and an odd number a male one.
  • Horizontal chigi almost always go with an even number of katsuogi, and vertical chigi with an odd number.

Treat it as a guide, not a law; Nippon.com itself says "almost always." But two famous buildings fit it. World History Encyclopedia counts 10 katsuogi on the Inner Shrine at Ise, which enshrines the goddess Amaterasu, and the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism database says the Izumo main hall carries three, wrapped in copper sheeting, for the god Okuninushi. I only checked the counts, not the chigi cuts, so I am noting a pattern, not proving the rule.

The Main Styles at a Glance本殿の様式

Japanese shrine styles are named with the suffix -zukuri, meaning "built in the manner of." The list below follows the Kokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto, which is the most careful source I found. I have kept to the features it gives and added links to our own guides where we cover the shrine.

  • Shinmei-zukuri (神明造): a gabled roof with the entrance on the long side, a straight roofline without upturned eaves, unfinished planed wood, and chigi and katsuogi. Found at the Ise shrines and their branches, and used for many shrines built since the Meiji period. See our Ise Grand Shrine guide.
  • Taisha-zukuri (大社造): entrance at the gable end, with a central "heart pillar," shin no mihashira. The model is Izumo Taisha. See our Izumo Taisha guide.
  • Sumiyoshi-zukuri (住吉造): entrance at the gable end, an interior divided into front and rear rooms, vermilion pillars and white walls. See our Sumiyoshi Taisha guide.
  • Nagare-zukuri (流造): a gabled roof with the entrance on the long side and a portico roof that sweeps forward over the full width. The encyclopedia calls it the most common style in the country, and gives the Upper and Lower Kamo shrines in Kyoto as examples. Our best shrines in Kyoto guide includes the Kamo area.
  • Kasuga-zukuri (春日造): entrance at the gable end, a small roof covering the entry stairway, and usually just one bay wide. It is the second most common style, concentrated in the Kinki region. See our Kasuga Taisha guide.
  • Hachiman-zukuri (八幡造): two gabled buildings joined front and rear, each entered from the side that is not the gable. The examples given are Usa Jingu and Iwashimizu Hachimangu.
  • Ryonagare-zukuri (両流造): a nagare-style building with the full-width portico roof at the front and the rear. Itsukushima Shrine is one example. See our Itsukushima Shrine guide.
  • Gongen-zukuri (権現造), also called ishinoma-zukuri: a haiden in front of the honden, joined by a low roofed passage called the ishi no ma. Kitano Tenmangu and Nikko Toshogu are the examples; see our Kitano Tenmangu guide. The name "gongen" comes from Tokugawa Ieyasu's posthumous title, Tosho Daigongen, and the encyclopedia warns that the term is sometimes applied loosely to any lavishly ornamented style. See our Nikko Toshogu guide.

The encyclopedia lists more regional forms, such as hiyoshi-zukuri and owari-zukuri, and a simplified misedana-zukuri that is usually used for small auxiliary shrines. For a first visit, the first five on the list will take you a long way.

Ise: The Shrine That Is Rebuilt伊勢と式年遷宮

The Ise shrines use a pure form called yuitsu shinmei-zukuri. The Encyclopedia of Shinto describes it as a linear, storehouse-shaped building with a thatched gabled roof on pillars sunk directly into the ground, with the entrance on the side that is not the gable. The materials are limited to cypress, miscanthus reeds (kaya) and gold and copper hardware, and a sacred central post, shin no mihashira, is ritually set beneath the main sanctuary's floor.

Then comes the part that surprises most visitors. The same encyclopedia says the transfer to a newly built shrine, shikinen sengu, was first performed in 690 for the Inner Shrine and in 692 for the Outer Shrine, in the reign of Empress Jito. The Wikipedia entry on Ise says the old buildings are dismantled and new ones built on an adjacent site to exactly the same design, and the World Economic Forum notes it had been rebuilt 62 times by 2013. The current cycle, the 63rd, began in 2025 and concludes in 2033, according to National Geographic.

It is not a quick job. Tokyo Weekender describes it as roughly an eight-year journey of about 30 rituals; the Ise city tourism site says about nine years. The difference is probably in where you start counting. An Associated Press report carried by WTOP says workers collect and process more than 10,000 cypress trees for it.

Why every twenty years? No single answer is certain. A practical one, from an English translation of the Japanese encyclopedia entry, is that plain wood pillars set in the ground and thatched roofs simply do not last long. The Japan for Sustainability newsletter adds that in the granary design the heavy thatched roof pressed the wall boards together and helped keep the stored rice dry. Smithsonian magazine adds that the cycle keeps artisan skills alive by passing them between generations. I find the second reason the more convincing one. A building that is rebuilt every generation is a building whose builders never forget how.

For visitors, the practical point is that the sanctuary itself is closed to ordinary worshippers. World History Encyclopedia says only senior priests and the emperor may enter the inner part. What you can see are the outer fences, the paths and the shinmei-style buildings of the subsidiary shrines.

Izumo: The Tallest Hall出雲大社の本殿

If Ise is about renewal, Izumo is about scale. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism database says the main hall was last rebuilt in 1744, stands 24 meters high and is the tallest shrine building in Japan. It was designated a National Treasure in 1952. The building has nine pillars in a three-by-three grid, with the thickest, the shin no mihashira, at the center, and its hinoki-bark roof carries chigi measuring over 7 meters. Web Japan gives the floor as roughly 11 meters on each side.

The ancient version was said to be taller. The Encyclopedia of Shinto notes that Heian-period records give a height of 16 jo, about 48 meters, but it calls reconstruction drawings of that scale somewhat unrealistic. I would treat 48 meters as a legend with some archaeology behind it, not a measurement.

Japan Guide says that until 1744 Izumo was periodically rebuilt like Ise, and that since then the tradition has been only partly kept, with major renovations about every 60 years instead. The most recent began in 2008 and was mostly complete in spring 2013, when the roof was replaced and the deity returned. For the story behind the building, see our Izumo Taisha guide and our Japanese mythology guide.

How Shrines Are Built宮大工と木組み

Shrines are built of wood, and World History Encyclopedia says the preferred wood is hinoki, Japanese cypress, left plain or painted. Roofs vary. At Ise the roof is thatch; at Izumo it is thick layers of hinoki bark. The Agency for Cultural Affairs lists thatching and roofing with cypress bark and wooden shingles among the traditional skills needed to conserve heritage buildings, which is a good reminder that these roofs are crafts, not just materials.

The people who build and repair them are miyadaiku, shrine and temple carpenters. The Web Japan feature on them says that one of their most important techniques is kigumi, joining wood without nails or metal fittings, and that it takes over ten years to become a full-fledged miyadaiku. The Embassy of Japan in Singapore notes that UNESCO designated Japan's traditional skills and knowledge for wooden architecture, including wooden joinery, as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2020.

One explanation of why they avoid metal in the framing, from a Live Japan feature, is that nails and fasteners rust in Japan's rain and humidity and make the wood rot, so a joint that holds by shape is easier to repair for a thousand years. I give that as one account, not a rule. As I said in the Ise section, metal is not absent from shrines. It appears as hardware and as copper wrapping, so the accurate version is that the frame is joined without nails, not that nothing metal is used.

How to Read a Shrine When You Visit鑑賞のコツ

Here is the five-step routine I use with groups. You can do it in a minute at any shrine.

  1. Look up at the roof first. Do you see chigi and katsuogi? If so, you are at an orthodox, older-style hall. Count the logs, and notice how the chigi tips are cut.
  2. Find the entrance. Is it on the long side or the gable end? That one question separates the shinmei and nagare family from the taisha, sumiyoshi and kasuga family.
  3. Check for a front porch roof. A roof that sweeps forward over the whole width is the mark of nagare-zukuri, the most common style.
  4. Walk around the side. The haiden hides the honden. From the side you can often see the second, smaller building behind it, and whether a heiden joins the two.
  5. Ask what is made of plain wood and what is painted. Plain, planed wood points to the oldest tradition. Red paint, gilding and carving point to the later layers that borrowed from temples.

Before you do any of this, remember the manners that come first: bow at the torii and rinse your hands. Our shrine etiquette guide covers them, and our what is Shinto guide explains the ideas behind the buildings.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is the difference between a honden and a haiden?
The honden is the main hall where the kami is enshrined; it sits at the back and is normally closed to the public. The haiden is the worship hall in front of it, and it is the building most visitors actually stand before. The Wikipedia entry on the honden and the overview of Shinto architecture both describe this order, with a heiden (offering hall) sometimes joining the two.
Why do some shrines have no main hall?
Some kami are a mountain, a tree or a rock, so the shrine needs a place to pray toward but not a building to house the god. Nippon.com gives Omiwa Shrine (Mount Miwa) as an example and also names Suwa Taisha. At such shrines the haiden is the most important building. Our Suwa Taisha guide explains how Suwa works.
What are the crossed beams on the roof?
They are called chigi, and the short logs lying across the ridge are katsuogi. They once held the thatch in place and are now mostly decoration, but they are the clearest sign of an old, orthodox shrine roof. Nippon.com adds a rule of thumb about what their cut and count say about the deity; see the section on reading the roof above.
Is Ise Shrine really rebuilt every 20 years, and can I see it?
Yes. The Kokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto dates the practice to 690 for the Inner Shrine and 692 for the Outer Shrine, and the 62nd rebuilding was in 2013. The current cycle began in 2025 and ends in 2033. The new building is put up beside the old one, and the inner sanctuary is closed to ordinary visitors. What you can do is walk the grounds and see the outer fences and the subsidiary shrines. Our Ise Grand Shrine guide has the visiting details.
Are shrines really built without nails?
The main structure is, yes. Traditional carpenters join the timbers with kigumi, which the Web Japan feature describes as joining wood without nails or metal fittings. But I would not say there is no metal anywhere. The Encyclopedia of Shinto lists gold and copper hardware among the materials at Ise, and the Izumo roof ridge carries copper-wrapped katsuogi. So "no nails in the framing" is accurate; "no metal at all" is not.
Which shrine style is the most common?
Nagare-zukuri. The Kokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto calls it the most common shrine style nationwide, with kasuga-zukuri second, and says most small and medium shrines use one of the two. If you have visited a handful of local shrines, you have probably seen both.
What is the tallest shrine building in Japan?
The main hall of Izumo Taisha. Japan Guide and the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism database both give 24 meters for the building rebuilt in 1744. Earlier versions are said to have been far taller, but the Encyclopedia of Shinto calls the 48-meter reconstruction drawings somewhat unrealistic. See our Izumo Taisha guide.

Final Thoughtsむすびに

If you remember one thing, remember the roof. The crossed horns and the row of logs are the oldest signature of a Shinto building, and once you notice them you will find them everywhere, from the great halls to the smallest roadside shrine. From there the rest follows: where the door is, whether the roof sweeps forward, and whether the building is plain or painted.

If you would like to see the extremes for yourself, start with the Ise Grand Shrine and the Izumo Taisha. And if you are curious about the old theater stage that borrows a shrine's roof, our noh guide shows how far that idea travelled.
