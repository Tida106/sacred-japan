Sumo: The Sacred Sport Guide

相撲ガイド

Shinto Rituals, the Six Tournaments, Tickets and Morning Practice

Two very large men crouch at opposite ends of a clay ring. Each throws a handful of salt, stamps one foot into the ground, claps, and stares. Then, after minutes of this, they charge, and the bout is often over in seconds. Most of the drama of sumo is in what happens before the clash, and almost all of that is ritual.

As a licensed guide, I think sumo is the easiest way to see Shinto practice in action without visiting a shrine. This guide explains the rituals, the rules, the six yearly tournaments, how to buy tickets, and how to follow sumo back to the shrines where it grew up.

A Shrine in the Middle of an Arena土俵という聖域

The ring is called the dohyo (土俵). The Japan Sumo Association describes it as 6.7 m square and 60 cm high, made of a special clay with a thin layer of sand on top. The fights take place inside a circle a little over 4.55 m across, marked by straw bales, and part of the ring is rebuilt for every tournament: 20 cm of soil is removed and new soil is added.

Above the ring hangs a roof, suspended from the ceiling by cables, and it looks like the roof of a shrine. Wikipedia's article on the Shinto origins of sumo says the canopy is reminiscent of a Shinto shrine, and that the referee dresses much like a priest. World History Encyclopedia goes further and says the whole arrangement still enjoys the status of a sacred Shinto shrine. I would put it more carefully: it is not a shrine you can pray at, but it is a space that is treated as sacred.

The photograph at the top of this page shows the roof in context. It is a big wooden roof with a plain, steep slope, and it hangs well above the ring, which is the small pale shape in the middle of the crowd. Crossed beams stand at the two ends of its ridge and a row of short round logs lies along it; these are the features I associate with shrine roofs (I believe they are called chigi and katsuogi, though the sources I used do not name them). A purple curtain with round crests hangs around its underside. The tall white banners behind it read 満員御礼 (man'in onrei), which I read as "full house, with thanks," a notice that the day is sold out, and the Japanese flag hangs beside them. Framed portraits run along the upper wall; they look like portraits of champions, but I cannot confirm that. The Commons file name suggests the photograph is from 2006, so details of the hall may have changed since.

The day before a tournament opens there is a ring-blessing ceremony, the dohyo matsuri. According to Wikipedia, it is led by one of the head referees with two other referees standing in for Shinto priests, and it purifies the ring and invites the kami to watch. Wikipedia's Shinto-origins article adds that salt, kelp, dried squid and chestnuts are buried in the center of the ring. I read both as describing the usual form of the ceremony, not a rule written in stone.

The referee, the gyoji, is part of the same picture. Sumo Fan Magazine says he wears a kimono-style costume that resembles the court dress of the Kamakura-period samurai, and a lacquered gauze hat that is quite like a Shinto priest's hat. If you have read our guide to Shinto, the look will feel familiar.

Here is a referee in full dress before a bout in January 2008. Commons identifies him as the 37th Shikimori Inosuke and says he is holding out his gunbai, the war fan, towards the photographer. The costume fits the description above: a wide-sleeved blue robe patterned with white crests, a black gauze hat, a white sash, and white split-toe socks with sandals. Behind him you can see the straw bales that edge the ring. The fan carries brush writing that I cannot read at this size, so I will not guess at it.

What to Watch Before Each Bout取組前の所作

These are the things that make first-time viewers ask "what is he doing?" The Japan Sumo Association's introduction and Wikipedia agree on the main points.

  • Salt: "Purifying salt is used to expel evil from the sacred area of the ring," the association says. The wrestlers scatter it before the bout.
  • Chikara-mizu: the "power water." The association says it symbolizes water that purifies the body, and the wrestlers rinse their mouths with it.
  • Stamping (shiko): raising a leg high and bringing it down hard. GLTJP describes it as stamping on the ring to stamp out evil spirits, and the association describes the champion's version in the same way.
  • Clapping: Wikipedia notes that this recalls the clapping at a shrine that attracts the attention of the gods.

This is the salt ritual caught mid-throw. The wrestler on the left has just flung a handful in a wide arc, and the grains hang in the dark like spray. The referee stands between the two wrestlers in a black court cap and a black-and-gold patterned robe, and the wrestler at the right waits with his arm lowered. Commons gives the camera location as about 35.59°N, 139.65°E, which is not Ryogoku (about 35.70°N, 139.79°E), so I would not assume that this was taken at a Tokyo tournament.

Here is one throw at close range. The salt fans out to the left of the wrestler's hand, and his other hand hangs at his side. A small straw basket stands near his feet, which I take to be the salt basket, though the photo does not say. His left knee is wrapped in white tape. Commons names him as Ushiomaru Motoyasu and dates the photograph to 15 March 2005; it does not give the venue, so I do not name one.

The most formal version is the champion's ring-entering ceremony, the dohyo-iri. The association says the highest-ranked wrestlers, the yokozuna, perform it with the most ceremony, wearing a heavy braided rope. Wikipedia says the ceremony is regarded as a purification ritual in its own right and is occasionally performed at shrines. The rope reminds me of the twisted shimenawa ropes that mark sacred places at shrines. That comparison is mine, not the association's, and our guide to Shinto explains the shrine rope.

This is the ceremony from above, which shows its layout well. At the back the yokozuna squats with his arms spread, with the white rope around his waist and zigzag paper streamers hanging down in front. A wrestler on the left holds a sword upright with a purple cloth over the hilt, and another kneels on the right. A tour company's ritual guide calls the sword-bearer the tachimochi and the other attendant the tsuyuharai (the "dew-sweeper"), according to its description; I take the figures in the photo to be those two. In front, the referee in a blue patterned robe kneels at the center of the ring, holding something I cannot identify. You can also see the straw bales that mark the circle, the two white lines in the middle, and what look like small baskets of salt at the corners of the ring.

Here is the same ceremony from the side, in a crowd, a decade earlier. The yokozuna squats in the white rope with his arms spread to the sides. At the left, a wrestler crouches holding a sword upright, and a third wrestler, in an orange apron, sits cross-legged on a purple cushion beside the ring's center. The referee kneels at the right in a white robe with round crests and a black cap. The two white lines are again visible in the middle. I do not know whether the seated pose is a fixed part of the ritual, so I only describe what I can see.

The ceremony travels as well. The banner behind the wrestlers reads 大相撲沖縄場所 (Grand Sumo Okinawa), and I read the smaller text at the right as a Heisei 29 winter tour, so this appears to be one of the regional exhibition tours and not one of the six tournaments. Commons names the yokozuna as Hakuho. He crouches in the white rope with his arms spread; the attendant at the left holds the sword in its purple cloth, and the referee kneels at the right in a gold brocade robe. The aprons of the two attendants are embroidered with scenes, a castle and a wooden bridge among flowers, though I cannot tell which places they show.

Where Sumo Comes From相撲の起源

The oldest story is a legend. The Japan Sumo Association's introduction points to the tale of the contest between Nomi no Sukune and Taima no Kehaya in the Nihon Shoki, the chronicle completed in 720. Wikipedia dates it, allegedly, to 23 BC: the emperor sent Nomi no Sukune against Taima no Kehaya, who had boasted that he was the strongest man under heaven. Nomi no Sukune won with kicks that killed him. Wikipedia is careful to say that this was not modern sumo, though Nomi no Sukune is regarded as its founder.

The association also mentions a divine version: according to legend, the origin of the Japanese people depended on the outcome of a sumo match. I believe this refers to the contest between the gods Takemikazuchi and Takeminakata, though the association's text does not name them. Takeminakata's defeat is the story behind the main deity of Suwa Taisha, which you can read in our Suwa Taisha guide.

A later layer is court ritual. The National Diet Library's entry, shown on the Japan Search portal, says that during the Nara period an agricultural divination ritual known as sumai no sechi-e started. Sumo as a ritual for the harvest is also how several tourism writers explain its origin, so I treat the harvest link as common wisdom rather than a documented fact.

The form we know belongs to the Edo period. The association says that, like kabuki, sumo became a mass attraction then. Sources agree that in 1684 fund-raising sumo (kanjin-zumo) was authorized, to raise money for building and repairing shrines and temples, and that it grew into the professional sport. They differ on details: GLTJP says the main tournament was held on the grounds of Tomioka Hachimangu for about a hundred years from 1684, while Sumo Fan Magazine says the first tournament after the revival was held at Eitaiji, a temple affiliated with the shrine. One source says the tournaments later moved to Ekoin temple in Ryogoku, in 1791, and I found that in only one place. You can see the Edo audience in our guide to ukiyo-e, where wrestlers were popular print subjects.

How a Bout and a Career Workルールと番付

The rules are short. According to the association, a wrestler loses if he is forced or thrown out of the inner circle, or if any part of his body other than the soles of his feet touches the ground, including a knee, a fingertip or the topknot. It also lists forbidden acts such as striking with a fist, hair pulling, eye gouging and choking. The association counts 82 winning techniques, called kimarite.

Commons calls this a fight, but the moment is the one just before it. The wrestler on the left, in a blue belt, crouches with his fists close to the ground, and the one on the right, in a purple belt, still stands. Salt lies thick across the ring where it has been thrown. As far as I know, the two short white lines in the middle are the lines the wrestlers set their fists behind. The referee in a pink robe stands between them, and a man in white at the left sweeps the edge of the ring with a broom, whom I take to be a ring attendant (yobidashi); one tour-company guide says that these attendants call the wrestlers into the ring and sweep it.

The wrestler wears a mawashi, a silk belt that the association says is about 9 m long and 80 cm wide, wound around the waist four to seven times. The association says there are about 700 wrestlers in professional sumo.

Ranks

Rank is called banzuke, and it is set by results in the six tournaments. From the top, the divisions are:

  1. Makuuchi (the top division, up to 42 wrestlers), which contains the top ranks: yokozuna, ozeki, sekiwake, komusubi, then maegashira
  2. Juryo
  3. Makushita
  4. Sandanme
  5. Jonidan
  6. Jonokuchi

In each 15-day tournament the two top divisions fight once a day for 15 days, and the lower divisions fight seven times, the association says. Only the top two divisions wear the elaborate topknot called oichomage. A wrestler can move up or down, with one exception: the association says that a yokozuna can never be demoted, but is expected to retire after persistently poor results, and that promotion to yokozuna requires winning two consecutive tournaments or equivalent results, together with a judgment of character.

The Six Tournaments年6回の本場所

Three tournaments are in Tokyo, at the Ryogoku Kokugikan. The dates below for 2027 are from The Japan Ode and WaTabi, which agree with each other. Please check the association's own site before you book flights.

  • January: Tokyo, Ryogoku Kokugikan, 10-24 January 2027 (general sale 5 December 2026)
  • March: Osaka, EDION Arena Osaka, 14-28 March 2027 (general sale 6 February)
  • May: Tokyo, Ryogoku Kokugikan, 9-23 May 2027 (general sale 10 April)
  • July: Nagoya, IG Arena, 11-25 July 2027 (general sale 15 May)
  • September: Tokyo, Ryogoku Kokugikan, 12-26 September 2027 (general sale 7 August)
  • November: Fukuoka, Fukuoka Kokusai Center, 14-28 November 2027 (general sale 18 September)

The November 2026 Fukuoka tournament runs 8-22 November, so it is on as I write this. Venue names for Nagoya have changed over the years, and older guides use the old names.

Ticketsチケット

This is the hard part, especially for Tokyo. For overseas visitors, WaTabi says the official English service is TICKET OOSUMO ENGLISH. Its description of the January 2027 sale is a "ladder": fan-club pre-lotteries in mid- and late November, a ticket-site pre-lottery in late November, then the general sale on 5 December 2026 at 10:00. If you are traveling in a Tokyo tournament month, the date to remember is the general sale date, or an earlier lottery if you can use one.

Prices (Tokyo, from January 2027, as reported by WaTabi)

WaTabi reports a price rise for 2027 and says older official price PDFs still show the earlier prices, so please confirm on the official ticket site. I give the weekday-to-final-day range per person.

  • Chair D (back row, online only): ¥3,000 for adults; children aged 4-15 are ¥500 on weekdays and ¥1,000 on weekends and holidays, which WaTabi calls the cheapest admission in the building.
  • Chair C / B: ¥5,000-7,000 / ¥6,000-8,000
  • Chair A / S: ¥10,000-12,000 / ¥11,000-13,000
  • Box seats (masu), sold by the box: from ¥9,500 (C) up to ¥22,500-25,000 (SS, new for 2027)
  • Ringside (tamari): ¥25,000-30,000, age 16 and over

Osaka and Fukuoka cost less according to the same article (for example, Fukuoka's Chair C is ¥2,000 on weekdays), and for Osaka it recommends buying before you fly.

If it is sold out

WaTabi describes an official face-value resale service on the ticket site, with a 10% fee for the seller. It also reports that the association prohibits resale for profit without permission, names several outside platforms, and says it took 138 enforcement actions across the six tournaments of 2025. I found those figures in that one article, so I cite them as WaTabi's report, but the advice is easy to follow: if you are buying, use the official channels.

A Day at the Tournament一日の流れ

This is the Ryogoku Kokugikan from across the street on a June day in 2018, as Commons dates it. The big green-tiled roof rises to a gold octagonal cap, and the lower white-tiled front is, as far as I can tell, where the entrance and ticket windows are. A white flag with a crest and a Japanese flag stand at the left, and a tall black lattice tower stands at the right, which I take to be a drum tower (yagura). Only a few people are about, so this is not a tournament day.

One outer wall carries a huge painting that looks to me like an old woodblock print blown up to wall size. In the middle a man in a patterned kimono holds a fan with a tassel and the hilt of a sword, which to my eye looks like a referee. Two wrestlers stand on each side of him, and the vertical characters at the right read Zaidan Hojin Nihon Sumo Kyokai, the Japan Sumo Association. Commons says only “Ryogoku Kokugikan” and the date, 29 August 2013, so I will not tell you who is pictured. If you like the look, our ukiyo-e guide explains the print tradition it recalls.

The sport starts in the morning and builds to an evening climax. WaTabi's approximate timetable for a Tokyo September tournament shows the shape of the day:

  • 8:45 doors open; the lowest divisions begin from about 9:20
  • About 14:15 the juryo ring-entering ceremony, with juryo bouts from about 14:35
  • About 15:40 the top-division ring-entering ceremony, then the yokozuna's ceremony at about 15:55
  • About 16:05 top-division bouts begin, with the top ranks after 17:00
  • About 17:55 the bow-twirling ceremony, and the finish at about 18:00

Six wrestlers stand in a loose row in front of the purple curtain and the lit underside of the hanging roof, each in a different decorated apron: orange with a gold crest and large characters in the middle, black with a white winged horse, red with gold designs. As far as I know, these aprons (keshomawashi) are worn only in ceremonies. I take this to be a ring-entering ceremony of the kind in the timetable above, but Commons says only "unknown sumo wrestlers" and dates the photograph to 22 September 2008, so I cannot be sure.

The article's advice is to arrive around 2 p.m., which fits my own feeling that the first half of the day is for sumo fans and the second half is for everyone. Times vary by tournament and day, so check the day's schedule before you go.

For food, GLTJP describes chanko-nabe, the wrestlers' stew, as a broth made from chicken bones flavored with soy sauce or miso, with ingredients such as meatballs and napa cabbage. It says recipes vary by stable, that many retired wrestlers run restaurants serving it, and that the Kokugikan has restaurants serving chanko-nabe supervised by stables. Our guide to popular Japanese dishes covers other everyday favorites.

This is a pot of it on a gas burner at a restaurant table. I can see napa cabbage, golden fried tofu, enoki mushrooms, leeks and seaweed in a pale, clear broth, with a ladle lifting out a round meatball. The meatball and the napa cabbage match GLTJP's description, but I cannot tell the broth base from a photo, and Commons does not name the restaurant.

Morning Practice at a Stable朝稽古の見学

If tickets fail, there is a second way to see sumo: morning practice (asa-geiko) at a stable, the heya where wrestlers live and train. Several tour companies run it. One guide describes meeting at Ryogoku Station and spending roughly 1.5 to 2 hours watching from the tatami, with a briefing on etiquette beforehand.

This is what practice looks like. In the middle two wrestlers in dark belts are locked together with their heads low, a little blurred by the movement, with white tape on their toes. At the left wall a third wrestler in a pale belt stands and watches, and at the right edge a fourth has his hands raised against the wall with his back to the camera. Behind them is a row of wooden plates with names in black brush, which I take to be the stable's wrestlers, though I cannot read them at this size. The floor is packed clay with a straw-bale rim, and a strip of pale mats runs along the front. Commons gives no stable name, so I do not name one or say whether this was a tour.

Etiquette is strict. One Tokyo tour company says there is no talking, eating or drinking during practice, and another says you may not leave until the practice has finished and asks guests to show respect to the stable master and the wrestlers, since a stable is not a tourist attraction. Timing matters too: a tour guide says viewing is off the table during Tokyo tournament periods and that the best time is the run-up to a Tokyo tournament.

One more point. An All About Japan writer says you can visit Arashio Stable without a tour, free and with no reservation, if you check its calendar. That article is a few years old, and rules for visitors change, so please check the stable's current policy before relying on it.

Sumo Shrines to Visit相撲ゆかりの神社

This is where sumo and a shrine guide meet.

Tomioka Hachimangu, Fukagawa, Tokyo

GLTJP calls it the largest Hachiman shrine of the Edo period and the birthplace of kanjin-zumo, the origin of today's sumo, with many sumo-related stone monuments, including one for yokozuna, one for ozeki and a stone with wrestlers' hand and foot prints. PhotoGuide Japan says the centerpiece is a 3.5 m, 20-ton monument made in 1900 by the 12th yokozuna, Jinmaku Kyugoro, after collecting donations. After the original stone filled up, two new slabs were added alongside it in 1983. Sumo Fan Magazine says a new yokozuna has his name inscribed on the monument, so the shrine and the champions stay linked. The shrine's big Fukagawa Hachiman Festival is held in mid-August.

Sumiyoshi Shrine, Fukuoka

Crossroad Fukuoka says this shrine has deep ties with sumo and that a statue of an ancient sumo wrestler stands in the grounds. Go Fukuoka places it next to the main hall, and GLTJP says that touching its hand is meant to give you strength.

He is more muscular than any wrestler you will see in the ring today, which is the point: he is an imagined ancient champion, not a modern rikishi. He has a beard, a topknot and gold-coloured eyes, and both open hands are held out towards you. Japan Journeys also mentions the gold eyes. The polished box in front of the base is marked with characters that I read as hosai, an offering box. The shrine is at 3-1-51 Sumiyoshi, Hakata Ward, about an 8-to-10-minute walk from Hakata Station according to GLTJP and Crossroad Fukuoka, and GLTJP lists it as free, open 9:00 to 17:00. Please confirm hours with the shrine.

Sumo Shrine, Sakurai, Nara

Japan Experience describes the Sumo Shrine (Sumo-jinja), near Anashinimasu Hyozu Shrine in Sakurai, as an ancient shrine claimed to be the birthplace of sumo, where the bout between Nomi no Sukune and Taima no Kehaya is said to have taken place. The grounds have cherry trees and hand prints left by wrestlers who made the journey. It is a claim, not a proven site, so I recommend going for the atmosphere.

I have only given you the shrines I could confirm in more than one source. For shrine manners at any of them, see our shrine etiquette guide.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How many sumo tournaments are there each year?
Six. They are called honbasho and are held in odd-numbered months: January, May and September in Tokyo, March in Osaka, July in Nagoya and November in Fukuoka. Each lasts 15 days, according to the Japan Sumo Association's own introduction.
Can I buy sumo tickets on the day?
Sometimes, but do not plan around it. WaTabi says same-day tickets are simply unsold reserved seats, sold through the web, convenience stores and the box office, and that if a date sold out in advance there are none. Treat it as a fallback.
Is it safe to buy tickets from resale sites?
I would stay with the official channels. WaTabi reports that the association prohibits resale for profit without its permission, names several third-party platforms, and says it takes no responsibility for tickets bought that way. The official face-value resale on the ticket site is the safer way to get a sold-out date.
What time should I arrive?
WaTabi suggests about 2 p.m. for a Tokyo tournament, because the top-division bouts come late in the day. For its September example it gives the top-division ring-entering ceremony at about 15:40 and the first top-division bout at about 16:05. Times shift a little by tournament, so check the day's schedule.
Can I watch morning practice during a tournament?
Usually not in Tokyo. One tour-company guide says that during the Tokyo tournament periods morning-practice viewing is off the table, and that the best window is the run-up to a Tokyo tournament. If your trip falls in a tournament month, look for dates just before it.
Why does sumo have so many rituals?
Because many of them come from Shinto. The Japan Sumo Association explains the salt as a way to expel evil from the sacred ring, and the roof over the ring resembles a shrine roof. Wikipedia's article on the Shinto origins of sumo adds that the referee dresses much like a priest and that a ring-blessing ceremony is held the day before each tournament.
Is sumo a religion?
No. It is a professional sport with a ranking system, salaries and a calendar. But it keeps a lot of Shinto form: the purifying salt, the stamping, the clapping and the shrine-like roof. I find it easiest to say that the sport grew out of ritual and still remembers it.

Final Thoughtsむすびに

If you only remember one thing, remember the salt. A handful of white crystals thrown onto clay tells you that this is a sport that still thinks of its ring as a place to be purified before it is used. Everything else, from the stamping to the shrine roof, follows from that.
