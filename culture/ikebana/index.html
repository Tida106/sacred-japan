The Japanese Art of Ikebana

生け花・華道ガイド

History, Schools and Where to See and Try It

When travelers ask me about ikebana, they usually expect a bouquet. What they find is closer to a drawing made with plants: a few branches, a container chosen with care, and a lot of empty space around them.

As a licensed guide, I think ikebana is one of the easiest ways to understand how Japanese taste works. You do not need to read a word of Japanese to enjoy it. You only need to learn what to look at. This guide shows you, using photographs from real exhibitions.

What Ikebana Is生け花とは

Ikebana (生け花) is often translated as "making flowers live." It is also called kado (華道), "the way of flowers." Arrangements use flowers, but also bare branches, leaves, berries and grasses, and the container is part of the design.

The roots are religious. Wikipedia: Ikebana describes how ancient Japanese decorated evergreen trees with flowers as yorishiro, objects that invite the gods, and how Buddhism later made flower offerings at altars common.

Some arrangements are built on three main points, a scalene triangle that stands for heaven, human and earth. Others, like the huge piece at the top of this page, ignore that structure and use the whole room.

A Short History生け花の歴史

  • Ancient times: evergreens decorated with flowers are used as yorishiro to invite the gods.
  • Buddhist altars: flower offerings spread. The Ikenobo school traces its beginnings to the Rokkaku-do temple in Kyoto, said to have been built in 587 by Prince Shotoku.
  • Mid-1400s: the rikka ("standing flowers") style takes shape, a Buddhist expression of landscape beauty built around nine branches.
  • 1500s: nageire ("thrown-in flowers"), a looser style, becomes independent.
  • Edo period: nageire is formalized into shoka for the tokonoma alcove. The fixed-model seika style develops after the 1780s, with three main parts: ten (heaven), chi (earth) and jin (human).
  • 1895: Ohara Unshin founds the Ohara school.
  • 1927: Teshigahara Sofu founds the Sogetsu school.

Tea, the Alcove and the Flower茶室と花

If you have read our tea ceremony guide, you know the alcove, the tokonoma: the place of honor in a tea room, where a hanging scroll and a few flowers are set out. Arrangements were made for exactly this space. The flowers used in a tea room are called chabana. Wikipedia: Ikebana treats chabana as separate from ikebana, but says its simplicity helped create the nageire style.

This photograph connects the two arts. Commons describes it as an arrangement in a mizusashi, the water jar that a tea host uses, with hidasuki markings, the red streaks left on the clay in firing. It is from the Saga Go-ryu, in a room called the Seisui no ma. A long branch of red hips arches across the picture, and a few small yellow flowers, which look like chrysanthemums to me, sit low in the jar. The round lid stands beside the jar, as it would in a tea room. Notice the shadow: the branch is drawn twice, once in the air and once on the wall.

The Schools流派

Ikebana is taught by many schools, each with its own forms and vocabulary. Three names you will meet often are Ikenobo, the school tied to the Rokkaku-do temple in Kyoto, Ohara (founded 1895) and Sogetsu (founded 1927).

The photographs below come mostly from an exhibition in November 2018 at Meguro Gajoen in Tokyo. According to the Commons descriptions, 57 schools belonging to the Japan Ikebana Art Association showed their work there, in exhibitions that changed weekly, from traditional arrangements with autumn flowers and fruit to free-style pieces that used a whole room. The school names in the captions come from those descriptions.

Seven Arrangements to Look At作品を見る

Big and Bold: Branches Against Gold

The photograph at the top of this page is a large arrangement from the Shozan Enshu-ryu, shown in a room called the Kiyokata no ma, according to Commons. Bare branches painted white fan out wide, with orange flowers that look like bird-of-paradise and red-orange berries on long dark branches reaching out to the sides. The vase is tall, rough and blue-grey. Behind it stands a gold-leaf folding screen, and the branches throw their shadows across it. On the red carpet lie a green fruit and a few fallen leaves, which I read as part of the composition. The name card in front appears to read the maker's name, 西崎一梨, under the school name.

Spring Blossoms in an Alcove

This is from the Senke Koryu Fuyokai, also in the Kiyokata no ma, per Commons. In one alcove there are two arrangements. On the left, a spiky, pale-edged green plant sits in a bronze trough on a black lacquer stand. On the right, thorny bare branches carry orange-red and white blossoms, which look to me like flowering quince, in a brown vase on a lacquer tray. Look at the right side of the wall: the shadow of the thorny branches is as much part of the picture as the blossoms.

Shelves and Small Worlds

Traditional rooms have staggered shelves, and ikebana fills them with small works. I match this photograph, by its thumbnail, to Commons' entry for the Ohara-ryu in the Chojo no ma, the top-floor room. On the upper shelf at left, a pink-and-white orchid and pine sit in an ornate yellow vessel. At right, a small pine bonsai grows in a shallow green dish, next to a dark stone on a carved stand. A curved dark object on a lacquer tray at lower left is something I cannot identify. Gold-leaf sliding doors fill the bottom.

Here several small arrangements share one shelf unit. I match it by thumbnail to the Saga Go-ryu's smaller arrangements in the Seisui no ma. A trailing green plant with small yellow flowers sits in an olive-glazed trough. Yellow chrysanthemums with red-green leaves rest in a deep red vase. A slim white vase holds pink-purple flowers. To the right is a pillar carved to look like a rough trunk. The green bamboo bar on the floor is, I take it, a simple barrier that keeps visitors back, though I have not seen a caption that says so.

A Modern Gallery Piece

Not every arrangement lives in an alcove. Commons says this one is from the Shinsei-ryu by Nishikawa Suishun, shown at the Nagoya Ikebana Art Exhibition in the Citizens' Gallery Sakae, in November 2018. The name card on the plinth reads 真生流. Two vases stand side by side, one cream and one woven bamboo, and curved bamboo strips sweep around them. Red and orange berries, white spider chrysanthemums, maple-like leaves and grey-green hydrangea heads fill the space. It reads as an autumn piece.

Flowers Against Black Lacquer

Orchids and pine make an unusual pair here. Spikes of purple and dark red orchids rise from a colorful painted porcelain vase on a carved stand, with pine branches reaching out on both sides. The vase sits on a black lacquer cabinet inlaid with birds and flowers, one of which looks like a pheasant. Behind is a dark wooden lattice. I could not match this photograph to its Commons description with certainty, so I do not name the school here.

The Whole Room

Stepping back, you see how the arrangements and the building work together: fan-shaped flower paintings along the upper walls, carved log pillars, gold-leaf doors, and a different arrangement in every alcove. The green bamboo bars laid along the floor look like markers that keep visitors back from the alcoves. The Commons description for this file was cut off on my screen, so I do not name the room, but the pillars and shelves look like those in the shelf photograph above.

How to Look at an Arrangement鑑賞のコツ

These are my own habits as a guide, not rules from a school.

  1. Find the longest line. Follow the main branch from the vase to its tip. Most arrangements are built around it.
  2. Look at the container. It is chosen as carefully as the flowers, as with the red-streaked water jar earlier.
  3. Notice what is left out. The empty space around the branches is part of the design.
  4. Look at the shadow. In several photographs here, the shadow on the wall is half the picture.
  5. Read the name card. It usually gives the school and the maker. You can see one in the photographs of the gold screen and the gallery piece.

Where to See and Try Ikebana鑑賞・体験するには

Meguro Gajoen's Hyakudan Kaidan, Tokyo

Most of the photographs above were taken in the Hyakudan Kaidan ("hundred-step staircase") at Meguro Gajoen. Japan Travel says it was built as a wedding hall in the 1930s, is registered as a Tangible Cultural Asset of Tokyo, and has 99 wooden steps and seven function rooms. A recent Time Out Tokyo report says it reopened on October 1 after a year-long closure, with daytime tickets from ¥1,800. Please check the official site before you go, because prices, hours and exhibitions change. The ikebana exhibition in my photographs was a temporary event in 2018, so do not expect to find those arrangements today.

Lessons in Kyoto

Ikenobo's English page lists an ikebana trial lesson for visitors at the Rokkaku-do temple: Thursdays from 13:30 to 15:30 except national holidays, with a guide in English or Chinese, ¥7,000 per person, for groups of 2 to 10, booked by email. These details were on the page when I checked, so please confirm them. Kyoto Travel also describes a lesson with an Ikenobo instructor at Sakura Japanese Cultural Experience near Nijo Castle, with English-speaking staff. It does not give a price or length.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is ikebana?
Ikebana (生け花) is the Japanese art of arranging flowers, branches, leaves and berries. It is also called kado (華道), the way of flowers. It grew out of flower offerings made at Buddhist altars and is now taught by many schools.
Is ikebana the same as flower arranging in the West?
Not quite. A Western bouquet is usually about fullness. Ikebana usually works with line, space, the container and seasonal material, and a good arrangement often looks as much like a drawing as a bouquet. The photographs in this guide show the range, from a huge branch display to a single branch of berries.
What do heaven, human and earth mean in ikebana?
In some arrangements the main stems form a scalene triangle of three points that stand for heaven, human and earth (or sun, moon and earth). In the seika style they are called ten, chi and jin. Not every school or style uses this.
How is ikebana related to the tea ceremony?
Both belong to the alcove (tokonoma) tradition, and the flowers used in a tea room are called chabana. Wikipedia describes chabana as separate from ikebana, but notes that its simplicity helped create the nageire style.
Do I need experience to try a lesson?
The visitor trial lesson that Ikenobo lists on its English page is aimed at visitors and offers a guide in English or Chinese. The page does not state any experience requirement. Please check the current details before you book.
Where can I see ikebana without taking a lesson?
Exhibitions are the easiest way. The photographs in this guide were taken at exhibitions at Meguro Gajoen in Tokyo and in Nagoya in 2018. Those were temporary shows, so check what is on when you travel.

Final Thoughtsむすびに

Ikebana asks you to slow down and look at a few things closely. A branch, a vase, a shadow on a wall: that is the whole lesson. If you enjoy it, pair it with a tea gathering, where the same alcove holds a scroll and a few flowers.

The same attention to humble, imperfect objects appears in the repair art of kintsugi and in pottery such as Bizen ware, whose clay is also used for vases.
