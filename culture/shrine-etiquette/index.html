How to Visit a Japanese Shrine

神社参拝の作法ガイド

A Complete Etiquette Guide

Japan has roughly 80,000 Shinto shrines. Every one of them follows the same basic logic: a boundary between the ordinary world and sacred ground, a purification ritual before you enter the presence of a deity, and a prayer that follows a specific sequence. None of this requires you to be Shinto or religious in any particular way. The customs exist because they were designed to help people focus — to arrive at a shrine not distracted by whatever they were just doing, but present, clean-handed, and attentive. Knowing the sequence does not make you perform it better; it makes you less anxious about performing it, which is probably the point.

The Torii Gate鳥居

At a shrine entrance, you'll usually pass through a torii (鳥居) gate, which symbolically marks the boundary between the ordinary and the sacred. The gate is not decorative. It is a threshold, and crossing it is understood as a transition from the everyday world into the realm of the kami (神).

The center of the approach path (sandō, 参道) is considered the pathway of the gods (seichū, 正中), so walk along either side as a sign of respect. This is not a strict rule enforced by anyone, but it reflects a genuine belief that the central path is reserved for the deity. Bow once, briefly, before passing through the gate. When you leave, it is also polite to turn back and bow once more after passing through the torii.

The Temizuya: Purification手水舎

Temizu (手水) is the basic purification ritual you perform at a Japanese shrine before approaching the main hall, cleansing your hands and mouth to compose yourself for prayer. The temizuya (手水舎), also called chōzuya, is the stone or wooden basin with a roof that you will find near the entrance of virtually every shrine.

AT THE BASIN ／ 手水舎にて
One scoop of water 柄杓ひとすくい
01

Take the ladle (hishaku, 柄杓) in your right hand. Scoop water.

02

Pour water over your left hand.

03

Transfer the ladle to your left hand. Pour water over your right hand.

04

Transfer the ladle back to your right hand. Cup a small amount of water in your left palm and rinse your mouth — do not drink directly from the ladle, and spit quietly to the side, not into the basin.

05

Rinse your left hand once more.

06

Hold the ladle upright so the remaining water runs down the handle, then replace it.

Note: Paper towels are rarely provided, so it is customary in Japan to carry a small hand towel or handkerchief to dry your hands. The essence of temizu is being considerate of those around you and acting quietly, rather than striving for perfection.

The Prayer Ritual二礼二拍手一礼

At the haiden (拝殿), gently place your offering in the box, ring the bell if there is one, then bow twice, clap twice, and bow once to express gratitude or a prayer.

AT THE MAIN HALL ／ 拝殿にて
Ni-rei, ni-hakushu, ichi-rei 二礼二拍手一礼
01

The offering box (saisen-bako, 賽銭箱). Toss a coin into the box before praying. A ¥5 coin is traditional. The word for five yen, go-en (五円), is a homophone for "good connection" or "good fortune."

02

The bell (suzu, 鈴). If there is a large rope hanging from the ceiling with a bell attached, shake it gently to ring it.

03

Two bows (ni-hai, 二拝). Bow deeply from the waist — roughly 90 degrees — twice.

04

Two claps (ni-hakushu, 二拍手). Press your palms together and clap twice. Your right hand should be positioned slightly lower than your left when your hands come together.

05

Prayer. With hands still pressed together, make your prayer silently.

06

One final bow (ippai, 一拝). Bow once more deeply to close.

Exception: Izumo Taisha uses four claps rather than two. If you visit Izumo, adjust accordingly.

Shrines and Temples: The Key Difference神社とお寺の違い

Shrines are Shinto; temples are Buddhist. Shrines are identified by the torii gate at the entrance and the practice of clapping hands. Praying at a temple usually involves quietly placing your offering, bowing once, and joining your hands together in front of your chest to pray, and then bowing again at the end. There is no clapping.

Omamori: Protective Charmsお守り

Omamori (お守り) are the small fabric amulets sold at shrine and temple offices. Each is dedicated to a specific purpose. The charm should not be opened. Most omamori are intended to be replaced annually; after about a year, return the old one to a shrine or temple where it will be ceremonially burned with thanks.

For International Travelers: If you are a visitor and cannot return to Japan, do not worry. It is perfectly acceptable to keep the omamori as a lifelong, respectful souvenir in your home country rather than throwing it away.

Choosing, carrying, and returning them is covered in our Complete Omamori Guide.

Goshuin: The Red Seal Stamps御朱印

Goshuin (御朱印) are the red-stamped, hand-calligraphed records that shrine and temple offices write into a dedicated album (goshuincho, 御朱印帳) as evidence of your visit. They are considered sacred records of pilgrimage. To receive a goshuin, bring your goshuincho to the shrine or temple office, hand it over open to the next blank page, and make a small offering (typically ¥300 to ¥500).

Etiquette, kanji reading, and troubleshooting are in our Complete Goshuin Guide.

Ema: Votive Plaques絵馬

Ema (絵馬) are small wooden plaques on which visitors write prayers or wishes, then hang them on a designated rack within the shrine precinct. Write your prayer or wish on the blank side of the plaque. There is no required formula for the wording.

What Not to Doしてはいけないこと

  • Do not walk down the center of the sandō approach path. The center is reserved for the kami.
  • Do not drink directly from the temizuya ladle, or spit into the basin.
  • Do not open an omamori.
  • Do not photograph inside worship halls unless you have confirmed it is permitted.
  • Do not be loud. Keeping voices low is not just courtesy — it is part of what makes the space work for everyone.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Do I need to be Shinto or religious to visit a shrine?
No. None of the customs require you to be Shinto or religious in any particular way. They exist to help people focus and arrive at a shrine present, clean-handed, and attentive.
How much should I put in the offering box?
A 5-yen coin is traditional, because the word for five yen, go-en, is a homophone for good connection or good fortune. There is no required amount.
Do I clap at a temple too?
No. Clapping is a shrine custom. At a temple you quietly place your offering, bow once, join your hands together in front of your chest to pray, and bow again at the end.
How many times do I clap at Izumo Taisha?
Four. Izumo Taisha uses four claps rather than the usual two, so if you visit Izumo, adjust accordingly.
Can I keep an omamori as a souvenir?
Yes. Omamori are normally replaced annually and returned to a shrine or temple, but if you are a visitor and cannot return to Japan, it is perfectly acceptable to keep the omamori as a lifelong, respectful souvenir in your home country.
How much does a goshuin cost?
A small offering, typically ¥300 to ¥500, paid at the shrine or temple office when you hand over your goshuincho, open to the next blank page.
Can I take photos at a shrine?
Do not photograph inside worship halls unless you have confirmed it is permitted.
