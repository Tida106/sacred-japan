Kabuki: The Theater of Japan Guide

歌舞伎ガイド

History, Makeup, Famous Plays and How to Watch at Kabukiza

A man in white paint and red lines across his face stops in the middle of a fight, glares at the audience and holds the pose, eyes crossed, for a long breath. Someone in the dark shouts a name. The whole theater exhales. That frozen moment, called a mie, is kabuki in a nutshell: it is not trying to look real, it is trying to look unforgettable.

As a licensed guide, I find that kabuki is the easiest of Japan's classical theaters to enjoy as a visitor, because it is built to be spectacular. This guide explains where it came from, why only men perform it now, how to read the makeup, and how to buy a ticket without committing to five hours.

A Shrine Maiden on a Dry Riverbed出雲阿国と歌舞伎の始まり

The story starts in Kyoto in 1603. The Web Japan introduction to kabuki says kabuki is thought to have begun with dances and light theater performed there by Okuni, a female attendant at the Izumo shrine. Metropolis magazine adds that Izumo no Okuni danced with her troupe on the dry bed of the Kamo River, and that she dressed in costumes considered off-kilter for the time. Many sources call her a shrine maiden of Izumo, and I would say "said to be," since the details of her life are thin.

The name explains the spirit. Facts and Details says kabuki is derived from a word meaning tilted, offbeat or outrageous, and the Gate says the performers copied the fashion of the kabuki-mono, the flamboyant rebels of the day. So the art form began as a show-off dance that borrowed its look from the street.

If you have read our Izumo Taisha guide, the link is a nice one: the shrine at the root of this story is the same Izumo, so the biggest popular theater in Japan has a shrine in its family tree.

Why Only Men Perform女形と禁止令

Kabuki started with women and ended up with men. The Web Japan introduction gives the sequence: the shogunate banned women's troupes in 1629 because of their links to prostitution; young men's (wakashu) kabuki became popular next but was banned in 1652; and adult men's kabuki (yaro kabuki) was then allowed to continue, under government rules to avoid sensual displays and to follow the conventions of kyogen.

Out of that restriction came a specialty. Male actors who play women's roles are called onnagata, and the same source says their goal is to express the essence of the feminine, not to imitate women. The JAPN guide goes further and says that many consider their movement more feminine than that of actual women; that is an opinion, not a fact, but it shows how the art is valued.

Two Edo-period actors also set the poles of acting. The Web Japan introduction says Ichikawa Danjuro I (1660-1704) pioneered the masculine, exaggerated aragoto style in Edo, while Sakata Tojuro I (1647-1709) developed the refined, realistic wagoto style in the Kyoto-Osaka area. You can still see the difference today: the Edo side is loud and heroic, the Kamigata side soft and human.

The Stage and What to Watch舞台と見どころ

Kabuki has a vocabulary, and a few words make the show much easier.

  • Mie (見得): the climactic pose where the actor stops and holds a fixed stare, per the Web Japan introduction.
  • Hanamichi (花道): the "flower path," a runway that cuts through the audience for entrances and exits. Sit near it, and an actor will pass within arm's reach.
  • Revolving stage: the same source says it was first used in 1758, which lets a set change in front of your eyes.
  • Draw curtain: added to the stage that evolved from noh, it made complex multi-act plays possible.

Music is live, with singers and shamisen players at the side, and a wooden clapper keeps time. I describe those from general knowledge of kabuki, not from the sources above, so treat them as a guide's impression.

Edo's audience loved this too. The print by Utagawa Toyokuni III of the July 1858 production of Shibaraku that the JRailPass guide mentions is a good example, and our ukiyo-e guide explains how actors became the pop stars of those prints. The same Edo crowd is also behind our sumo guide: the Japan Sumo Association itself says that, like kabuki, sumo became a mass attraction in the Edo period.

Makeup and the Colors隈取

The painted faces are called kumadori. Kyoto Travel says the face is painted white, with thick red or blue lines on top, that the lines depend on the role, and that all the makeup is applied by the actors themselves. The Web Japan introduction says there are about a hundred styles.

  • Red: heroes, courage, justice and strength (Kyoto Travel, Web Japan, JAPN).
  • Blue or indigo: villains, jealousy, cold cunning or vengeful spirits. GLTJP specifically says blue is for powerful villains or vengeful spirits.
  • Brown: demons, ogres and supernatural beings (GLTJP, JAPN).

Other sites give longer lists, with pink for youth or purple for nobility, but I found those in less careful sources, so I keep to the three that several agree on. The rule of thumb is simple: red is good, blue is bad, brown is not human.

Three Kinds of Play時代物・世話物・所作事

The Web Japan introduction divides the repertoire into three main genres, each with an example it names:

  • Jidai-mono (historical plays): often set in an earlier era to avoid the censors. Kanjincho is adapted from the noh play Ataka and features Benkei's trick at a barrier checkpoint, and Kanadehon Chushingura is based on the 47 ronin incident of 1701-1703 (set in the Muromachi period on stage). If the warriors interest you, our samurai guide gives the background.
  • Sewa-mono (domestic plays): more realistic, often based on recent scandals. Benten Kozo by Kawatake Mokuami (1816-1893) follows a thief and his gang.
  • Shosagoto (dance pieces): often used to show off the best onnagata. Kyo-ganoko musume Dojoji is the example given.

For a first visit, I would pick a dance or a famous historical piece, since the plot matters less than the look. If the program is a dance, you will not need to follow the lines at all.

Where to See Kabuki劇場ガイド

  • Kabukiza, Ginza, Tokyo: the main venue. The Gate says the first Kabukiza opened in 1889 and the fifth, current one in 2013, with quakeproof construction and barrier-free access. Seating is reported at roughly 1,800 (JAPN) or 1,900 (Facts and Details), so I give no exact figure. Higashi-Ginza Station Exit 3 connects to the theater directly.
  • Minami-za, Kyoto: described as the oldest kabuki theater in Japan and the only one left in Kyoto, in the Gion area and near the Kamo River; Kanpai Japan says it was renovated in 2016. Both it and Kabukiza are run by Shochiku.
  • Shochikuza, Osaka: shows run less often, according to GetNomad.
  • National Theatre, Tokyo: GetNomad says it has "discovery" shows aimed at beginners, with English synopses, and Japan Travel says audio guides are provided for selected performances.
  • Others: Shinbashi Enbujo in Tokyo and Hakataza in Fukuoka also host kabuki runs, according to several guides.

Kabukiza also has a gallery where, GLTJP says, you can try a carriage used in actual performances and touch the props. It is a good fallback if the schedule does not suit you.

Tickets and Single Actsチケットと一幕見

A normal Kabukiza program has a day and an evening session, each about 3 to 4 hours with intermissions of about 25 to 30 minutes, according to The Gate. That is a long time for a first visit, which is why the single act is so useful.

Full-session tickets

The Gate says standard tickets are sold online through Ticket Web Shochiku, or by phone, at about ¥3,000 to ¥20,000 per session. Other guides quote up to ¥25,000, so check the official site. Monthly stories and casts are listed on Kabuki Web, which the Gate and the Shinohara guide both mention.

Single-act tickets (hitomakumi)

These are unique to Kabukiza. The Gate says the seats are on the fourth floor, about 90 seats plus a standing area for about 60, first-come, first-served, and sold at a box office to the left of the main entrance. The price is reported as about ¥1,000 to ¥3,000, and GLTJP and others say about ¥2,000. The sources differ on the sale: the Gate and GLTJP say same day only, while GOGO Nihon says the seats were revived in June 2023 and can also be booked online from noon the day before. I would check the official page, and I would arrive early if a famous actor is in the cast, since the Gate says those acts sell out fast.

Captions

The Gate lists a caption guide with Japanese and English text for ¥500 plus a ¥1,000 deposit for a single act, and ¥1,000 with an ID for a full show. GLTJP quotes an older price of ¥700. Because the numbers differ, treat them as approximate.

Manners and Kakegoe観劇のマナー

  • Phones and cameras: Pimsleur's guide says to turn off your phone, refrain from photography and stay in your seat.
  • Food: The Gate says you may eat and drink at your seat during intermission, and a designated vendor sells bento boxes on the second basement floor.
  • Kakegoe: shouted calls from the audience, an old part of kabuki. Sakura's guide says only men traditionally took part, and women do now. As I said above, listen first.
  • Dress: the sources I read give no dress code, so I would say smart-casual is fine; that is my own view.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Do I need to understand Japanese to enjoy kabuki?
No, but you will want help. The Gate (a Tokyo tourism site) says kabuki's old-style Japanese is hard even for many native speakers, and that Kabukiza offers an English caption guide on a small screen, like an airplane seatback. GLTJP and the National Theatre also mention English guides. Please check the day's availability before you go.
Can I watch just one act?
At Kabukiza, yes. These single-act tickets are called hitomakumi or makumi. The Gate says the seats are on the fourth floor, about 90 seats plus standing room for about 60, and that an act runs from about 30 minutes to 1.5 hours. Prices are reported as roughly ¥1,000 to ¥3,000, depending on the source.
How much do full tickets cost?
Sources disagree a little. The Gate gives ¥3,000 to ¥20,000 per session; other guides give up to ¥25,000 for the best seats. Prices depend on the seat and the program, so check the official ticket site for your date.
Can I take photos?
Not during the performance. Pimsleur's kabuki etiquette guide says to turn off your phone, refrain from any photography and stay in your seats. Kabukiza also has a gallery where, GLTJP says, you can touch props and take photographs, which is a better place for pictures.
Why are all the actors men?
Because of a shogunate ban. The Web Japan introduction says women's troupes were banned in 1629 because of links to prostitution, and young men's kabuki followed in 1652. The male actors who played women's roles, onnagata, then became a specialty. Kabuki was started by a woman, Izumo no Okuni, and that is one of its best-known ironies.
What should I shout from the audience?
I would not shout anything on your first visit. Kakegoe are shouted calls, usually an actor's house name, at a precise moment, and Sakura's guide says that traditionally only men took part, though women now do too. Learn the timing by listening to the regulars before you join in.
Is kabuki the same as noh?
No. Noh is the older, slower, mask-based art that kabuki borrowed from. The Web Japan introduction says the kabuki stage gradually evolved out of the noh stage, and that some kabuki plays are adapted from noh. Noh is a separate guide for later.

Final Thoughtsむすびに

If you remember one thing, remember the single act. One hour, a fourth-floor seat, a pair of English captions: that is enough to see a hero's pose, a villain's blue face and a revolving stage, and to understand why people have loved this for four centuries.

If you enjoy this side of Japan, the Edo-period world around it is in our ukiyo-e guide and sumo guide.
