Noh: The Oldest Theater Guide

能楽ガイド

Masks, Ghost Stories, the Stage and How to Watch Your First Performance

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

A traveling monk arrives at a quiet place and meets a stranger who tells him a local story. As the evening goes on, the stranger turns out to be the ghost of someone who has been dead for centuries, and after an interval the ghost comes back in his true form to dance the story out. That is the plot of a surprising number of noh plays, and it explains why noh feels less like a drama and more like a ceremony for the dead.

As a licensed guide, I recommend noh to visitors who like Japan's quieter side. This guide explains where it came from, how the stage and the masks work, the five kinds of play, and how to watch a first performance with English subtitles without falling asleep.

Kan'ami, Zeami and the Shogun観阿弥と世阿弥

The Web Japan introduction to noh says noh emerged as a distinct theatrical form in the 14th century and describes it as the oldest extant professional theater in the world. It grew out of a popular genre called sarugaku, which Kan'ami (1333-1384) and his son Zeami (1363-1443) transformed into noh. Kan'ami added music and dance from the popular entertainment kuse-mai and won the patronage of the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu (1358-1408).

After Kan'ami died, Zeami led the Kanze troupe, refined the aesthetics and wrote essays that set the standards of performance for later centuries. Two of his ideas are worth knowing. Yugen is a Zen-influenced ideal that stresses the suggestion of mystery and depth, and monomane means the imitation of things. If you remember only one, remember yugen: noh tries to hint, not to show.

Later history is a story of institutions. Japan Guide says that Zeami fell out of favor and was banished to Sado Island, which is a single-source detail I give only as reported. The Web Japan introduction says that in the 17th century noh became an official property of the Tokugawa shogunate and grew slower and more solemn; Japan Guide adds that the shogunate made it its official ceremonial art and issued regulations, and that five main troupes perform to this day. After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, performers such as Umewaka Minoru I (1828-1909) and noble patronage kept it alive, and since the end of World War II it has depended on public support. If you have read our samurai guide, the warrior-class patronage will make sense.

A Stage Built Like a Shrine能舞台

The noh stage looks almost empty, and that is the point. Web Japan says the main stage measures 6 by 6 meters, is built of polished hinoki cypress and has a Shinto-style roof. Japan Guide adds that the roof is supported by four corner pillars and that the stage is open on three sides. A painted pine tree on the back wall is the only backdrop for every play, and an angled bridge, the hashigakari, serves as the entrance passage. The stage was originally outdoors and is now usually housed in a larger building.

I would compare it with the ring in our sumo guide: both are spaces with a shrine roof, and both are treated as set apart. Keep your eyes on the bridge. Many entrances happen there, slowly, and the audience watches a ghost walk toward them for a full minute.

Who Is Who on Stage役柄

Roles are fixed, and learning them makes the stage readable. These come from the Web Japan introduction and Japan Guide, which agree except where I flag it.

  • Shite: the leading character, usually masked. Depending on the play, Japan Guide says the shite may be a holy old man, a deity, a demon, a spirit or a living man.
  • Waki: the supporting role, unmasked, and always a living person such as a priest, monk or samurai. The waki is the one who meets the ghost.
  • Tsure, wakitsure, kokata: accompanying characters, and the child role.
  • Jiutai: the chorus, which voices the lead's words and thoughts. The two sources disagree on which side of the stage it sits on (stage right in one, left in the other), so I give no side.
  • Hayashikata: the musicians, with a flute, a small hand drum (kotsuzumi), a large hand drum (otsuzumi) and, when needed, a floor drum (taiko).
  • Koken: stage attendants in black who help the performers; Japan Guide says they are not part of the play.

The other great tool is the folding fan. Web Japan calls it a key hand prop that stands in for objects or actions, and Japan Guide says it can stand for a dagger or a lantern.

The Masks能面

Masks are worn by the shite to signal the character type, according to Japan Guide, and are carved from Japanese cypress. The types include young woman, old man and demon, and the Web Japan introduction says each has a level of dignity, kurai, that shapes the performance. Kanpai Japan says the woman's mask follows the idealized beauty of the Heian period, and that demon masks have golden or red hues, big eyes and a grin showing the teeth.

The most famous is the hannya, a female demon mask. In Aoi no Ue (Lady Aoi), a play Zeami revised from The Tale of Genji, Web Japan says the shite switches from a woman's mask, deigan, to the hannya, which is the turn from grief to jealousy in a single costume change. Japan Guide says a skilled actor can change the expression of a fixed mask by tilting the head. The next time someone calls a noh mask blank, watch the tilt of the head.

Five Kinds of Play五番立て

Web Japan says about 240 plays survive today, in five groups. Guides name some of the groups differently, so I give the Web Japan list first:

  1. God plays (waki noh): a deity blesses the land. Kanpai Japan says the god appears as an old man and the piece mixes song, dance and Shinto ritual.
  2. Warrior plays (shuramono): usually a monk meets the soul of a past warrior who is stuck in the cycle of rebirth.
  3. Wig plays (kazura-mono): woman plays, centered on female characters, often caught between love and sorrow.
  4. Miscellaneous plays (zatsu noh): other guides call this group "madness" plays, with characters driven to madness by tragedy.
  5. Demon plays (kiri-noh): short, fast pieces with supernatural beings.

Web Japan says plays follow the jo-ha-kyu pattern: introduction, exposition and a rapid finale. A traditional program had five noh plays and three or four kyogen; Japan Guide says today's programs usually have two or three noh plays. If you are choosing, JAPN's advice is to pick a demon or warrior play for more visible action. The plot of a woman play can be a single memory, and I find those the most moving, but I would not start there.

Kyogen: The Comedy in Between狂言

Noh is not performed alone. Web Japan says independent kyogen are comic plays performed between noh plays, with roots in entertainment brought from China in the 8th century or earlier. Japan Guide says they last about 15 to 20 minutes, use two or three actors and rely on satire, wit, rhythmic language and exaggerated action, with plots from everyday life. Web Japan also says that within a noh play a kyogen actor often bridges the two acts, retelling the story in colloquial language while the lead changes costume. That is the interval I mentioned in the opening.

Web Japan names about 260 independent kyogen plays, of which about 50 use masks, usually for non-human characters. The kyogen in a noh program is the part everybody understands, and it is the part you are allowed to laugh at.

Where to Watch能楽堂

  • National Noh Theatre, Sendagaya, Tokyo: opened in 1983 according to Japan Travel, with seating for 627 and English subtitles on the seats. Japan Travel also lists it as 5 minutes from the Sendagaya Ekimae bus stop, and an operator listing puts it near Kita-Sando and Sendagaya stations; check a map for your route.
  • Other Tokyo venues: Japan Guide lists the Kanze Noh Theater in Ginza and the Cerulean Tower Noh Theatre in Shibuya. Matcha adds the Hosho Noh Theater in Suidobashi, with the caution that outside the National Noh Theatre it is hard to find programs and explanations in English.
  • Elsewhere: Otsuki Noh Theatre in Osaka and the Nagoya Noh Theatre next to Nagoya Castle (Japan Guide).
  • Shrines and temples: Japan Guide lists historic outdoor stages at Itsukushima Shrine on Miyajima, where the stage stands on pillars in the sea, Chusonji Temple in Hiraizumi and Fushimi Inari Shrine, as well as Yasukuni Shrine in Tokyo. Performances there are occasional and tickets can be hard to get.

Tickets and First-Timer Tips鑑賞のコツ

Prices vary by source and program. Japan Travel and JAPN say ¥3,000 to ¥6,000, Time Out quoted ¥3,400 to ¥5,200 for one program, and Japan Guide gives ¥3,000 to ¥12,500. Japan Travel says some performances are split into two parts with tickets sold separately, and that buying both can qualify for a discount. Japan Guide says tickets are sold at the venue counter or online and that online purchases often require Japanese; some venues sell discounted single-act tickets, which suits a first visit.

For a ready-made option, one listing on a Japan tour-booking site describes a 2025 Noh Experience Tour at the National Noh Theatre with a 40-minute lecture, a mask experience and a two-hour performance with English subtitles, for ¥15,000. I only saw that listing, so treat the details as unconfirmed and check the operator.

My own advice, as a guide, is to read the synopsis before the lights go down, since the subtitles give you the words and the program gives you the story, and to let the slowness happen. If a scene feels empty, look at the shite's hands and at the fan.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is noh boring?
It is slow on purpose, and I would not pretend otherwise. Japan Guide describes it as built on song and dance with slow movement, poetic language and a monotone delivery. The way in is to treat it as a ritual you watch, not a plot you follow. JAPN suggests that first-timers pick a program with a demon or warrior play, since those have more visible action than the subtler woman plays.
Do I need Japanese?
Not at the National Noh Theatre. Japan Travel and Time Out both say it has English subtitles on small screens at the seats, though Japan Travel also says that at some performances an English booklet is what is offered. Kyogen is usually performed without subtitles, according to a report about one other theatre, so check the program.
How long is a performance?
Less than you fear. Japan Guide says a typical program today lasts a couple of hours, with two or three noh plays and short kyogen pieces in between, and JAPN says individual plays run 30 to 90 minutes. Web Japan says a traditional program had five noh plays, so a full day is the old format.
How much do tickets cost?
Japan Travel and JAPN give about ¥3,000 to ¥6,000, Time Out quoted ¥3,400 to ¥5,200 for one program, and Japan Guide gives a wider ¥3,000 to ¥12,500. Japan Travel adds that students under 26 may get half price. Please check the theatre's own page for your date.
Why are the actors all men?
Japan Guide states that all performers are male, and the shogunate made noh its official ceremonial art in the Tokugawa period. Women performers have appeared in recent times, but the sources I read do not cover that, so I leave it at the tradition.
What is the difference between noh and kabuki?
Noh is the older, quieter, mask-based art of suggestion; kabuki is the flamboyant, popular theater of the Edo period. The Web Japan introduction to kabuki says its stage gradually evolved out of the noh stage and that some plays are adapted from noh. Our kabuki guide explains the other side.
Can I see noh at a shrine?
Sometimes. Japan Guide lists Itsukushima Shrine, Chusonji Temple, Fushimi Inari Shrine and Yasukuni Shrine as places with historic outdoor stages and occasional performances, and warns that tickets can be hard to get. Web Japan adds that outdoor night performances by firelight, takiginoh, have grown popular in recent years.

Final Thoughtsむすびに

If you remember one thing, remember the ghost. In noh the dead return to tell their stories, the living listen, and a pine tree painted on a wall is all the scenery you need. Give it one program and let it be slow.

If you enjoy this side of Japan, the livelier theater of the same country is in our kabuki guide, and the tea room next door to it in our tea ceremony guide.
