The Three Sacred Treasures of Japan

三種の神器

A Sword Lost at Sea, a Mirror No One Has Seen, a Jewel in a Sealed Box

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ・ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

The sea at Dan-no-ura was running red. It was the spring of 1185, the last battle of the great war between the Taira and the Minamoto, and the Taira ships were breaking apart in the narrow strait. On the imperial vessel stood Taira no Tokiko, the child emperor's grandmother. She knew the war was lost. According to The Tale of the Heike, she gathered the little emperor into her arms, took two of Japan's Three Sacred Treasures with her, and stepped over the side.

The mirror was caught before it sank. The jewel was found floating in its box. The sword, the Kusanagi no Tsurugi, was never seen again. Or so the story goes, and that is the strange part: more than eight hundred years later, Japanese emperors are still handed a sword and a jewel at their accession.

I am a licensed guide, and when I take people to Ise or Nagoya, this is the story they ask about most. How can the treasures be lost and still be there? How do you guard objects that no one is allowed to look at? Let me walk you through the myth, the battle and the shrines, in the order the Japanese tell it.

The Cave, the Dance and the Mirror天岩戸とヤタノカガミ

Begin with the sun. Amaterasu, goddess of the sun and ancestor of the imperial line, quarrelled with her brother, the storm god Susanoo, and withdrew into the Heavenly Rock Cave. The world went dark. The eight hundred myriad gods gathered outside and tried everything. They hung a jewel and a mirror on a tree before the entrance, and the goddess Ame-no-Uzume stepped onto an overturned tub and danced until the gods roared with laughter.

Amaterasu, curious, opened the cave door a crack. In the mirror she saw a shining figure and, startled by her own reflection, leaned closer. A strong god pulled her out, a sacred rope was strung across the entrance so she could never retreat again, and light returned to the world. That mirror is the Yata no Kagami (八咫鏡), and the jewel hung beside it is the Yasakani no Magatama (八尺瓊勾玉).

You can stand where Japanese tradition places this scene. Takachiho, in Miyazaki, has a gorge, a cave shrine and a river bed full of stacked stones that locals say belong to the gods.

The Serpent, the Storm God and the Sword八岐大蛇と草薙剣

Susanoo was exiled from heaven for the trouble he caused, and he came down to Izumo with nothing. By a river he found an elderly couple weeping. Seven of their eight daughters had been devoured by the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent Yamata no Orochi, and it was about to return for the last.

Susanoo asked for her hand in marriage and set a trap: eight vats of strong sake, one for each head. The serpent drank, fell asleep, and the god cut it to pieces. When his blade struck the middle tail, the edge chipped. Puzzled, Susanoo opened the tail and found a great sword inside, wrapped, the Nihon Shoki says, in gathering clouds. It was named Ame-no-Murakumo-no-Tsurugi, the Sword of the Gathering Clouds of Heaven.

He did not keep it. He sent it to Amaterasu, the sister he had wronged. It is a quiet, human moment in a violent story: the treasure of an empire begins as an apology. If you want to visit the land of this tale, Izumo Taisha is the great shrine of the Izumo region.

The Prince, the Burning Field and Atsuta倭建命と熱田神宮

Generations later the sword reappears with Prince Yamato Takeru, the great warrior-hero of the early chronicles. Before he set out to subdue the east, he visited his aunt Yamatohime, a priestess at Ise, who gave him the sword and a bag of fire-making tools.

A treacherous lord lured him onto an open plain during a hunt and set the grass alight. Trapped, Yamato Takeru cut away the grass with the sword, struck fire and let the wind carry the blaze back at his enemies. He renamed the blade Kusanagi, the Grass-Cutter.

Later he married Princess Miyazu and left the sword with her when he went to face the god of Mount Ibuki, a fight that he lost. According to the chronicles, he did not survive the journey home, and Miyazu enshrined the sword where Atsuta Shrine stands in Nagoya. The sword, a hero's last unfinished business, has stayed there ever since.

History adds its own footnote. The Nihon Shoki records that in 688 the sword was blamed for the illness of Emperor Tenmu and was sent back to Atsuta.

The Gift That Made an Emperor天孫降臨

Now the treasures meet. When Amaterasu sent her grandson Ninigi down to rule the earth, the event the Japanese call Tenson Korin, she gave him the three: the mirror, the jewel and the sword. The mirror was meant to be worshipped as if it were her own spirit. From Ninigi the line runs, in the tradition, to Emperor Jimmu and on to the present.

That is why Japan has a regalia but no crown. The three objects stand for three virtues: wisdom in the mirror, benevolence in the jewel and valor in the sword. More than that, they are proof of descent. Whoever holds them is the sun goddess's heir.

TREASURE 01 ・ MIRROR

Yata no Kagami (八咫鏡)

Virtue: Wisdom

The mirror that coaxed the sun out of a cave. Tradition places it in the inner sanctuary of Ise. Records describe palace fires that damaged or destroyed a mirror, with sources dating one to 1005 or 1040, and a copy is kept in the Kashikodokoro, the palace sanctuary in Tokyo.

Enshrined at: Ise Grand Shrine (Mie Prefecture)
TREASURE 02 ・ SWORD

Kusanagi no Tsurugi (草薙剣)

Virtue: Valor

The blade from the serpent's tail. Atsuta Shrine is its traditional home, but the Tale of the Heike says it sank in 1185. A replica is used at accessions, and one tradition says the sword at Atsuta is a replacement.

Enshrined at: Atsuta Shrine (Nagoya, Aichi)
TREASURE 03 ・ JEWEL

Yasakani no Magatama (八尺瓊勾玉)

Virtue: Benevolence

A string of magatama, comma-shaped beads found across ancient Japan. It is the treasure that, in the story, floated on the waves at Dan-no-ura. Tradition keeps it in the palace in Tokyo, and the emperor receives it at accession.

Kept at: The Imperial Palace (Tokyo)

1185: The Sea Battle at Dan-no-ura壇ノ浦の戦い

The Genpei War ended on boats in the narrow Kanmon Strait. The Minamoto fleet under Yoshitsune pressed the Taira, who carried the child Emperor Antoku and the regalia with them. As the Taira line collapsed, Antoku's grandmother Taira no Tokiko took the boy and, in the telling of The Tale of the Heike, plunged into the sea with two of the treasures.

The mirror was saved in the nick of time, when a lady-in-waiting was about to jump with it. The jewel was found floating in its box. Of the sword, the tale simply says it was lost.

At Akama Shrine, where the drowned Emperor Antoku is enshrined, two stone monuments stand beside the buildings. The tall one is carved with 八咫鏡 奉鎮, roughly "the Yata no Kagami, enshrined", and, as I read the characters, says that the three treasures were passed down from the tenth emperor, Sujin, to the eighty-first, Antoku, over 1,276 years. The wide slab beside it appears to honour a man named Haruna Yoshio for digging up and dedicating a mirror. I cannot read the whole inscription with confidence, so take this as a sign of how alive the story still is at the place where it ended, not as evidence about the real mirror.

Then comes the part I love as a guide, because every Japanese person knows it is not settled. Medieval texts disagree. Some say a replica was forged afterwards. Some say the sword that went into the sea was already a replica. Others say that divers or the sea itself returned it. A few years earlier, a rival emperor had even been enthroned without the regalia, so the idea of an emperor without the treasures was not new, and it hurt.

What no one disputes is the effect. The Taira lost, and the regalia, in some form, passed on. Whether the sword at Atsuta is the blade from the serpent's tail, a copy made in the early thirteenth century as some accounts say, or a replacement made after 1185, nobody outside the priesthood can say. That is exactly why it is still compelling.

Why Has No One Seen Them?なぜ誰も見ることができないのか

In Shinto, an object that holds a deity's presence, a shintai, is not put on display. Power is felt more strongly when it is hidden, and the treasures are the most hidden of all. By tradition, only an emperor and certain priests may see them.

There is one famous exception, and it is a warning. During repairs at Atsuta in the Edo period, a priest named Matsuoka Masanao claimed to have seen the sword. He described a stone box inside a wooden box, and a blade about 82 centimetres long, shaped like a calamus leaf. The story adds that the chief priest was banished and the other priests who had looked died of strange illnesses, leaving him as the only witness. I tell it as a legend, because it is one account, and no one can check it.

"In Shinto, power often lies in the unseen. The invisible presence is felt more keenly precisely because it is hidden away from the mundane world."

The Treasures Today現代の三種の神器

In the summer of 1945, with Japan on the brink of defeat, Emperor Hirohito told his Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal that he thought it best to move the treasures of Ise and Atsuta close to him and protect them, and that in an emergency he would protect them himself and share their fate. His great fear was that they would fall into enemy hands.

On 1 May 2019, when Naruhito became emperor, the ceremony took place in the Imperial Palace. The sword and the jewel were carried in and presented, wrapped, to the new emperor. The mirror stayed in its sanctuary, because a copy of it is kept in the palace and never leaves. To an observer, the treasures were only brocade-wrapped boxes. Even today, the object of the ceremony is an act of faith, and it still works.

The Three Treasures in Animeアニメの中の三種の神器

You may already know the treasures without having read the chronicles. They keep turning up in anime, and two of the best-known cases are in series watched all over the world.

In One Piece, the Marine Admiral Kizaru (Borsalino) has a body of light and three signature attacks named after the regalia. Yata no Kagami has him reflect light to travel at light speed. Yasakani no Magatama is a rain of light beams. Ame no Murakumo is a sword made of light, and that is the old name of the Kusanagi sword in the myths. Fans and analysts have pointed out the naming (The Library of Ohara, My Anime Thoughts). I have not seen the author comment on it, so I offer it as an observation, not an announcement.

In Naruto, Orochimaru, whose name contains orochi, the serpent of the myth, wields a Sword of Kusanagi that he draws from his own mouth and can extend at will. Sasuke later carries a sword with the same name. It is a plain straight blade, a chokuto, without Orochimaru's special powers, though he can run lightning chakra through it (CBR). Sportskeeda says Orochimaru gave it to him. Since it is a second blade with the same name, I would not describe it as one sword handed down. The echo with the myth is still there: a sword tied to a serpent ends up with a young man who has left home.

Sources for this section: The Library of Ohara, My Anime Thoughts, CBR, Sportskeeda. For anime places you can actually visit, see our anime pilgrimage guides.

Where to Stand Near the Legend伝説の舞台を訪ねる

You cannot see the treasures, but you can visit the places that guard them. Here is how I would sequence them for a traveler:

01

Ise Grand Shrine (Jingu)

Mie Prefecture

Home of the sacred mirror and of Amaterasu herself. Walk the gravel approach to the Inner Shrine and the sanctuary stays hidden behind thatched fences. That absence is the experience. Read our Ise guide before you go.

02

Atsuta Shrine

Nagoya, Aichi

Where Miyazu enshrined the sword, in a camphor forest in the middle of Nagoya. It is the easiest of the three to reach, and the Treasure Hall displays swords donated over the centuries. See the Atsuta guide.

03

Imperial Palace East Gardens

Tokyo

The jewel and the palace sanctuary are not open to visitors, but the East Gardens next door are, and they sit on the grounds of the old Edo Castle. Our Edo Castle guide explains what remains.

04

Akama Shrine

Shimonoseki, Yamaguchi

For the sea. It enshrines the child Emperor Antoku, who drowned at Dan-no-ura, and its vermilion Suiten-mon gate is built in the dragon-palace style, as if offering an undersea palace to the boy. The shrine was destroyed in wartime bombing and rebuilt in 1965 (MLIT). The Heike mounds and the hall of the ghost story Hoichi the Earless are in the grounds.

From the road, Akama Shrine announces itself with a long stone stairway and a white base lifted under a red tower. The people on the steps in the first photo show the scale. The second photo shows the gold ornaments on the roof and the green tiles, which are what make the gate look like a palace rather than a shrine gate. It faces the Kanmon Strait, so on a clear day the gate and the water are in the same view.

At Ise, the walk is the point. This gate marks the approach to the Inner Shrine, and beyond it the sanctuary stays hidden behind thatched fences, so what you take home is the quiet of the approach.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくある質問

What are the Three Sacred Treasures of Japan?
They are the Imperial Regalia of Japan, the Sanshu no Jingi: the mirror Yata no Kagami (wisdom), the sword Kusanagi no Tsurugi (valor) and the jewel Yasakani no Magatama (benevolence). In myth, the sun goddess Amaterasu gave them to her grandson Ninigi, and they stand for the emperor's divine descent.
Where are the Three Sacred Treasures kept?
By tradition, the mirror is at Ise Grand Shrine (Mie), the sword at Atsuta Shrine (Nagoya) and the jewel at the Imperial Palace in Tokyo. Their locations are never officially verified by outsiders, and none can be viewed by the public. A replica sword and the jewel are the two items brought to a new emperor, and a copy of the mirror is kept in the palace sanctuary.
Was the Kusanagi sword really lost at the Battle of Dan-no-ura?
The Tale of the Heike says it sank with the Taira in 1185 and was never recovered. Other medieval accounts say the lost sword was only a replica, or that a new one was forged afterwards, or that it returned from the sea by supernatural means. Atsuta Shrine continues to hold what it venerates as the sword. No outsider can confirm any version.
Can anyone see the treasures?
No. By tradition only an emperor and certain priests may see them, and at the 2019 accession the sword and jewel were presented to Emperor Naruhito wrapped in boxes, with the mirror never leaving its sanctuary. An Edo-period Atsuta priest claimed to have glimpsed the sword, but his is a single account.
Are the treasures real, and how old are they?
No one can say. They are never examined publicly, so their age and authenticity rest on faith and tradition. Historians note fires, thefts and sea battles in the records, and replicas of the sword and mirror are believed to have been made as early as the 9th century.
Do the Three Sacred Treasures appear in anime?
Yes. In One Piece, Admiral Kizaru's light-based attacks are named Yata no Kagami, Yasakani no Magatama and Ame no Murakumo, which is another name for the Kusanagi sword. In Naruto, Orochimaru wields a Sword of Kusanagi, and Sasuke later carries a different sword with the same name.
Can I visit the shrines connected to the treasures?
Yes. Ise Grand Shrine, Atsuta Shrine and Akama Shrine in Shimonoseki all welcome visitors, though you will not see the treasures. The Imperial Palace itself is closed to ordinary visitors, but its East Gardens are open to the public.

A Mystery Worth Traveling For旅する価値のある神秘

A sword may have sunk off Shimonoseki in 1185. A mirror was burned in a palace and copied. A jewel travels in a box no one opens. And still the line of emperors goes on, carried by objects that cannot be looked at. That is what I find most moving about Japan: a nation can build its deepest memory around something it chooses not to see.

Stand at Ise or beneath the camphor trees of Atsuta, and you are not looking at the treasures. You are standing inside the story that gives them their power.
