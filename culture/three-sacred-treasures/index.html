The Three Sacred Treasures of Japan

三種の神器

The Mirror, Sword, and Jewel No One Is Allowed to See

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ・ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

Somewhere in Japan, locked away in shrines and a palace vault, are three objects so sacred that almost no living person has ever laid eyes on them. Not tourists. Not scholars. Not even most of the imperial family. When a new emperor ascends the throne, two of them are carried into the ceremony — still sealed inside their boxes, unopened, unseen. They are Japan's crown jewels, except there is no crown, and the jewels themselves are a mystery.

These are the Sanshu no Jingi (三種の神器), the Three Sacred Treasures: a mirror, a sword, and a jewel that together form the Imperial Regalia of Japan. As a licensed guide, I find few stories capture the strange and beautiful heart of Japanese spirituality better than this one — a tale where mythology, the world's oldest monarchy, and shrines you can actually visit all converge. Let me tell you about each treasure, what it means, and where the legends say they rest today.

A Gift From the Sun Goddess天照大神からの授け物

To understand why these objects matter so much, you have to start with a story Japanese children still learn today.

In Shinto mythology, the imperial family is descended from Amaterasu, the sun goddess and the most revered deity in the entire pantheon. According to legend, she handed these three treasures to her grandson Ninigi when he descended from the heavens to rule the earth, and they were eventually passed to Emperor Jimmu, traditionally counted as Japan's first emperor. For centuries, possessing the regalia was the symbol of an emperor's divine right to rule — proof of descent from the sun goddess herself.

Together, the three treasures represent three virtues a worthy ruler should embody: valor (the sword), wisdom (the mirror), and benevolence (the jewel). Let's meet them one by one.

The Treasures & Their Meanings三種の神器の正体

TREASURE 01 ・ MIRROR
Yata no Kagami (八咫鏡)
Virtue: Wisdom

The mirror is arguably the most sacred of the three. According to myth, it was used to lure Amaterasu out of the Heavenly Rock Cave (Ama-no-Iwato) when she plunged the world into darkness by hiding away. When she looked into the mirror and saw her own radiant reflection, curiosity got the better of her, and light was restored to the world.

Today, the mirror is believed to be enshrined at the inner sanctuary of Ise Grand Shrine (Mie Prefecture), the most sacred Shinto site in Japan.

Enshrined at: Ise Grand Shrine (Mie Prefecture)
TREASURE 02 ・ SWORD
Kusanagi no Tsurugi (草薙剣)
Virtue: Valor

Also known as Ame-no-Murakumo-no-Tsurugi (Sword of the Gathering Clouds of Heaven), this legendary blade was supposedly found inside the tail of the Yamata no Orochi, an eight-headed dragon slain by the storm god Susanoo. He presented it to Amaterasu as an apology.

The name Kusanagi ("Grass-Cutter") comes from a tale where legendary prince Yamato Takeru used it to cut away burning grass and save his life. It is traditionally housed at Atsuta Shrine in Nagoya.

Enshrined at: Atsuta Shrine (Nagoya, Aichi)
TREASURE 03 ・ JEWEL
Yasakani no Magatama (八尺瓊勾玉)
Virtue: Benevolence

Unlike the sword and mirror, which are housed at major shrines, the magatama—a comma-shaped bead characteristic of ancient Japanese craftsmanship—is kept directly at the Imperial Palace in Tokyo.

It was also used during the cave ritual to entice Amaterasu out. Made of high-quality jade, it represents benevolence, gentleness, and the interconnectedness of life.

Kept at: The Imperial Palace (Tokyo)

Why Has No One Seen Them?なぜ誰も見ることができないのか

Secrecy is absolute. Even the Emperor himself does not casually open their boxes. When Enthronement ceremonies (Sokui-no-Rei) take place, the sword and jewel are brought into the hall resting inside heavy wooden boxes covered with brocade cloth. To the naked eye, observers see only wooden containers.

This secrecy is not born of mere paranoia; it is deeply rooted in Shinto theology. Sacred objects that house or symbolize divine presence (shintai) are often considered too spiritually potent for ordinary human eyes. Direct exposure could bring misfortune or shatter the sacred mystery that protects their power.

"In Shinto, power often lies in the unseen. The invisible presence is felt more keenly precisely because it is hidden away from the mundane world."

Visiting the Shrines Behind the Legend伝説の舞台を訪ねる

While you cannot see the treasures, you can absolutely visit the magnificent shrines that guard their legacy. Here are the key locations every traveler interested in Japanese mythology should know:

01
Ise Grand Shrine (Jingu)
Mie Prefecture

Home of the sacred mirror. Set amid ancient cedar forests, it is the spiritual heart of Japan. Though you only see thatched wooden fences, the serene atmosphere is unforgettable.

02
Atsuta Shrine
Nagoya, Aichi

Guardian of the Kusanagi sword. Surrounded by peaceful woods in the middle of bustling Nagoya, it houses thousands of donated art pieces and ancient swords.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくある質問

What are the Three Sacred Treasures of Japan?
They are the Imperial Regalia of Japan: the mirror Yata no Kagami (wisdom), the sword Kusanagi no Tsurugi (valor), and the jewel Yasakani no Magatama (benevolence). Together they symbolize the emperor's legitimacy and divine descent from the sun goddess Amaterasu.
Where are the Three Sacred Treasures kept?
By tradition, the mirror is enshrined at Ise Grand Shrine in Mie, the sword at Atsuta Shrine in Nagoya, and the jewel at the Imperial Palace in Tokyo. None can be viewed by the public.
Can anyone see the treasures?
No. They are kept in deep secrecy and are never displayed publicly. Even during imperial ceremonies, the sword and jewel appear only inside sealed boxes. Only a few senior priests and imperial household members are permitted near them.
Are the treasures real, and how old are they?
This is genuinely uncertain. Because they are never examined publicly, their age and authenticity are matters of faith and tradition rather than verified fact. Historical accounts even suggest some may have been lost and replaced over the centuries.
What do the three treasures symbolize?
The sword represents valor, the mirror represents wisdom, and the jewel represents benevolence — the three virtues a Japanese emperor is meant to embody.
Can I visit the shrines connected to the treasures?
Yes. Ise Grand Shrine and Atsuta Shrine both welcome visitors, though you won't see the treasures themselves. The Imperial Palace grounds in Tokyo are limited, but its East Gardens are open to the public.

A Mystery Worth Traveling For旅する価値のある神秘

The Three Sacred Treasures remind us that not everything in travel needs to be photographed, touched, or fully explained. Part of Japan's magic lies in its reverence for the unseen—in sacred spaces where imagination, ancient history, and deep-rooted faith meet.

Whether you walk the gravel paths of Ise Grand Shrine or stand quietly beneath the camphor trees at Atsuta Shrine, knowing these legendary objects rest nearby adds an extraordinary layer of wonder to your journey.
