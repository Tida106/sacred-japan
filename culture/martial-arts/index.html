Jujutsu, Judo, Kendo, Karate and Where to Try Them

Judo, the gentle way, grew out of a fighting style meant for men in armor on a battlefield, who had lost their weapons and had only their hands left.

As a licensed guide, I find that this one fact changes how people look at martial arts in Japan. Jujutsu was an answer to a very practical problem, and judo, kendo and the rest of the modern arts are what later generations made of that old toughness. This guide follows that line from the battlefield to the modern dojo, and then points to places where you can watch or try a class. Where a fact comes from a single source I say so.

Budo, Bujutsu and Koryu: Three Words武道・武術・古流

Wikipedia (Japanese martial arts) lists three terms that are used interchangeably in English for Japanese martial arts: 武道 budo (the martial way), 武術 bujutsu (martial technique or the craft of war) and 武芸 bugei (martial art). The same entry explains 古流 koryu, "old school," as a school that began before the Meiji Restoration.

The key shift is from jutsu to do. Japanese Taste describes arts that existed before the Restoration as koryu and those that appeared afterward as gendai budo, "modern martial arts," with the ending changed from technique (jutsu) to way (do) to stress self-development. That is why you will see jujutsu next to judo, kenjutsu next to kendo and kyujutsu next to kyudo.

Jujutsu: Grappling in Armor柔術

Wikipedia (Jujutsu) separates two broad kinds of school. Sengoku-period (1467 to 1603) systems were katchu bujutsu or yoroi kumiuchi, fighting or grappling while wearing armor. Edo-period (1603 to 1867) systems were suhada bujutsu, fighting in ordinary clothes. It adds that on the battlefield, striking an armored opponent did not work well, so the techniques favored holds, throws and joint locks, often with a short weapon.

Kids Web Japan tells the same story from the samurai side: besides swords, bows and arrows, they developed jujutsu for close fighting on the battlefield, called kumi-uchi, and by the start of the Edo period several styles had appeared and hand-to-hand combat had become important military training. It also says the roots of jujutsu lie in sumo, which appears in the Kojiki (712) and the Nihon Shoki (720). Wikipedia (Jujutsu) dates the Takenouchi school to the 1530s, among the early schools it names.

One detail I like: the word 柔道 already appears as an alternative name for jujutsu in the early 1700s. The Wikipedia-based entry on encyclopedia.pub dates it to as early as 1724, almost two centuries before Kano. I rely on that single entry for the date.

Judo: Kano Jigoro and the Kodokan柔道

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 ended samurai rule and Western culture spread. Kids Web Japan says jujutsu declined and nearly disappeared, until a young man named Kano Jigoro began training at 17 to build his strength. In May 1882, at 21, he combined the best elements of several jujutsu styles into one new school, with nine students in a 12-mat dojo at a temple. Wikipedia (Kodokan) identifies the temple as Eishoji in Ueno.

a history of judo reposted from Judoinfo says Kano was born on October 28, 1860 in Mikage near Kobe, studied Tenjin Shinyo-ryu and then Kito-ryu, and called his new method judo. I rely on that page for those details, which I have not checked elsewhere.

The result spread fast. According to Kids Web Japan, Kano visited Europe in 1889, later served on the International Olympic Committee, and judo became an official Olympic event for men in Tokyo in 1964, with women's judo official in 1992. The Olympic format uses weight classes. As japan-guide.com describes it, the art covers throwing, grappling and, in kata, striking, and learning to fall is an important part of training.

The photo above shows a throw demonstrated at the Kodokan, as described on Commons; it was taken during an official visit in 2000, not at a regular practice. Even so, it shows the heart of judo clearly: one player's balance is broken and the other uses it, rather than force, to bring the partner to the mat.

Judo is also a worldwide sport, and this photo shows it far from Japan. Commons describes it as a seoi-nage (a shoulder throw) that won the match by ippon, the full point, at the Mose Woodson Classic tournament in Denton, Texas, in 2009. The thrower, wearing the white marker, has the partner over the back, and the red competition mat is clear in the frame. The Commons file notes that its colors were adjusted after upload. Compare it with the Kodokan photo above: the same idea of using the partner's balance, in a very different hall.

Wikipedia (Jujutsu) also lists Brazilian jiu-jitsu and sambo among arts descended from jujutsu through judo, which is a useful thing to know if you meet a traveler on the same trip who trains in either.

Kendo, Kyudo, Karate and Aikido剣道・弓道・空手・合気道

  • Kendo (剣道): "the way of the sword," it evolved from kenjutsu (Japanese Taste). Web Japan's martial arts fact sheet says the All Japan Kendo Federation was established in 1952, after which kendo returned as a sport and school subject. The "sword" is a shinai of bamboo, and points are scored by striking the head, trunk or wrist, or thrusting at the throat; two out of three points win a match.
  • Kyudo (弓道): Japanese archery, with a strong mental and spiritual side. Japanese Taste says it is rooted in Zen Buddhism and Shinto and calls it "moving meditation," using an asymmetrical bamboo bow gripped about a third of the way up. The Web Japan fact sheet gives 1949 as the year the All Nippon Kyudo Federation began, which opened a new period for it as a sport.
  • Karate (空手): originated in Okinawa, influenced by early Chinese fighting arts, with Shotokan as the most popular of four major styles. It became an Olympic sport at the Tokyo 2020 Games (Japanese Taste). To see the Okinawan side of the story, our Shuri Castle guide covers the Ryukyu Kingdom's palace.
  • Aikido (合気道): a self-defense art that avoids harming the attacker, using joint holds and throws that redirect the attacker's energy. Japan-zone says its founder had developed his own techniques, which he called aiki bujutsu, by 1922, and later renamed them aikido; its source is a single page, so please treat the date as approximate.
  • Iaido (居合道): drawing the sword from the scabbard and cutting in one motion at an imagined opponent, practiced largely solo, sometimes from a seated position (Japanese Taste).

This is kendo practice at the Noma Dojo in Tokyo, as described on Commons. The players wear the full armor, a mask, a padded jacket and gloves over a dark hakama, and hold bamboo swords. Spare armor hangs in a row along the wall, and the long wooden floor is bright with skylights and tall windows. It is a useful picture of what a visitor will see: a large hall, a lot of footwork, and no one standing still.

Kyudo looks very different. The photo shows a team competition at the Second Kyudo World Cup, held in Paris in July 2014 according to Commons, and the poster on the left wall appears to say the same. The archers wear white tops and black hakama, and the bows are very long and held asymmetrically, with the grip well below the middle. Several are at full draw at once, while a seated official in a grey jacket watches from the front. It is also a reminder that kyudo is practiced far beyond Japan.

Karate is shown here at a competition outside Japan: the Commons file name calls it a fighting championship, and the photo was entered in a Wikimedia Indonesia photo contest, so I take it to be in Indonesia, though the page does not say where. One competitor in a white uniform lands a high kick while the other blocks with a raised knee, a referee in a dark shirt stands at the right, and a full crowd watches from the blue seats. It makes the point from the list above: karate began in Okinawa, and today it is practiced in gyms all over the world.

This shihonage throw was photographed in 2008 at an aikido dojo in Esztergom, Hungary, according to Commons, which describes it as a technique performed from a half-seated position, with the person being thrown taking a forward fall to land safely. The scroll behind them reads 合氣道 in the older character form, as I read it, with a teacher's name below. On the shelf beside it stands a small suit of armor on a stand. It shows the aikido idea well: no punch, just a joint hold that turns the partner's own movement into a fall.

Iaido is the sword drawn and cut in one motion, usually against an imagined opponent, as described above. This photo is of a cutting demonstration at a dojo in La Paz, Bolivia, according to Commons. The swordsman has just cut a bundle of green stalks, which look like sugar cane to me, and the cut piece is still in the air. The black banner at the right reads 翔鷹会, as I read it, and wooden practice swords hang on a rack at the left. It is a small reminder of how far the Japanese sword arts have traveled.

One more art deserves a mention, because it is both a martial art and a shrine ritual: yabusame, archery from a galloping horse. Commons describes this photo as a yabusame offering at Meiji Jingu, taken in November 2011. The rider, in a light blue robe, draws the bow at full speed while the horse's hooves throw up dirt, and the targets are a white board marked with a diamond crest and a black board with a white round disc. Behind the rope, officials in brown robes kneel and watch. If you want to see it in person, check the shrine's own schedule, since I have not confirmed current dates.

How Budo Was Rebuilt After 1868武道の再建

The arts did not simply survive the end of the samurai class. Web Japan's martial arts fact sheet says that after the Sino-Japanese War, in 1895, a national organization called the Dai Nippon Butokukai (Great Japan Martial Virtue Association) centralized martial arts and oversaw their introduction into education. The same fact sheet says the occupation authorities banned the arts for five years after the Second World War, until 1950, because the prewar revival was seen as feeding militarism; the arts were then promoted as sports. Ryukyu Bugei adds that the Butokukai was dissolved in 1946 by an Occupation directive, and that the All Japan Kendo Federation, established in 1952, became its de facto successor.

Visiting the Kodokan, Tokyo講道館

The Kodokan Judo Institute is the headquarters of the world judo community, founded in 1882 by Kano and now an eight-story building in Bunkyo ward (Wikipedia (Kodokan)). The address is 1-16-30 Kasuga, a one-minute walk from exits A1 and A2 of Kasuga Station on the Mita and Oedo lines, according to Trip to Japan's judo article.

  • Watching practice: japan-guide.com says spectating is free from the 8th floor, on weekdays and Saturdays, 16:30 to 19:30, with no reservation.
  • Museum: a small judo exhibit on the 2nd floor, free. japan-guide.com lists weekday hours of 11:00 to 18:00; the Kodokan website says it is closed on Saturdays, Sundays and national holidays, and also during summer and year-end breaks.
  • Training: Practice at the Kodokan says randori classes accept trainees with a certain amount of judo experience, and beginners can study from the basics in the judo school.
  • Manners: AFP (via Taipei Times) reports that foreign visitors who train there bow to the statue of Kano before stepping on the tatami, and are not excused from roll call or the group warm-up.

This is the building you are looking for: a pale block with a grid of windows, easy to pick out among the street trees. The lettering on the windows reads, as I read it, 講道館ビル along with the names of clinics on other floors. Spectators head for the 8th floor, as japan-guide.com describes.

The bronze figure in hakama and a long jacket, standing on a stone base, is catalogued on Commons as Kano Jigoro. The wall behind it carries a signboard that reads 講道館, right to left. I cannot say from the photo whether this is the statue that visitors bow to in the AFP (via Taipei Times) report, since I have not confirmed which statue that article means.

Hours and class details change, so please check the Kodokan's website for the current schedule before you plan your day around it.

The Butokuden in Kyoto武徳殿

Beside Heian Jingu (see our Kyoto shrines guide) stands the Butokuden, the main hall of Kyoto's Budo Center. Kenshi 24/7 says it was completed in 1899 on the north-west side of the Heian Jingu grounds as a demonstration hall for the Butokukai, and that every May 2 to 5 it hosts the Kyoto Taikai (the All Japan Kendo Enbu Taikai); Budo Japan says the event was first held in 1895. Kanpai Japan says kendo is the main discipline there, with an archery range for kyudo and a sumo hall, and calls it a must-see for martial arts enthusiasts.

Kanpai Japan gives 1895 as the year the hall was built, which does not match the 1899 completion given by Kenshi 24/7, so I use 1899 for the building and 1895 for the first taikai. Whether you can watch practice on a given day depends on the schedule, so I would treat the grounds as a pleasant stop beside Heian Jingu and check ahead if you want to see a session.

This is the old Butokuden in Sakyo ward, Kyoto, as Commons describes it, uploaded in June 2010. The long hall has white plaster walls between dark timbers, a lattice of wooden screens along the side, and layers of gray tiled roofs with a decorated gable at the center. The pine at the left and the maple at the right frame it well. A small sign for bicycle parking reminds you that this is still a working place, so please respect any events or practice in progress.

Where to Try a Class体験できる場所

Koryu schools (the old, pre-Meiji lineages) are small and traditional, but several teach visitors in English. These are listings I found; I have not tried them, so please check each page for the current program, price and reviews.

  • Tokyo, Kojimachi: Tsurugi Goshin Budo. Trip to Japan lists a 2-hour hands-on koryu swordsmanship session in English or Japanese at the Tokyo headquarters (B2F, MK Kojimachi Building), and a separate 2-hour self-defense session based on aiki-jujutsu. Live Japan shows a Viator version of the sword experience at 12,000 yen, and a related listing says the training is based on Daito-ryu Aiki Jujutsu.
  • Tokyo, Shinjuku: Koshu-ryu Jujutsu. A Live Japan listing says you receive direct instruction in Koshu-ryu jujutsu from Master Haibara Yukiji.
  • Tokyo: swordsmanship and horseback archery. A Trip.com listing says the Toyama-ryu Bizen Association teaches both swordsmanship and traditional horseback archery (yabusame) in one class.
  • Kyoto: Asayama Ichiden-ryu. The Abroad Guide describes a roughly three-hour evening class (starting 6:30 pm) at the Kyoto branch of this lineage, practicing iaijutsu and kenjutsu with wooden swords in a traditional dojo, for up to four travelers, at a reported price of about $33. I have only this one source for the price.
  • Tokyo, Kuramae: Mugai-ryu iaido. japan-guide.com mentions a 1-hour Klook "samurai experience" at 22,200 yen.

To compare more, see all martial arts experiences on Viator, or search koryu and samurai sword classes in Tokyo. For the people behind these arts, our samurai guide covers armor and swords, and the ninja guide covers Iga and Koka.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is jujutsu the same as judo?
No. Jujutsu is the older family of grappling arts that samurai schools taught, and judo is the school Kano Jigoro founded in 1882 by combining the best elements of several of them (Kids Web Japan). Judo sparring leaves out strikes to lower the risk of injury, while strikes survive mainly in prearranged kata (japan-guide.com).
Why did jujutsu begin on the battlefield?
Wikipedia (Jujutsu) describes the Sengoku-period (1467 to 1603) systems as ways for a lightly armed or unarmed warrior to deal with a heavily armed and armored enemy, since striking an armored opponent was ineffective. After the Edo period began, schools for fighting in ordinary clothes became common.
What is the difference between jutsu and do?
Japanese Taste describes koryu as traditional arts that existed before the Meiji Restoration and gendai budo as modern arts that appeared afterward, with "jutsu" (technique) replaced by "do" (way) to stress self-development. Kano's choice of the name judo rather than jujutsu is one of the best-known examples (Wikipedia (Bujutsu)).
Can a tourist watch judo practice at the Kodokan?
Yes. japan-guide.com says spectating is free from the 8th-floor seating area, on weekdays and Saturdays from 16:30 to 19:30, with no reservation needed. Schedules can change, so check the Kodokan's own site before you go.
Can a complete beginner try a martial art in Japan?
Yes, there are English-language experiences for beginners, mostly sword classes (koryu swordsmanship, iaido) and aiki-jujutsu sessions in Tokyo and Kyoto, plus the Kodokan's beginner judo classes. Listings and prices change, so use the section below as a starting point and confirm on each page.
Is karate Japanese?
It is practiced and organized throughout Japan, but its roots are in Okinawa, with influence from early Chinese fighting arts according to Japanese Taste. It joined the Olympic program at the Tokyo 2020 Games, in the city that had hosted judo's Olympic debut in 1964 (Kids Web Japan).

Final Thoughtsむすびに

If you have one evening in Tokyo, sit on the 8th floor of the Kodokan and watch the throws; if you have a free afternoon in Kyoto, walk past the Butokuden beside Heian Jingu. If you want something in your own hands, a koryu sword class is the closest thing to the way the samurai learned. Once you know that jujutsu began as a way to fight in armor, every judo mat and every kendo bamboo sword has a longer story behind it.
