Japanese Mythology Guide

日本神話ガイド

Kojiki Gods, Famous Myths and the Shrines Where They Happen

A man turns around in the dark of the underworld, lifts a torch, and sees what he promised not to look at. Everything that follows in Japanese mythology, the sun, the moon, the storm, the imperial line, grows out of that one broken promise. These stories are not dusty. You can stand on the beach where the gods are said to arrive this very month, and you can walk down a river to the cave where they once held a meeting.

As a licensed guide, I find that most visitors meet these gods first as names on shrine signs, and the stories are the key that makes those signs readable. This guide gives you the main plot in the order the old books tell it, flags where the sources disagree, and ends with a map of the real places where each story is set. Our guides to the Three Sacred Treasures and the Izumo Taisha go deeper on two of the threads below.

Two Books, 712 and 720古事記と日本書紀

Almost everything below comes from two works. The Collector says most of what we know about Shinto belief traces back to two documents commissioned in 712 and 720: the Kojiki (Record of Ancient Matters) and the Nihon Shoki (Chronicle of Japan). World History Encyclopedia calls the Kojiki the oldest book of Japanese history and the oldest text of any kind from Japan, written in Chinese characters with some Japanese adaptations, and with poems and songs inserted into the prose.

The Nihon Shoki came later, written by a committee of court scholars in 720. The same source says it repeats many of the Kojiki's myths but often from a different viewpoint and with changed details. Fun! JAPAN adds that it is the oldest official history of the nation, running from mythical beginnings to the year 697. That is why you will see two versions of some stories, and why I note below where they split.

One more point of context: New World Encyclopedia notes that the myths gave Japan a creation story and attributed divine origins to the Imperial family. Keep that in mind when you read them; they are religion, literature and a family tree at the same time.

A Spear, a Drop and the First Island国生み

Before Japan there was, in The Collector's words, undifferentiated chaos, from which a line of gods appeared. The last pair were Izanagi and Izanami, whose names Fun! JAPAN translates as "he who invites" and "she who invites". The other gods handed them a spear and told them to consolidate the world, according to a Kojiki summary on James Bishop's blog. They stood on the Floating Bridge of Heaven and stirred.

The result, in the Kojiki passage quoted by World History Encyclopedia, is that the spear's drips piled up and became the island of Onogoro. The couple then produced the islands of the archipelago; Fun! JAPAN counts eight children. Izanagi greeted Izanami first, in the Collector's retelling, and that is why the next children were the other islands, a detail that says a lot about what the compilers thought of correct manners.

Where was Onogoro? The myth does not say, but tradition points to Awaji Island in Hyogo, and several shrines there claim the story, as the map below shows.

Yomi and the Three Children黄泉の国と三貴子

Giving birth to the fire god killed Izanami. Fun! JAPAN says the grieving Izanagi killed his own child in revenge and then went to find her in Yomi, the underworld. The Collector says she asked him to wait and under no circumstances to look at her. Like Orpheus, he looked. Bishop's summary of the Kojiki says she had already eaten food from the furnace of Yomi, and that after her army was driven back she pursued him herself, until Izanagi blocked the Even Pass of Yomi with a boulder the book calls the Thousand-Draught Rock.

Defiled by death, he washed. The Collector says the gods who came from the washing include the three most important in the pantheon: the sun goddess Amaterasu from his left eye, the moon god Tsukuyomi from his right, and the storm god Susanoo from his nose. Fun! JAPAN tells it with a tear from the left eye; the sources vary, and the washing is the version I will use. The Collector adds that Amaterasu was given the heavens, Tsukuyomi the night and Susanoo the seas.

If you ever wondered why Shinto cares so much about purification, this is the origin story. The rite you perform at a water basin is a small copy of a god cleaning himself after the underworld. Our shrine etiquette guide shows how to do it.

The Sun Goddess, the Storm God and the Cave天岩戸とヤマタノオロチ

The Collector says the two siblings Amaterasu and Susanoo had a rivalry, and that the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki differ in their versions, but the gist is a dispute over which gods spawned from her sword and his necklace would serve whom. The sun goddess withdrew into a cave and the world went dark.

The ministry's multilingual guide (MLIT) tells the rescue clearly. The gods gathered, made a mirror from heavenly metal, and one of them, Ame no Uzume, began a dance that so pleased the assembly that they filled the air with laughter. Amaterasu, puzzled, opened the stone door a crack to ask why. Japan Experience adds that the strongman Tajikarao then pulled the rock aside. The dance is said to be the first kagura, the sacred dance still performed at shrines, which is why the story is told every night in Takachiho (more below).

Susanoo, banished, goes down to Izumo and kills the eight-headed serpent. In the Kojiki as quoted by World History Encyclopedia, his sword broke on the middle tail, and when he cut into the flesh he found "a sharp great sword". That sword becomes part of the regalia; the full story is in our Three Sacred Treasures guide, and Tsunagu Japan says it is kept at Atsuta Shrine.

Okuninushi, the White Hare and the Land Transfer大国主と因幡の白兎

The next hero is a kind one. In the Kojiki, Okuninushi (called Onamuji then) trails behind his eighty brothers on the way to court Princess Yagami of Inaba, as Wikipedia puts it. Fun! JAPAN calls him a kind-hearted descendant of Susanoo; the sources differ on how many generations. On the beach they find a flayed hare.

Wikipedia says the hare had tricked sharks (in the San'in region, wani means the common shark) into lining up as a bridge from the island of Oki to Cape Keta, and the last shark tore off its fur. The brothers' advice makes it worse, and Onamuji's kindness cures it. Cape Keta is now identified with the Hakuto Coast in Tottori. Fun! JAPAN reads the tale as a sign of Okuninushi's medical skill, and says Hakuto Shrine is believed to help with skin diseases and burns. Japan Experience describes it as a favorite for matchmaking, noting the claim that this is the first love story in Japan's ancient myths. The hare prophesies that Yagami will choose him, and she does.

The jealous brothers then try to kill him more than once; in the Fun! JAPAN version one trap is a split tree, and he is sent to Susanoo for trials. The story ends in what the MLIT database calls kuni-yuzuri, "relinquishing the land": Okuninushi hands the country to Amaterasu's line. For the shrine itself, its grounds and photos, see our Izumo Taisha guide. Tsunagu Japan says Izumo Taisha was built as a condition of that handover. It is the founding bargain of Japanese mythology: the visible world goes to the sun goddess's family, and the hidden world, in later tradition, stays with Okuninushi.

That hidden world is the matchmaking job. The next section shows what happens in Izumo when the gods report to him.

The Descent to Takachiho天孫降臨

Amaterasu sends her grandson Ninigi down to rule (Tsunagu Japan says grandchild). Japanese Taste says he descended on Mount Takachiho in Kyushu with the three sacred objects, a mirror, a jewel and a sword, and that Kirishima Shrine and Kushifuru Shrine nearby celebrate the event. Tsunagu Japan adds that Takachiho Shrine is said to be the spot. Our Takachiho guide covers the gorge and the evening kagura. For the shrine that worships Ninigi’s landing mountain, see our Kirishima Jingu guide.

Two romances follow, both with real shrines. Tsunagu Japan says Princess Konohanasakuya, daughter of the mountain god, married Ninigi, and that Fujisan Hongu Sengen Taisha in Shizuoka enshrines her. It also says Udo Shrine in Miyazaki is where Princess Toyotama gave birth in a sea cave, a story it ties to the Hoori and Hoderi tale and to the folktale of Urashima Taro. I have not checked those links myself, so treat them as the source's reading.

Kamiarizuki: The Month the Gods Meet神在月

Here the myths become a live event. In the lunar tenth month, most of Japan calls the month kannazuki, the month without gods, and Izumo calls it kamiarizuki, the month with gods. The MLIT database says the myriad deities gather in Izumo to determine the relationships people will form in the coming year. JNTO says the welcome rite, kami-mukae-sai, is held after sunset at Inasa Beach on the 10th day of the 10th lunar month, and the Kamiari Festival runs from the 11th to the 17th. The MLIT database adds that the gods are guided ashore by a dragon-serpent called Seguroumihebi.

2026 dates: Watabi reports, from Izumo Taisha's official calendar, that the gods are welcomed at Inasa Beach on the night of November 18, 2026, and that Sata Shrine holds its own rite from November 20 to 25. By my arithmetic, since the lunar tenth month begins November 9, the Kamiari Festival days of the 11th to 17th fall on November 19 to 25. The date moves every year with the lunar calendar, so please confirm on the Izumo Taisha website before you travel.

I would not treat this as a spectacle. Watabi's own guide describes the month in Izumo as one where a district deliberately goes quiet, and some of the rites are held out of public view. Go to listen, not to photograph.

A Shrine Map of the Myths神話ゆかりの神社

Tsunagu Japan lists twelve shrines tied to the myths. Here is that list reorganized by story, with extra detail from other sources where I found it. Where a shrine's link to a story is the shrine's own claim, I say so.

Creation (Awaji Island, Hyogo)

  • Onokoro Shrine: honors Izanagi and Izanami and the first island they made, per Tsunagu Japan. Yuugen Guide describes Onokorojima Jinja as in Minamiawaji, in the middle of the island.
  • Izanagi Jingu: in Taga on Awaji Island. Jinja Journey says Izanagi chose this first island for his last years and built a hidden palace here. KIJI describes it as the only grand shrine (jingu) in Hyogo. Yuugen Guide could find no firm record of when it was built.

A Closer Look at Izanagi Jingu

Because the shrine on Awaji is the one place on this map that claims to be Izanagi's own, I want to walk you through it in the order you would meet it. The Commons descriptions of the photos below name each building; the comments on them are mine. Jinja Journey says Izanagi chose Awaji for his last years and built a hidden palace here, and that is the story the shrine tells about itself.

Start at the big torii. The next two photos show the front gate, with its rope and white streamers.

Past the gate, a straight path leads to the worship hall. This is the place to bow, and behind it stands the main sanctuary.

Last, the office where amulets are given out. The board in this photo lists charms for matchmaking, safe birth, exams and travel, among others.

The underworld and the washing

  • Iya Shrine (Shimane): said to mark Yomi Tsuhirasaka, the boundary between the living and the dead (Tsunagu Japan).
  • Eda Shrine (Miyazaki): said to be where Izanagi purified himself after returning, according to Tsunagu Japan; JAL lists it as the place where Izanagi and Izanami are enshrined.

The three children

  • Ise Grand Shrine (Mie): Amaterasu. See our Ise guide.
  • Tsukiyomi Shrine (Kyoto): the moon god, also a place to pray for safe childbirth (Tsunagu Japan).
  • Yaegaki Shrine (Shimane): Susanoo, with a mirror pond used for marriage fortune-telling (Tsunagu Japan).

The cave and the serpent

  • Amano Iwato Shrine (Takachiho, Miyazaki): the cave where Amaterasu hid. See the trip section below.
  • Atsuta Shrine (Aichi): the sword found in the serpent, per Tsunagu Japan. Our Atsuta guide has the visit.

Okuninushi and the descent

  • Hakuto Shrine (Tottori): the white hare. Japan Experience says it is 40 minutes by bus from Tottori Station, and that small rabbit statues stand around it and nearby.
  • Izumo Taisha (Shimane): Okuninushi and the annual gods' meeting. See our Izumo Taisha guide.
  • Takachiho Shrine (Miyazaki), Fujisan Hongu Sengen Taisha (Shizuoka), Udo Shrine (Miyazaki): Ninigi, Konohanasakuya and Toyotama, as above.

Planning a Myth Trip旅のモデルコース

My suggestion for a first trip is Takachiho, because the cave myth is spread over a few kilometers. Japan-guide says Amano Iwato Shrine is about ten kilometers outside central Takachiho, with a bus from Takachiho Bus Center (15 minutes, 200 yen one way, one bus every one to two hours) and a taxi at about 2,700 yen one way. Amano Yasukawara, the cave where the gods met, is a ten-minute walk along the river, and japan-guide says the stone piles become thousands as you approach it. Japan Experience says admission is free and that if you knock a pile over, tradition says you build two to make up for it.

JAL's guide says you cannot actually go to the cave where Amaterasu hid; the shrine's west sanctuary gives a view toward it. On timing, one source (Japan Activity) says Takachiho Shrine in town stages a 50-minute evening kagura at 20:00 with four dances, and Tokyo Weekender mentions a 33-dance night kagura; schedules and seasons differ, so check the current program.

The second trip I would suggest is Shimane: Izumo Taisha, then Iya and Yaegaki, which Tsunagu Japan lists as nearby shrines in the same prefecture. Tottori's Hakuto Shrine can be added from Tottori City. Awaji, for the creation story, is a day trip I would leave for a Kansai stay.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is the difference between the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki?
Both are early eighth-century records, and they tell many of the same myths. The Collector says they were commissioned in 712 and 720. World History Encyclopedia calls the Kojiki the oldest book of Japanese history, and says the Nihon Shoki, written by a committee of court scholars in 720, repeats many of its myths but often from a different viewpoint and with changes in detail. Fun! JAPAN adds that the Nihon Shoki is the oldest official history of the nation and runs up to the year 697.
Where do Japan's islands come from in the myth?
From a spear. In the Kojiki as quoted by World History Encyclopedia, brine dripped from the spear of Izanagi and Izanami and piled up to become the island of Onogoro. The couple then produced the other islands, which Fun! JAPAN counts as eight children. Which real island Onogoro was is not settled; Awaji Island in Hyogo is the traditional candidate, and its shrines are described below.
Is the Emperor descended from Amaterasu?
In the myths, yes: Fun! JAPAN calls the sun goddess the ancestor of the Imperial Family, and Japanese Taste traces the line through her descendant Ninigi, who came down to Takachiho. New World Encyclopedia notes that the myths gave the Imperial family divine origins. I present this as what the myths say, not as history; the Ise shrine is where the Imperial link is still honored in ritual.
Which shrine should I visit for Amaterasu?
Ise. Japanese Taste says her divine status is honored at the Ise Grand Shrine, which it calls the most important Imperial Shinto shrine in Japan. For the cave story itself, go to Amano Iwato Shrine in Takachiho.
Why is Izumo's tenth month the month with gods?
In the lunar tenth month, most of Japan calls the month kannazuki, the month without gods, because the gods are believed to have left for Izumo. In Izumo it is kamiarizuki, the month with gods. The Ministry of Land (MLIT) multilingual database says the myriad deities gather there to decide the relationships people will form in the coming year.
Can I actually visit Amaterasu's cave?
Not the cave itself. JAL's travel guide says you cannot go to Amano Iwato, the cave where she hid, which is across the gorge behind the shrine's west sanctuary. What you can visit is Amano Yasukawara, the riverside cave where the gods met to plan, about a ten-minute walk from the shrine according to japan-guide.
If I only have two days, where do I start?
My suggestion is Takachiho, because several stories sit close together: Amano Iwato and Amano Yasukawara for the cave myth, Takachiho Shrine for Ninigi's descent and the evening kagura, and the gorge nearby. Our Takachiho guide has the logistics. Izumo is the second choice if you want Okuninushi and the gods' meeting.

Final Thoughtsむすびに

If you remember one thing, remember the broken promise at the gate of Yomi. Light, water, purification and the three great gods all come out of it, and once you know it, the water basin, the shimenawa and the festival calendar start to read as one story. Start with the cave in Takachiho, then follow the plot to Izumo.
