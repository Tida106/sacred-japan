The Yakitori Guide

焼き鳥ガイド

Cuts, Salt vs Sauce and How to Order

When a visitor asks me for an ordinary night out in Japan, one that is not a tasting menu and not a tourist show, I point them to a counter where someone is grilling skewers over charcoal.

As a licensed guide, I find the menu is the hard part: a list of cut names, a choice between salt and sauce, and a few unwritten habits. This guide covers the cuts, how to choose, and how to order, and it says where the sources disagree.

What Yakitori Is焼き鳥とは

Yakitori (焼き鳥) is small pieces of chicken, and often its organs, threaded on skewers and grilled over a charcoal fire, then seasoned with salt or brushed with sauce. The skewers are served a few at a time, which makes it easy food to share over drinks.

On history, FUN! JAPAN (history of yakitori) says duck, quail and pheasant were eaten in Japan long before yakitori, that it first appears as a dish in a cookbook of 1682 in the Edo period, and that yakitori restaurants began to open in the Meiji period. It adds that early yakitori was mainly chicken organs and that broiler chickens, in 1954, made it cheap enough to spread. The article cites no sources, so I would treat the dates as approximate.

The plate at the top shows what a mixed order looks like. I count nine skewers on a pale blue plate: chicken with a wedge of onion, darker meats, a deep-red piece and three glazed meatballs. Commons describes it as "A set of 6 for JPY 979" with single skewers at 176 and 198 yen, so the description probably covers only part of what is on the plate. I will not name each skewer, because I cannot tell them apart with confidence from the photo.

The Cuts to Know部位

Names vary by restaurant, but these are the ones TableCheck and FUN! JAPAN (how to enjoy yakitori) describe most often:

  • Momo (もも): thigh, the default and juicy.
  • Mune (むね): breast, lean and mild.
  • Negima (ねぎま): chicken pieces alternating with green onion.
  • Tsukune (つくね): minced chicken formed into a patty or balls.
  • Sasami (ささみ): lean tenderloin, sometimes served with plum sauce, wasabi or shiso.
  • Seseri (せせり): neck meat, fatty and juicy.
  • Kawa (皮): skin.
  • Nankotsu (なんこつ): cartilage, chewy and crunchy.
  • Reba (レバー), hatsu (ハツ), sunagimo (砂肝): liver, heart and gizzard. TableCheck calls the liver rich and pâté-like, the heart meaty and softer, and the gizzard crisp.
  • Bonjiri (ぼんじり): tail meat, rare and fatty.
  • Tebasaki (手羽先): wings, which TableCheck calls a specialty of Nagoya.

This is negima. Commons gives it the Japanese name momo kizoku-yaki (もも貴族焼), which points to thigh meat, though the sources differ on whether negima uses thigh or breast, so the cut can vary. In the photo the chicken and the leek are both well charred and glazed, and the sauce has pooled on the plate.

Tsukune is the one I suggest to anyone nervous about unfamiliar cuts. FUN! JAPAN says it is minced chicken with herbs, often served with a raw egg yolk for dipping, and recommends sauce. The photo is labeled as the salt version, so it is a good reminder that most cuts can be ordered either way.

Seseri is the neck. TableCheck calls it fatty and juicy, and the crinkled surface in the photo, with its crisp edges, suggests how the fat renders over the fire. I will not say more about its texture than the sources do.

Sasami looks very different: pale, lean and only lightly marked by the grill. These two skewers have a few grill marks and dark specks of seasoning. Because the meat is so lean, it is the cut where the accompaniments matter, and TableCheck mentions plum sauce, wasabi or shiso.

This is bonjiri, the tail. TableCheck calls it rare and fatty. The two skewers have a crinkled, golden surface, and the small dark tips at the ends of some pieces look like bone to me, so I would expect a few bits that need care. I am describing the picture, not a texture I can promise.

Mune is breast, the lean cut. Here it is grilled and dressed with a pink mayonnaise made with mentaiko (seasoned cod roe) and a few strips of nori. Commons gives that description, and the Japanese title adds that it is "grilled" (yaki). It shows how a mild, lean cut is often paired with a strong topping.

Kawa is the skin. These five skewers, labeled by Commons as Hakata torikawa with salt, are crinkled and golden and packed closely on the stick. I will not explain how the skin is prepared, because I have no source for it, but the photo shows how different it looks from the lean cuts above.

These are hatsu, the heart, ordered with tare. TableCheck calls the heart meaty and softer than the gizzard. The sauce has turned the pieces dark and glossy, and the extra has dripped onto the plate.

Tebasaki are wings. TableCheck calls them a specialty of Nagoya, though they appear on yakitori menus elsewhere too. These two are blistered golden with black pepper, and the wing tips are still attached.

Salt or Sauce塩とタレ

When you order, you are usually asked whether you want shio (salt) or tare (sauce). FUN! JAPAN (how to enjoy yakitori) gives a rule of thumb: sauce for fatty cuts such as thigh, salt for lean cuts such as breast, and it says shio lets you taste the difference between cuts while tare shows off a restaurant's own sauce. TableCheck says staff typically ask, so you can simply ask what they recommend.

This is the sauce side. Commons calls these beef skewers, with the Japanese title meaning sauce-grilled beef, and they are a reminder that yakitori menus often go beyond chicken. The glaze is dark and glossy, and the sticks are charred at the ends from the grill.

Pork turns up too. Commons says buta-bara (pork belly) skewers are often served at yakitori shops in Kyushu, in prefectures such as Saga and Fukuoka, and the photo is from a charcoal yakitori shop in Kitakyushu. Here the thick pieces alternate with onion on a blue-and-white plate. I cannot tell from the picture whether it is salt or sauce.

At the Izakaya居酒屋で

Yakitori is served at everything from a smoky counter with a handful of seats to a large chain. Prices follow: TableCheck lists chef's-choice courses from about 2,980 yen up to more than 13,000 yen, though the article does not date them, while the photo at the top shows single skewers at 176 to 198 yen in 2023.

This is the street front of a chain branch in Osaka, with a bright sign, dark wooden doors and a bicycle at the door. I include it to show what many casual yakitori izakaya look like from the outside, and it is a 2016 photograph, so the signs may have changed.

A few habits from the two guides I have been quoting:

  • Eat them hot. Order a few kinds at a time, not everything at once, so the skewers do not go cold.
  • Order in pairs. FUN! JAPAN says it is general etiquette to order at least two of the same cut.
  • Off the skewer or onto the plate. Both are fine at a sit-down restaurant, according to TableCheck.
  • Used skewers. Put them in the container provided, not on a shared platter.
  • Drinks. TableCheck mentions beer, oolong tea or soda as common pairings, and wine or sake at some upscale places.

If you would rather have a guide lead you, a local food tour is one way to try it in a neighborhood with someone who can read the menu.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is yakitori always chicken?
The name means grilled bird, and chicken is the core of the menu, including organ cuts such as liver, heart and gizzard. Many menus add other meats, though. The photos on this page include a beef skewer and a plate of pork-belly skewers, and Commons describes one set as including pork harami, so ask or check the menu.
Should I choose salt or sauce?
Shio is salt and tare is a sweet-savory sauce. FUN! JAPAN gives a rule of thumb: sauce for fatty cuts such as thigh, salt for lean cuts such as breast. TableCheck says staff typically ask which you prefer, so you can ask for their recommendation.
What is negima?
Negima is chicken pieces alternating with green onion or leek on a skewer. FUN! JAPAN describes it as thigh with green onion, and TableCheck says thigh or breast pieces, so the cut can vary by restaurant.
How many skewers should I order at once?
FUN! JAPAN says it is general etiquette to order at least two skewers of the same cut, since the chef cooks many at once. TableCheck advises ordering a few types at a time, not everything at once, so the skewers do not go cold.
Can I take the meat off the skewer?
Yes, at sit-down restaurants. TableCheck says eating straight off the skewer is fine and that sliding the pieces onto your plate with chopsticks is also acceptable. Put used skewers in the container provided, not on a shared platter.
How much does yakitori cost?
It varies a lot. The set in the photo at the top is described on Commons as six skewers for 979 yen in August 2023, with single skewers at 176 to 198 yen. A 2019 FUN! JAPAN article gave 120 to 160 yen per skewer, and TableCheck lists chef's courses from 2,980 yen to over 13,000 yen. These are undated or old, so please check the current menu.

Final Thoughtsむすびに

Start with negima or tsukune, add something lean like sasami and something richer like seseri, and ask the staff about salt or sauce. For more dishes, including breaded and deep-fried kushiage, see our guide to popular Japanese dishes.
