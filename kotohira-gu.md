# Kotohira-gu: The Shrine That Survived Everything by Becoming Something Else

**Meta description:** Kotohira-gu's 1,368 steps lead to a shrine that started as an Indian crocodile god, survived Japan's most violent anti-Buddhist purge by changing its name, and now has a spacecraft model in its offering hall.

---

The shrine at the top of Mount Zozu in Kagawa Prefecture has been many things. It began as a water deity worshipped by ancient Indians on the banks of the Ganges River. It arrived in Japan through Buddhism, merged with mountain asceticism, absorbed a feared imperial ghost, and became the most popular pilgrimage destination in Edo-period Japan — the one that ordinary people, who could not afford or manage the journey themselves, sent their dogs to complete on their behalf. In 1868, it was ordered to purge every trace of Buddhism from its identity. It complied so completely and so quickly that it survived while hundreds of similar sites were destroyed. Today it guards not only sailors and fishermen but space missions, and its offering hall contains the hull of a solar-powered boat that crossed the Pacific Ocean.

All of this is the same shrine. Understanding how it kept becoming something new without ceasing to be itself is the most interesting thing about the 1,368 steps you are about to climb.

## From the Ganges to the Seto Inland Sea

The thread that connects Kotohira-gu's present to its deepest origin is water. The shrine's founding deity traces back to Kumbhira — an ancient Indian river god embodied in the Ganges crocodile, a creature associated with the dangerous, life-giving power of sacred rivers. When Buddhism absorbed this water deity, Kumbhira became Kubira Daisho, one of the twelve divine generals who guard Yakushi Nyorai, the Medicine Buddha. Traveling with Buddhism through Central Asia and China and eventually to Japan, the deity landed in the mountains of Shikoku, where it encountered a landscape that needed exactly this kind of protection: the Seto Inland Sea, one of the most trafficked and treacherous stretches of water in Japan.

The fusion with local tradition that followed was complete enough that the resulting deity — Zozu-san Konpira Daigongen, the Great Incarnation of Konpira of Elephant Head Mountain — was understood as a new entity rather than a foreign import. Sailors and fishermen of the Inland Sea adopted it as their patron with an intensity that has not weakened in a thousand years. Model ships, propellers, navigation instruments, and photographs of vessels whose crews prayed here before departure have filled the shrine's ema halls continuously since the Edo period. A solar-powered catamaran that crossed the Pacific sits there now among boats made of lacquered wood that date from the 17th century. The deity that started as a Ganges crocodile has ended up, for the moment, guarding spacecraft.

## The Emperor Who Became a Ghost

In 1156, Emperor Sutoku lost a succession dispute and was exiled to Sanuki Province — present-day Kagawa — by his own son. He died in exile in 1164, reportedly bitter enough to have bitten through his tongue and written sutras in his own blood, cursing the capital as he died. Almost immediately afterward, a series of disasters struck Kyoto: fires, epidemics, the deaths of prominent figures. The conclusion reached by the court was that Sutoku had become an angry spirit, one of the most powerful onryo in Japanese history.

The solution was enshrinement. In 1165, Sutoku's spirit was formally placed in the main hall at Kotohira, alongside the existing deity. An embittered imperial ghost made into a guardian: this is the logic of Japanese religious history, where the most dangerous spirits are the ones most worth having on your side. Sutoku's presence transformed Kotohira from a regional maritime shrine into something with national significance — a place where the power of the wronged dead was harnessed for the protection of the living.

## The Purge and the Pivot

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 came with a religious directive that threatened the existence of every site like Kotohira: the shinbutsu bunri, or separation of Shinto and Buddhism, followed by the haibutsu kishaku — the destruction of Buddhism. Shrines that had operated for centuries as integrated Buddhist-Shinto institutions were ordered to eliminate all Buddhist elements immediately. Many did not survive the process.

Kotohira's head administrator at the time, a Buddhist priest named Yujo, responded with a transformation so rapid and thorough that it reads more like a corporate rebranding than a religious conversion. He changed the principal deity from the Buddhist Konpira Daigongen to the Shinto god Omononushi-no-Kami — a native Japanese deity with no Buddhist associations. He changed the name from Konpira Daigongen to Kotohira-gu. He had himself defrocked, became a Shinto priest, and took the position of chief priest of the newly constituted shrine. Buddhist statues and ritual objects that could not be explained away were quietly designated "cultural treasures" and hidden, or transferred to nearby Buddhist temples for safekeeping.

The Eleven-Faced Kannon that had been one of the shrine's principal objects of worship disappeared into protective obscurity. The crocodile-river-god-turned-Buddhist-general-turned-Shinto-deity had become, at least on paper, something Japan's new government could accept. The shrine survived. The transformation was so complete that visitors today encounter a Shinto institution with no visible trace of its Buddhist centuries — unless they know to look for what's missing.

## The Step That Isn't There

The approach to Kotohira-gu is 785 steps to the main hall and 1,368 to the inner shrine at the summit. This is well known. Less well known is that the actual physical count of steps to the main hall should be 786 — but 786 can be read in Japanese as *na-ya-mu*, meaning "to suffer" or "to be troubled." This was considered inauspicious for a place people visit specifically to have their troubles resolved.

The solution was a single step placed at the Yaminotoge waypoint that goes down rather than up. Pilgrims descend one step before the final ascent, so that the count they climb is 785 rather than 786. The step that removes suffering is a step downward. It is a small piece of design that encodes an entire theology: you put your troubles down before you continue up.

The climb itself is a sustained physical encounter with the idea of ascent. The stone steps are wide and smooth from centuries of feet; the approach is lined with stone lanterns and cedar trees that provide shade in summer. At around the 365-step mark, the Omotemon gate marks the entry into the shrine precinct proper. Higher up, five vendors — the Gonin Byakusho, a group granted hereditary rights to sell within the precinct during the Edo period — operate small stalls selling Kamiyoshiame, a traditional golden candy that comes with a small wooden hammer for breaking it. The hammer and the candy are shared: the act of breaking the candy and distributing it to companions is part of the transaction.

## Konpira-inu: The Dogs That Went Alone

The Edo-period pilgrimage boom that made Kotohira one of Japan's most visited shrines produced a custom unlike anything at any other site in Japan. Ordinary people who could not make the journey themselves — because of illness, poverty, age, or the difficulty of travel — would send a dog.

The dog would be equipped with a small pouch containing a wooden identification tag, the appropriate offering money, and enough funds for food and lodging along the way. It would be released into the care of strangers on the highway. A system of informal mutual obligation took over: travelers who encountered a Konpira-inu would feed it, house it if needed, pass it along to the next willing party, and eventually someone would climb the steps with the dog, make the offering on its owner's behalf, collect a return amulet, and begin the process of sending the dog home.

That this worked — that dogs routinely completed the journey from Edo to Kagawa and returned — says something about the quality of mutual obligation that the pilgrimage generated in Edo-period Japanese society. The Konpira-inu is not a legend. It is documented in contemporaneous records and illustrated in woodblock prints. A bronze statue of a Konpira-inu stands near the bottom of the steps today. Travelers who encounter it are not required to take it anywhere.

## The Offering Hall That Became a Museum

The Ema-do offering hall partway up the approach has accumulated, over centuries, a collection that reflects every era of Japanese maritime history. The oldest pieces are painted wooden votive plaques depicting storms survived and catches landed. More substantial donations followed as the shrine's reach grew: scale models of wooden sailing ships, then iron steamships, then naval vessels. A propeller from a 20th-century freighter. Navigation instruments. Photographs of ships at sea.

Among them, incongruous in aluminum and solar panels, is the hull of Mermaid III — the solar-powered double-hulled boat in which the adventurer Kenichi Horie crossed the Pacific Ocean in 1996. It sits in the same hall as the 17th-century ship models, offered for the same reasons. The water deity that guards crossings does not distinguish between centuries.

In 1990, the astronaut Toyohiro Akiyama carried a white Kotohira-gu protective charm in the breast pocket of his spacesuit as he traveled to the Mir space station — the first Japanese person to go to space. The shrine now receives votive offerings from aerospace organizations, and what the Edo-period records called "maritime safety" has been quietly extended, without any formal announcement, to cover trajectories that require escaping Earth's gravity.

## The Sweep Mine Memorial

On a hill off the main approach, reached by a path most visitors do not take, stands the Minesweeper War Dead Memorial — a monument to the 79 naval personnel who died clearing sea mines from Japanese waters after World War II ended, including men who participated in secret Korean War minesweeping missions that the Japanese government did not officially acknowledge for decades. Prime Minister Yoshida Shigeru wrote the inscription in 1952. The Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force holds a memorial ceremony here every May.

The water deity's protection, understood from the beginning as covering those who risk their lives on water, extended here too — to men who died doing dangerous, largely unrecognized work in the years after a war that had officially ended. The memorial is not on most tourist maps of the shrine.

## Visiting Today

Kotohira-gu is free to visit; there is no admission fee for the main precinct. The inner shrine at the summit charges a small fee. The Treasure House and Museum of Art on the approach charge separate admission.

The closest station is Kotohira Station, served by the JR Dosan Line and the Kotoden Kotohira Line. From Takamatsu, the journey takes approximately one hour by JR limited express. From Okayama on the Shinkansen main line, the Marine Liner across the Seto Ohashi Bridge to Tadotsu and then a local train to Kotohira takes approximately 75 minutes.

The climb to the main hall (785 steps) takes most visitors 30 to 45 minutes at a moderate pace. Sedan chairs (kago) are available for hire at the foot of the steps for those who cannot manage the climb. The inner shrine at 1,368 steps requires an additional 583 steps beyond the main hall and takes another 30 to 40 minutes; the path becomes steeper and less maintained above the main hall, with tree roots and uneven surfaces. The full round trip from bottom to inner shrine and back takes approximately two and a half to three hours.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What god is worshipped at Kotohira-gu?**
The principal deity is Omononushi-no-Kami, alongside the spirit of Emperor Sutoku. The shrine's historical roots go back to Konpira, a deity derived from Kumbhira — an ancient Indian water god associated with the Ganges crocodile — who arrived in Japan through Buddhism and merged with local Shinto tradition before the official separation of the two religions in 1868.

**How many steps are there at Kotohira-gu?**
785 steps to the main hall (Gohonden) and 1,368 steps to the inner shrine (Okusha / Izutama Shrine) at the summit. The climb to the main hall takes most visitors 30 to 45 minutes; the full round trip to the inner shrine takes approximately two and a half to three hours.

**What is a Konpira-inu?**
A dog sent to Kotohira-gu as a proxy pilgrim by someone who could not make the journey themselves. The practice was common in the Edo period: the dog was equipped with an identification pouch, offering money, and travel funds, then passed along by strangers from one traveler to the next until it reached the shrine and was sent home with a return amulet. The custom is documented in period records and illustrated in woodblock prints.

**Why does the step count seem to skip a number?**
The "missing" step is at the Yaminotoge waypoint, where a single step goes downward rather than upward. This was added because the otherwise correct count of 786 steps can be read in Japanese as *na-ya-mu* (to suffer/be troubled) — inauspicious for a pilgrimage site. The descending step reduces the count to 785 and is interpreted as symbolically putting down one's troubles before the final ascent.

**How do I get to Kotohira-gu?**
From Takamatsu, take the JR Dosan Line limited express to Kotohira Station (approximately 1 hour). From Okayama on the Shinkansen, take the Marine Liner to Tadotsu and transfer to a local train to Kotohira (approximately 75 minutes total). The shrine steps begin a short walk from the station.

**Is the inner shrine worth climbing to?**
The inner shrine at 1,368 steps is significantly harder than the main hall and the path is rougher above the main precinct. The view from the summit is expansive and the atmosphere is quieter and more austere than the busier main hall. If you are physically able and have the time, the additional climb is worthwhile; if you can only do one, the main hall at 785 steps is the historical and religious center of the site.
