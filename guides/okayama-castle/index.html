Okayama Castle: The Black Crow Castle, a 1966 Rebuild and Korakuen Next Door

岡山城

Japan's black castle, a 1966 rebuild of the keep, two original turrets and Korakuen next door.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I send people to Okayama Castle for one reason: it is black. Most castles in Japan are white, and Himeji, the one everyone knows, is the whitest of all. Okayama's keep is dark, with black boards and gold on the roof, and the locals call it the Crow Castle. It is also a rebuild, which I say first, because that changes what you should expect inside.

This guide covers who built it, why it is black, what the 1945 fire took, what is still original, and the practical details, including Korakuen next door. Where my sources disagree, I say so.

From the Nawa Clan to the Ikeda築城

Wikipedia says the Nawa clan built the first castle here between 1346 and 1369. In 1570, it says, Ukita Naoie killed the castle lord Kanemitsu Munetaka and began remodeling. Naoie's son, Ukita Hideie, completed the main keep in 1597. GLTJP says the work took about eight years and was guided by Toyotomi Hideyoshi.

Hideie sided with the Toyotomi at Sekigahara in 1600, was captured by the Tokugawa and was exiled to Hachijo. Wikipedia says the castle then went to Kobayakawa Hideaki, who died two years later without an heir, and then to the Ikeda clan, who held it from 1602 to 1869. GLTJP adds that the lords after Hideie expanded the castle town, which grew into today's Okayama City, and Wikipedia says the Ikeda added Korakuen as a private garden.

After the shogunate fell, the castle passed in 1869 to the Ministry of War. Wikipedia says the outer moats were filled in between 1873 and 1882 and most buildings were dismantled, so the castle's walls gradually disappeared under the city.

This is the castle in spring, seen through fresh leaves and a few pale blossoms. The black siding is plain even from this angle, and the white gables and gold ornaments at the roof line pick it out against the pale sky. It is also a reminder of how the site works today: the keep stands above a wooded hill, so you often see it through trees.

Here is the water side of the story. The keep sits small at the right, and the river-fed moat fills most of the frame, with low wooded hills behind. Wikipedia says the outer moats were filled in between 1873 and 1882, so what you see here is what is left of the water, not the original ring.

Commons titles this view as taken from Korakuen, the garden next door, and the dark keep framed by orange leaves is the reason I suggest pairing the two. I only have the file title and no shooting date, so I cannot say which season or year this is, but the colors suggest autumn.

Why It Is Called the Crow烏城

GLTJP describes a jet-black exterior with black lacquered siding boards, and says that is why the castle is called Ujo, the Crow Castle. Wikipedia sets it against the white Himeji Castle. The two sources then differ on the gold. GLTJP mentions gold-leaf roof tiles and a second nickname, Kin-ujo, or Golden Crow Castle. Wikipedia says the keep had gilded roof tiles before Sekigahara and that today only parts of the roof, including the shachihoko, are gilded, and that the rooftop ornaments were gilded in 1996 for the 400th anniversary.

I would not promise a golden roof, then. What you can count on is the contrast: a dark keep with bright ornaments at the roof line, which looks sharpest against a clear sky.

This is the gold I would count on. The shachihoko, the tiger-headed fish ornament on the roof ridge, is plated, and the moat, the trees and the city spread out behind it. Wikipedia says the roof ornaments were gilded in 1996 for the 400th anniversary, which fits what you see here: bright gold against a dark roof.

At dusk the keep looks different. The upper floors catch a warm light, the roof edges look gilded, and the lower black walls fall into the shadow of the trees. A couple of dark shapes on the ridge look like birds, which I find fitting for a Crow Castle, though I cannot tell you what they are.

This is the castle after dark during a lantern event. The Commons description calls it the Okayama Castle Togokyo light-up, which I read as a seasonal event. The keep is washed in green, and thousands of paper lanterns glow on the lawn in front of it, with a crowd and a small stage under the walls. Events like this change from year to year, so check the dates before you plan around one.

The 1945 Fire and the 1966 Rebuild再建

GLTJP says the keep was designated a National Treasure before the air raids of 1945 burned it down. Wikipedia gives the date as 29 June 1945 and says the raid also destroyed an adjacent gate, leaving two turrets and some stone walls. Reconstruction of the keep and gate began in 1964 and finished in 1966, and Wikipedia calls the keep a concrete replica.

GLTJP says the rebuilt keep now serves as a museum of Okayama's history from the Sengoku through the Edo period. Both GLTJP and Jeepe mention extras inside, such as projection mapping, a kimono dressing experience and the Ujo Cafe, though they add that exhibits and events change by season.

The reward for climbing the museum is this: from the top you look over a curved dark roof tile to the park, the river side and the city, with low mountains beyond. It gives you a sense of why a lord wanted this hill, and of how the castle town grew into the modern city.

The exhibits include armor, and this one has red lacing and a large gold crescent on the helmet. As I read the label, it relates to Hanafusa Masanari, but the label is hard to read in my copy, so check it on site. Commons only calls it an armor at the keep.

This second armor is shown seated, in black with white and gold lacing and a yellow tassel, in front of a painted screen. Commons says only that it is an armor at the keep, and I cannot see a name for the owner, so I will not guess one.

A third armor stands in a tall case with a small label at its foot. It has a rounded helmet and a pale chest plate. Again, Commons says only that it is an armor at the keep, so I cannot tell you whose it was.

This sword has a gold-fitted sheath wrapped in dark cord, and it lies on a shelf under a panel of blue and gold. The label is partly cut off, but it seems to mention Toshogu. I would not rely on that, so read the card in the case.

This black lacquered palanquin is covered in small gold crests, which look like butterflies to me. The owner is not named in the Commons description, so I will leave it at that. It is a good example of the kind of luxury goods the museum shows alongside the weapons.

What Is Original現存する遺構

Two turrets survived the war. Wikipedia says the Agency for Cultural Affairs lists both as Important Cultural Properties. GLTJP names them as Tsukimi Yagura and Nishite Yagura. It dates Tsukimi Yagura to the Edo period, says it is the only turret still standing on the Honmaru site, and says it looks like two stories from outside but has three. It puts Nishite Yagura at around 1603 to 1615.

If you want the original wood and stone rather than a museum, these two and the surviving walls are where to spend your time.

Commons calls this the Corridor Gate. It has white plaster walls and a tiled roof, with a high stone wall at the right and a gravel path leading through to a flight of steps. I do not know whether this gate is original or rebuilt. Wikipedia says an adjacent gate was destroyed in 1945 and rebuilt with the keep, so check the signs on site.

This is the stone I mean by the surviving walls. Large rough blocks make up the lower wall, and a white-walled gatehouse with a tiled roof sits above them. Commons does not name the gate in this file, so I will not put a name on it.

This is one of the two turrets that came through the war. The lower walls are white plaster, the upper level is bare wood, and the roofs are gray tile with a curved gable, all on a low stone base with a white wall running off on both sides. Commons does not tell me which of the two turrets this is in the part of the description I could read, so I will not name it. Compare it with the descriptions above and the signs on site.

Visiting Okayama Castleアクセス

  • Admission: About 500 yen for adults. GLTJP says under 14 is free, and Jeepe says elementary and junior high students are free. A combined ticket with Korakuen is listed at 800 yen (Jeepe). Please confirm on the official site.
  • Hours: 9:00 to 17:30, last entry 17:00. Hours may be extended during events (GLTJP).
  • Closed: 29 to 31 December (GLTJP).
  • Getting there: Okayama Electric Tramway, Higashiyama Line, to Shiroshita Station, then about 10 minutes on foot, or about 15 minutes in all (GLTJP). Jeepe also mentions a bus from the east exit, about 10 to 12 minutes.
  • Stamp: A stamp for the 100 Famous Castles is available in the paid area, according to GLTJP, which says this comes from a visitor review, so confirm it.
  • Korakuen: Joined to the castle by the Tsukimi Bridge (Jeepe). Adults are about 500 yen, and hours change in October and March (Jeepe).

Korakuen is the garden next door, and this is why I pair the two. Commons says it was built in 1700 by Ikeda Tsunamasa, lord of the Okayama clan, and calls the black building the keep of Okayama Castle. The Japanese description says the view is from the shore of Sawa no Ike pond, looking at Yuishinzan hill and the keep. In early May the azaleas on the hill are pink and white, and the whole scene is doubled in the water.

The same garden in late October looks very different. The keep is a small dark shape between the trees, a few leaves have turned orange, and pine branches frame the right side. Commons gives only the date and says it is Koraku-en Garden, so it is a good guide to what to expect at that time of year, with the caveat that peak color comes later.

Commons titles this Tsukimi Bridge, and Jeepe lists it as the link between the castle and Korakuen. It is a steel truss bridge, pale gray, crossing the river with a wooded bank behind it, and a small cafe and a row of boats sit at the right. The photo was taken in August, and the green water and strong sun are what summer looks like here.

Making It a Trip滞在

GLTJP says illuminations called Ujo Togenkyo run in spring, summer and autumn, and that Korakuen holds its own illumination, Genso Teien, in the same seasons. Themes change by year, so check the dates before you plan an evening. For a white castle to compare, see our Himeji Castle guide, and for a mountain castle in the same prefecture, our Bitchu Matsuyama Castle guide.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Okayama Castle original?
No. GLTJP and Wikipedia agree that the main keep burned in the air raids of 1945, and Wikipedia gives the date as 29 June 1945. The keep you see now is a concrete replica, rebuilt between 1964 and 1966. Two turrets survived and are Important Cultural Properties, so the original is not entirely gone.
Why is Okayama Castle black?
GLTJP describes a jet-black exterior with black lacquered siding boards, which earned it the nickname Crow Castle, or Ujo. Wikipedia contrasts it with the white Himeji Castle. The same sources mention gold on the roof, and they differ a little on how much, so I would call the gilding a detail to look for rather than a promise.
How much does it cost, and when is it open?
GLTJP gives 500 yen for adults, free for under 14, from 9:00 to 17:30 with last entry at 17:00, and closed from 29 to 31 December. Jeepe says elementary and junior high students enter free, so the age line differs by source. Jeepe also lists a combined ticket with Korakuen for 800 yen. Confirm all of it on the official site, because hours can be extended for events.
Can I visit Korakuen on the same day?
Yes. Jeepe describes Korakuen and the castle as neighbors joined by the Tsukimi Bridge. It gives Korakuen as 500 yen for adults and 200 yen for ages 65 and over, with hours of about 7:30 to 18:00 from 20 March to 30 September and 8:00 to 17:00 from 1 October to 19 March, and entry closing 15 minutes earlier. Check the official page for current hours.
How do I get there from Okayama Station?
GLTJP says to take the Higashiyama Line of the Okayama Electric Tramway to Shiroshita Station, then walk about 10 minutes, for about 15 minutes in all. Jeepe gives about 4 minutes on the tram, and also mentions a bus from the east exit that takes 10 to 12 minutes. Walking the whole way is possible but takes longer.
How long should I allow?
None of my sources gives a figure. My own estimate is about an hour for the keep museum and the two turrets, and a half day if you add Korakuen. The keep is a museum rather than a climb through original rooms, so it is a short visit.

A Rebuilt Keep, an Honest Visitむすびに

Okayama Castle is worth it for its color, its two surviving turrets and the garden next door, not for original rooms. Go knowing the keep is a 1966 replica, see the turrets for the real thing, and finish in Korakuen.

⛩️

The crow was rebuilt. The color is still its own.
