Enoshima Shrine: Three Shrines, the Five-Headed Dragon and Hadaka Benzaiten

江島神社

Three shrines on an island hill, tied to a goddess and a dragon, a bridge and an escalator away from the mainland.

As a licensed guide, I like shrines you have to cross water to reach, and Enoshima Shrine is one: a bridge, a shopping street and a climb up a hill in the sea.

Enoshima Shrine is really three shrines spread along the island, all connected with the goddess Benzaiten. It is about an hour from Tokyo. This guide covers the founding legend, what each of the three shrines is, the things people come for (a Hadaka Benzaiten statue, a pair of trees, a money-washing pond) and the practical side.

The Dragon and the Goddess五頭龍と弁財天

The island has a legend. Per the Enoshima Engi, a five-headed dragon called Gozuryu terrorised the nearby villagers for about a thousand years, until in 552 the goddess Benzaiten raised Enoshima from the bay as her home (Wikipedia). Tokyo Weekender says the Engi was written in 1047 by a monk named Kokei. LIVE JAPAN adds that the dragon fell in love with her, she persuaded him to repent, and he turned into a hill that protects the area.

Discover Fujisawa says the shrine was first built at Iwaya in 552 on the order of Emperor Kinmei, and that the shrine crest, three scales surrounded by waves, comes from the Legend of the Three Scales. Treat 552 as the shrine’s tradition rather than documented history. Our Itsukushima Shrine guide covers another of the three major Benzaiten shrines.

The Three Shrines辺津宮・中津宮・奥津宮

The Zuishinmon is the white gate at the foot of the climb. Tokyo Weekender says that beyond it is a stone carving of Benzaiten and Gozuryu made for the shrine’s 1,450th anniversary. I have not checked that the gate in this photo and the carving are in the same spot.

Hetsunomiya, nearest the entrance, is the shrine most visitors reach first. Kanpai Japan says it was built in 1206 and renovated in 1976; Tokyo Weekender calls it the most picturesque of the three, with a grass purification wheel and a dragon pond. The two Benzaiten statues are kept in the Hoanden here, for a small fee (Kanagawa tourism, Discover Fujisawa).

Nakatsunomiya, halfway up, is the vermilion one in the first photo; Kanpai Japan says it dates to 853 and was last renovated in 1996. Okutsunomiya is more rustic and has an unknown founding date, near the Iwaya caves, and enshrines Takirihime, a deity who protects the sea (GLTJP). LIVE JAPAN also mentions Wadatsunomiya, built in 1994.

Wishes and Luck縁結び・金運

LIVE JAPAN describes the Musubi no Ki as a tree whose two trunks share one stem, said to bring good fortune, with wishing plaques at 500 yen. The wall of pink plaques in the photo is labelled with that name by its photographer; I read the rope-wrapped trunks on top as part of the same spot, but the source does not say so.

Near the main hall is the Zeniarai pond, where visitors wash money with water from a spring to increase it (LIVE JAPAN, GLTJP names the spot Zeniarai Hakuryuoh). I have not tried it. Plaques and charms cost around the figures LIVE JAPAN gives, so check at the office.

Visiting Enoshima Shrine参拝案内

  • Address: 2-3-8 Enoshima, Fujisawa, Kanagawa 251-0036 (Japan Documented).
  • On foot: about 14 to 15 minutes from Katase-Enoshima Station, over the bridge and up the Nakamise street (GLTJP, LIVE JAPAN). Kanagawa tourism also lists Shonan-Enoshima Station on the Enoden and the Shonan Monorail.
  • The climb: about 20 minutes on foot or about 4 minutes by escalator (LIVE JAPAN); GLTJP gives about 5 minutes. The Escar runs 8:50 to 19:05; fares in the sources differ (360 or 500 yen).
  • Hours: 8:30 to 17:00 (Kanagawa tourism), 9:00 to 17:00 (GLTJP) or 8:30 to 16:30 (Tokyo Tourism); check on the day.
  • Hoanden fee: 200 yen adults, 100 yen junior high and high school, 50 yen children (Kanagawa tourism).
  • Iwaya caves: about a 30-minute walk from the station; a 152 m lit route open about 9:00 to 16:00 (GLTJP). The eno=pass includes the Escar, the caves and two other attractions (GLTJP).
  • Time to allow: about 30 minutes for the three shrines alone (Kanagawa tourism); more with the caves.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is Enoshima Shrine?
It is a general name for three shrines, Hetsunomiya, Nakatsunomiya and Okutsunomiya, on Enoshima island in Fujisawa (Kanagawa tourism). Each enshrines three sister goddesses along with eight-armed Benzaiten and Myoon Benzaiten, one of Japan’s three major Benzaiten (Kanagawa tourism). Shrine tradition dates the first shrine to 552, in the Iwaya caves, on the order of Emperor Kinmei (Discover Fujisawa).
What is the legend of the five-headed dragon?
According to the Enoshima Engi, villagers were plagued for a thousand years by the five-headed dragon Gozuryu, until in 552 the goddess Benzaiten raised Enoshima from the sea as her home (Wikipedia, Tokyo Weekender). LIVE JAPAN says the dragon fell in love with her, she persuaded him to repent, and he became the area’s protector by turning into a hill.
Do I need to take the escalator?
No. It is an option on the climb: the Enoshima Escar, opened in 1959 as Japan’s first outdoor escalator, runs from 8:50 to 19:05 (GLTJP, LIVE JAPAN). LIVE JAPAN gives 360 yen for the full ride and about 4 minutes, against about 20 minutes on foot, while Discover Fujisawa lists 500 yen, so check the current fare. It only goes up.
How long does it take to walk from the station?
About 14 to 15 minutes from Katase-Enoshima Station to the shrine (GLTJP, LIVE JAPAN), including crossing the bridge, which takes about 5 minutes (LIVE JAPAN). Tokyo to Katase-Enoshima takes about an hour on the Odakyu Line (LIVE JAPAN).
What are the hours and fees?
The shrine grounds are free. Opening hours differ by source: 8:30 to 17:00 (Kanagawa tourism), 9:00 to 17:00 (GLTJP) and 8:30 to 16:30 (Tokyo Tourism), so check on the day. To see the Benzaiten statues in the Hoanden you pay a small fee: 200 yen for adults, 100 for junior high and high school students and 50 for children (Kanagawa tourism).
What is the “Hadaka Benten”?
Myoon Benzaiten, popularly known as Hadaka (Naked) Benzaiten, is one of the two Benzaiten statues kept at Hetsunomiya (Discover Fujisawa). The same source says Happi Benzaiten was donated by Minamoto no Yoritomo in 1182, and that the Hadaka statue was donated later, in the Edo period, though it is believed to have been made in the Kamakura period.

Before You Goむすびに

Go early on a weekend, since the Nakamise street gets busy. Kamakura is close, and our Tsurugaoka Hachimangu guide covers its main shrine.
