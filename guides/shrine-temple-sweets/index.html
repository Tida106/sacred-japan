Shrine and Temple Sweets: Five Treats Worth the Detour

神社・お寺の門前スイーツ

Walk up to almost any famous shrine or temple in Japan and the last stretch of the approach smells of grilled rice cake and sweet bean paste. Here are five I would walk a long way for.

As a licensed guide, I always tell visitors to arrive hungry. In Japan the approach to a famous shrine or temple is called the monzen, the area in front of the gate, and for centuries it has been lined with teahouses selling something sweet to pilgrims who had just walked a long way. Many of these shops are older than the buildings you came to see.

The reason is practical and a little spiritual. A pilgrim on foot needed sugar and rest, and a sweet taken after prayer was a way to share the blessing. Today the habit survives, and the sweets are easy to like: soft rice cake, sweet bean paste, a bowl of green tea. I have picked five that each belong to a particular place and have a story you can taste. Prices and opening days change, so I have left them out and suggest you check each shop or shrine before you go.

Umegae Mochi at Dazaifu Tenmangu梅ヶ枝餅（太宰府天満宮）

Dazaifu Tenmangu, near Fukuoka in Kyushu, is dedicated to Sugawara no Michizane, the scholar-statesman who is now worshipped as the god of learning. Students come from all over Japan to pray for exam success, and the approach to the shrine is lined with shops grilling its signature sweet, umegae mochi.

It is a flat, round rice cake filled with sweet red bean paste, pressed and toasted until the surface is lightly crisp. The name means plum-branch rice cake, and it comes from Michizane's famous love of plum blossoms. A well-known story says that a plum tree in his Kyoto garden flew to Dazaifu to join him in exile, and the shrine still treasures that tree today. Eat it hot, straight from the grill, and add a bowl of matcha if you want to do it the way the tea shops serve it.

🌸 The guide's tip: Umegae mochi is best eaten fresh, so buy it from a shop with a grill facing the street and eat it on the spot. Plum blossoms bloom here from about late January to March, which is the best time to pair the sweet with the real thing.

Aburimochi at Imamiya Shrineあぶり餅（今宮神社）

Imamiya Shrine sits in the north of Kyoto, away from the usual circuit, and I like it for exactly that reason. Just outside its east gate two shops face each other across the lane: Ichiwa and Kazariya. According to Discover Kyoto, Ichiwa was founded in 1002 and Kazariya in 1656, and both specialise in one thing, aburimochi.

Small pieces of rice cake, dusted with soybean flour (kinako), are skewered on bamboo, grilled over charcoal, and served with a sweet white miso sauce. The result is smoky, chewy and gently salty-sweet, closer to a savoury snack than a dessert. You sit on a bench, someone brings you a plate and a cup of tea, and you eat the skewers one by one.

🍢 The guide's tip: Both shops are small, family-run and have their own closing days. Look at their official pages or ask your hotel to check before you set out, and combine the visit with Daitoku-ji, which is close by.

Akafuku at Ise赤福（伊勢）

You cannot visit Ise without being offered Akafuku. The sweet is a soft rice cake topped with smooth red bean paste, and the paste is shaped with three ridges. According to the company, which has sold it since 1707, the ridges represent the clear water of the Isuzu River, and the white mochi underneath represents the pebbles on its bed. It is a river, in other words, served on a plate.

The best place to eat it is in Okage Yokocho, the reconstructed old street of Edo-style shops at the end of the road to Naiku, the Inner Shrine of Ise. You sit down, a tray arrives with the sweet and a cup of hot roasted tea, and for a few minutes you are doing what pilgrims did hundreds of years ago. I would eat it after the shrine visit, not before, as a quiet reward at the end of the walk.

🍵 The guide's tip: My full guide to the shrine is here: Ise Grand Shrine. Akafuku is made fresh each day, so eat it that day and not as a gift for the following week.

Momiji Manju on Miyajimaもみじ饅頭（宮島）

On the island of Miyajima, where Itsukushima Shrine's great torii stands in the sea, the signature sweet is shaped like a maple leaf. Momiji manju is a small sponge-cake pastry, usually filled with smooth red bean paste, and it is said to have been created on the island by a confectioner in the late Meiji period. The maple leaf is no accident: the island is famous for its autumn colours.

Walk along the covered shopping street, Omotesando, and you will find shops baking them in front of the window, with a line of people waiting for the first hot batch. The classic filling is red bean, but there are also custard, chocolate, cheese and matcha versions, and some shops sell a deep-fried one on a stick. I recommend starting with a plain one, still warm, while you walk toward the shrine.

🍁 The guide's tip: If you come in November, walk through Momijidani Park for the real maple leaves after the sweet one. My guide to Itsukushima Shrine explains the tides and the best time to see the torii.

Yatsuhashi in Kyoto八ツ橋（京都）

Yatsuhashi is the sweet every visitor to Kyoto ends up carrying home, and you will find it near Kiyomizu-dera, Fushimi Inari and almost every other major site. There are two kinds. The baked version is a thin, crisp, curved cracker made of rice flour, sugar and cinnamon. The soft version, nama yatsuhashi, is the same dough left unbaked and folded into a triangle around bean paste or another filling.

Its origin is debated. A popular legend credits Yatsuhashi Kengyo, a blind koto master of the 1600s, with the name, and the curved shape is said to recall the surface of the instrument. According to Niponica, the sweet was first sold to travellers at Shichijo Station, today's Kyoto Station, in the early 1900s, and the soft triangular version became popular after a suggestion in 1960. Take both home if you can: the baked kind lasts, and the soft kind should be eaten within a day or two.

How to Enjoy Them楽しむコツ

  • Pray first, eat after. The sweet is a reward for the walk, not a substitute for the visit.
  • Eat them fresh. Grilled and baked sweets are at their best within minutes. Ask a shop how long each keeps before you buy.
  • Sit down if you can. Most of these shops have a bench or a tea room, which is part of the experience and keeps crumbs off the shrine grounds.
  • Mind the grounds. Please finish eating before you pass through the gate, and keep your wrapper until you find a bin.
  • Check the details. Opening days, prices and menus change, so look at each official page before you go.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is umegae mochi?
Umegae mochi is a flat, round rice cake filled with sweet red bean paste and grilled until the surface is lightly toasted. It is the specialty of Dazaifu Tenmangu in Fukuoka, a shrine dedicated to Sugawara no Michizane, who loved plum blossoms. The name means plum-branch rice cake, and many shops along the approach grill it fresh.
What is aburimochi, and where do I eat it?
Aburimochi is small pieces of rice cake on bamboo skewers, grilled over charcoal and served in white miso sauce. The famous place for it is outside the east gate of Imamiya Shrine in northern Kyoto, where two long-established shops, Ichiwa and Kazariya, face each other. Check opening days before you go, as small shops close on set days.
Why does Akafuku look like that?
Akafuku, sold at Ise since 1707 according to the company, is soft mochi topped with smooth sweet bean paste marked with three ridges. The company explains that the ridges stand for the clear water of the Isuzu River and the white mochi underneath for the pebbles on its bed. You can eat it in Okage Yokocho, just before or after visiting Naiku, the Inner Shrine.
Is momiji manju only a Miyajima thing?
It started on Miyajima in the late Meiji period, made by a confectioner who shaped sponge-cake batter into a maple leaf, and it is now sold across Hiroshima Prefecture as a souvenir. On Miyajima itself you can watch it baked fresh in the shopping street, and many shops also sell a deep-fried version.
What is the difference between yatsuhashi and nama yatsuhashi?
Baked yatsuhashi is a thin, crisp, cinnamon-flavoured rice-flour cracker. Nama yatsuhashi is the soft, unbaked version: the same dough, folded into a triangle around bean paste or another filling. The soft kind does not keep long, so buy it for the day or the next day rather than for a long trip.
Can I bring these sweets back home?
Many of them are made fresh and eaten within a day, so ask the shop how long each one keeps. Baked yatsuhashi and boxed momiji manju are made as souvenirs and travel well. Check your own country's customs rules for bean-paste products, since these differ.

The Sweet Last Stepむすびに

I think of these sweets as the last step of a visit. You have bowed, made your wish, rung the bell, and then you sit down on a wooden bench and eat something warm. It is a small, unhurried part of the day, and it is the part my guests tell me they remember best. Pick one of the five and build a morning around it.
