Matsue Castle: The Black Keep and the Talismans That Proved Its Age

松江城

Shimane's black national-treasure keep, its 24-meter well, and the moat boats beneath it.

As a licensed guide, I tell guests that Matsue Castle is the quiet one among Japan's great keeps. It is not as famous as Himeji, but it is a black, battle-ready tower that has stood since 1611, and for decades it was not recognized as a National Treasure because nobody could prove when it was built. The proof turned up in 2012, in a shrine, in the form of two wooden talismans.

This guide covers who built the castle, why it is called Plover Castle, how those talismans changed its status, what to look for inside the keep, and the moat boats and samurai street around it, with practical details at the end.

Plover Castle千鳥城

According to the Visit Matsue tourism site, the castle was completed in 1611 after a five-year build by Horio Yoshiharu, the lord who founded Matsue. It is one of only 12 original castles in Japan, and Nippon.com adds that it is the only one in the San'in region. Another travel site counts it as one of five castles registered as National Treasures.

The keep is nicknamed Plover Castle (chidori-jo). Nippon.com explains this with its half-hipped gables, and the tourism site compares the swooping roofs to a plover's wings. Nippon.com also points out the black rain shutters on the outside, a look that is typical of the Momoyama period. Together they give the keep a darker, sharper outline than the white castles most visitors know.

Here it is in winter. The photo shows the keep in January 2017 with snow on its roofs and the ground, which makes the structure easy to read: a stone base, black lower stories, and white upper stories under layered gray roofs, with a pair of ornaments at the top corners of the roof. At the foot of the keep, a few staff are working near the entrance beside a blue sheet. It is a reminder that the castle is a living monument, and that Shimane in winter is very different from the bright July photo above.

After the Horio family, the Matsudaira clan ruled Matsue for ten generations, according to Nippon.com, which is a good reason the castle stayed an administrative center for so long.

Matsue's keep also looks good in early spring. This March 2007 photo shows red poles with pink lanterns printed with cherry blossoms scattered across the lawn, and the trees in the foreground only just in bud. The Commons file does not name an event or say how far the blossoms had come, so please treat this as one spring scene rather than a promise, and check the local blossom forecast before you plan around it. Behind the hedges the black keep stands on its stone base, with a low white-walled structure at its foot.

The Talismans That Proved Its Age国宝への道

This is my favorite part of the story. Nippon.com says the castle lost its National Treasure status in 1950 because there was no solid evidence of when it was built. Then, according to the Matsue city office, two talismans were rediscovered at Matsue Shrine on May 21, 2012. They carry an inscription dated to the first month of Keicho 16, which is 1611, and nail holes show they had once been fixed in the cellar of the keep.

That settled the question. The city says the national government's advisory council recommended the designation on May 15, 2015, and the notice was published on July 8, 2015, making the keep a National Treasure. Four more talismans and three ritual objects were included in the designation, and the city says they are kept at the Matsue History Museum.

Inside the Keep天守内部

The keep is a working defensive building, and I tell guests to look for the details. Nippon.com says the cellar holds a 24-meter-deep well, the only one surviving in a pre-modern castle. On the second to fourth floors you can find archer slits and stone-dropping brattices, which are small projecting openings for dropping stones on attackers.

At the top, the fifth-floor lookout gives open views over the city. Nippon.com says a city ordinance keeps nearby buildings from rising higher than the castle, so the view still has the castle as its center. The Visit Matsue site also notes that the keep houses a museum.

The view from the keep is worth the climb. In this September 2015 photo, the edge of a gray tiled roof with a green ridge ornament fills the lower left, and below it a lawn is laid out with hedge-lined paths among low tiled buildings and pines. Beyond the trees the city spreads out to a stretch of water and a line of hills. I am not naming the water or the buildings, because the Commons file does not.

Inside, the keep is a forest of heavy timber. This June 2011 photo, which Commons simply calls the castle's interior, shows thick square pillars and beams over a plank floor, with a bronze statue of a rider on horseback at the left and glass cases of helmets along the far wall at the right. There is very little decoration here, because the structure itself is the decoration.

Armor and Helmets

The keep also works as a small museum, and the cases are worth slowing down for. The labels are in Japanese and often too small to read in a photo, so I describe only what can be seen.

This case holds a suit of armor with black plates and gold-yellow lacing, a tall stand of arrows with a bow leaning beside it, and a second helmet on a white plinth at the back left. I cannot tell from the photo whose armor it is, so please ask at the keep or check the museum's own notes.

Along another case, a row of helmets each sits on a black lacquer box with a purple cloth draped over it, in front of a blue banner with a round family crest. The sheet pinned on the left is in Japanese. It says that the helmet is the most representative part of a suit of armor and the part that best shows the wearer's character, and that families still display helmets on Children's Day in May, wishing boys courage and health.

Here is one of those helmets close up: a dark, ribbed bowl with a fierce face-shaped ornament, two upright horns and large ear-like flaps. The label on the box reads 六十二間総覆輪兜, which I read as a 62-plate helmet with a fully rimmed edge. Please confirm the details with museum staff.

Another is quite different. A smooth, ridged, pointed bowl carries tall, branching crests on either side and a gold crescent with a manji (卍) mark. The manji is an ancient Buddhist symbol of good fortune and has no connection with the Nazi emblem that Western visitors may think of. The label is not visible in the photo, so I will not guess at the owner.

Moat Boats and Samurai Street堀川と塩見縄手

The moat is part of the experience. Nippon.com says the Horikawa canals form part of the castle's moat and are fed by the Ohashi River, which links Nakaumi lagoon and Lake Shinji. You can ride a boat around them. The boats lower their roofs to pass under low bridges, and passengers duck or lie down when the skipper signals, which is more fun than it sounds.

This July 2008 photo shows two of the boats on the moat. Each carries a group of passengers under a light canopy, with a boatman in a straw hat on the one at the back. Behind them, a long stone wall of rough, irregular blocks rises from the water, topped by pine and broadleaf trees. From the water you see the castle's defenses at the height an attacker would have seen them.

Along the moat on the other side, Shiomi Nawate Street is a stretch of about 500 meters lined with old samurai-era buildings. On it you will find the Buke Yashiki samurai residence, the Lafcadio Hearn former residence and memorial museum, and, a short walk off the street, the Meimei-an tea house. The Visit Matsue site gives these as a walkable group beside the moat.

This is Shiomi Nawate in March 2015. A quiet road runs beside long earthen walls and old timber houses with tiled roofs, and on the left, the trunks of the pines are wrapped in bamboo. A couple at the right are stopping to take photographs, which is exactly what most visitors do here. The Visit Matsue site gives the street's length as about 500 meters, so it is an easy, slow walk.

Inside a house like these, the rooms are low and dark, and the view is out to a small garden. Wikimedia Commons describes this January 2019 photo only as "at Matsue City," so I am using it as an illustration of a traditional house interior rather than naming the building. A round lattice window on the left, tatami floors, a bamboo fence and bright green pines beyond the door make a good picture of the light inside Japanese houses of this kind.

The Lafcadio Hearn Memorial Museum and the writer's former residence stand side by side on Shiomi Nawate Street. The Visit Matsue site lists the former residence at 400 yen and the memorial museum at 600 yen. Please confirm current prices on the official site.

The gate is a good place to start. It is a plain, dark timber gate with layered tiled roofs and white walls, with a wooden signpost carrying the museum's Japanese name at the front. The notice board on the right is from 2008, so please do not rely on the prices or hours on it.

The former residence has its own entrance, marked by two signboards. The Japanese board on the left introduces Hearn as the Meiji-era writer known for the ghost stories Yuki-onna and Mimi-nashi Hoichi, who came to Matsue as an English teacher. It says that after he married Setsu he rented this samurai house, which belonged to a retainer family of the Matsue domain, and that the garden wraps around its rooms. On the right are a portrait of Hearn and a plate with admission and hours. That plate dates from 2014: it lists adults at 300 yen, 8:30 to 18:30 from April to September and 8:30 to 17:00 from October to March, with no closing days. The Visit Matsue site now lists a higher price, so please treat the sign as out of date.

This view is from the veranda of the former residence, which the Commons file calls Lafcadio Hearn's Old Residence. A strip of tatami sits in the shade in the foreground, and beyond the glass doors a courtyard garden of raked gravel, mossy stones, clipped shrubs and a stone lantern fills the frame. It is a very small garden, but it was designed to be looked at from exactly this spot.

Here is the garden from another angle, in September 2006. The gravel paths curve between islands of plants, with a stone lantern at the left and a twisted old tree in front of the white wall. Commons says the garden is part of a site designated as a national historic site, which fits the care you can see in the layout.

Visiting Matsue Castleアクセス

  • Getting there: Visit Matsue says the castle is about 2 km from JR Matsue Station, or about 10 minutes by Ichibata bus to the Kencho-mae stop. Another site lists a 25 to 30 minute walk.
  • Hours: the castle tower is open daily. Visit Matsue lists 8:30 to 18:00 from April to September (last entry 17:30), and 8:30 to 17:00 from October to March (last entry 16:30). The central bailey (grounds) opens earlier, at 8:00.
  • Tickets: Visit Matsue lists 1,200 yen for adults, with online booking. Some older pages list lower prices, so please confirm the current price on the official site. Visit Matsue also lists a three-museum pass (castle keep, Hearn Memorial Museum and Samurai Residence) and a two-museum pass (castle keep and Matsue History Museum), valid for ten days after first entry.
  • Boat: Visit Matsue lists the Horikawa boat at 2,000 yen for adults, from 9:00 daily, with three piers where you can get on and off. Other sources list lower fares, so check before you book.
  • Time needed: about an hour for the keep, and a half day with the boat and Shiomi Nawate Street.
  • Check first: hours, closing days and prices can change. Please confirm on the official Matsue Castle and Visit Matsue sites before you go.

Making It a Day in Matsue滞在

Matsue pairs well with the shrines of Shimane, so many guests combine it with a visit to nearby Izumo. If you want to slow down, a night in Matsue lets you see the castle and the moat in the evening light.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How much does Matsue Castle cost?
The official Visit Matsue tourism site lists 1,200 yen for adults for the castle tower, with online booking available. Some older or third-party pages list lower prices, roughly 670 to 680 yen, with a reduced rate for overseas visitors, so please confirm the current price on the official site before you go.
Why is Matsue Castle a National Treasure?
The keep was completed in 1611 and is one of Japan's 12 original castles. According to the Matsue city office, two talismans dated to the first month of Keicho 16 (1611) were rediscovered at Matsue Shrine on May 21, 2012, and they proved when the keep was built. The castle was designated a National Treasure on July 8, 2015.
How do I get to Matsue Castle?
The official Visit Matsue site says the castle is about 2 km from JR Matsue Station. An Ichibata bus takes about 10 minutes to the Kencho-mae stop, in front of the Shimane Prefectural Office. Another site lists a 25 to 30 minute walk from the station.
How long do I need at Matsue Castle?
I plan about an hour for the keep and grounds. If you add the Horikawa moat boat and a walk along Shiomi Nawate Street, it becomes a comfortable half day.
What is the Horikawa boat tour?
It is a boat tour around the canals that form Matsue Castle's moat. Nippon.com says it takes about 50 minutes and that the boats lower their roofs to pass under low bridges. Visit Matsue lists about 40 minutes and three boarding piers. Prices and hours differ between sources, so confirm on the official site.

A Keep That Waited for Its Proofむすびに

Matsue is the castle I recommend to guests who want a keep that feels like a fortress, not a postcard. It is dark, practical, and for a long time it simply waited for two wooden talismans to be found.
