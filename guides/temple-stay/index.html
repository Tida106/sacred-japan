Temple Stay in Japan: The Beginner's Guide

宿坊体験の完全ガイド

What a shukubo really costs, what to expect, and which temples welcome first-timers

Most travelers' first question about a Japanese temple stay is whether it is "allowed" — whether an ordinary visitor with no Buddhist background can really sleep inside a working monastery, eat beside the monks, and be woken by the sound of a morning service. The answer is yes, and it has been yes for over a thousand years. Shukubo (宿坊) — temple lodging — exists precisely for this.

As a licensed guide, I send first-time visitors here more than almost anywhere else, because nothing else on a Japan itinerary slows a traveler down quite the same way. This guide is the one I wish every guest had before they booked their first night: what it actually is, what it costs, where to start, and how to avoid the two or three mistakes that catch nearly everyone.

What Is a Shukubo, Exactly?宿坊とは何か

宿坊 literally means "lodging quarters," and historically that is exactly what it was: rooms built inside a temple compound to house traveling monks and pilgrims making religious journeys. Today, hundreds of temples across Japan — from tiny single-room guesthouses to entire mountain towns of them — open those same rooms to any traveler, regardless of faith.

A shukubo is not a themed hotel. It is a real, functioning temple that happens to have guest rooms. The monks living and training there continue their normal routine around you; you are a guest in their home, not a customer in a resort built to resemble one. That distinction shapes almost everything else in this guide.

Is a Temple Stay Right for You?こんな旅行者におすすめ

You do not need to be Buddhist, or even particularly spiritual. You do need a tolerance for simplicity: thin walls, shared bathrooms at most temples, an early bedtime, and a dinner with no meat. In exchange, you get something almost no hotel can offer.

You'll love it if...

You want one night of your trip to feel genuinely different from a hotel — quiet gardens, candlelit halls, a meal eaten in silence, the chance to sit in on a real morning service.

Think twice if...

You need a private bathroom, late check-in, Wi-Fi you can count on, or a full night of uninterrupted sleep without an early wake-up call. Most shukubo can't promise any of these.

Where to Do One: Four Starting Pointsどこで体験できるか

Shukubo exist all over Japan, but these four places are where most English-speaking travelers actually begin — each with a different flavor of the experience.

EASIEST

Koyasan

高野山・和歌山県

Around fifty temples to choose from, well-established English booking, and the Okunoin night cemetery walk. The single best first temple stay in Japan. Full guide →

MOST CONVENIENT

Kyoto

京都

Stay inside the old capital itself. Shunkoin and Myoshin-ji Daishin-in offer English-friendly rooms from around ¥4,500–¥4,700 with breakfast; Chishakuin and Ninnaji suit travelers who want a larger, more formal temple.

MOST MONASTIC

Eiheiji

永平寺・福井県

Founded by Dogen in the 13th century, Eiheiji is a genuine Zen training monastery, not a casual stopover. Reservations must be made well ahead, and the schedule is strict. For visitors who want the real monastic discipline, not a taste of it.

LEAST CROWDED

Natadera

那谷寺・石川県

A Shingon temple near Kanazawa, perched on a small mountain overlooking the Japan Sea. Around ¥13,000–¥15,000 with two meals, and a fraction of the foreign visitors Koyasan sees.

What to Expect: Rooms, Food, and Morning Practice宿坊の一日

Rooms are almost always Japanese-style: tatami flooring, a low table, and a futon laid out on the floor in the evening by staff (or by you, at simpler temples). Most bathrooms are shared, though a small number of temples now offer rooms with a private bath — ask specifically if that matters to you.

Dinner is nearly always shojin ryori (精進料理), Buddhist vegetarian cuisine made without meat, fish, onion, or garlic, built on the principle of not taking life. Expect a tray of small, carefully arranged seasonal dishes rather than a single large plate — closer to a tasting menu than a typical dinner. Alcohol policy varies by temple: some permit it as a "medicinal drink," others forbid it outright, so check in advance if it matters to you.

The defining moment of most stays is the optional morning service — chanting, a short Buddhist sermon, sometimes zazen seated meditation or shakyo sutra-copying — held at or before sunrise depending on the sect. Guests are invited, never required, to attend. Many travelers set an alarm anyway; it is usually the single thing they remember most.

A shukubo isn't a hotel with a theme. It's a real temple that happens to have a spare room — and for one night, you get to live inside someone else's daily devotion.

What It Actually Costs宿泊費の目安

Prices vary more than hotel rates, because you're really booking into a wide range of temple sizes and services. As a rough guide:

  • ¥4,500–¥5,000: A simple room with breakfast only — small Kyoto temples like Shunkoin or Rokuo-in.
  • ¥11,000–¥13,000: A room with two meals at a mid-size temple, such as Ninnaji's guest hall in Kyoto.
  • ¥13,000–¥15,000: A full shukubo stay with dinner and breakfast at Koyasan or Natadera — the most common range for a first-timer's night.

Many temples, especially smaller or more remote ones, accept cash only. Confirm the payment method and any alcohol policy before you arrive — these are the two most common surprises.

How to Book One予約の方法

01

Start With an English-Friendly Temple

Koyasan's official Shukubo Association, Chishakuin, and Shunkoin all run English booking pages. If a temple's site is Japanese-only, a travel agent or your hotel's concierge can often book it on your behalf.

02

Book Well Ahead in Peak Seasons

Spring cherry blossom season and autumn foliage (especially November) fill the best rooms weeks in advance, particularly at Koyasan. A monastic temple like Eiheiji may require written reservations a month or more ahead.

03

Ask the Three Questions That Matter

Is breakfast/dinner included? Is the bathroom private or shared? Is alcohol permitted? Getting clear answers before you book prevents almost every common disappointment.

Etiquette: What Not to Do宿坊でのマナー

You are a guest inside someone's home and workplace at once. A short list of rules covers nearly every situation:

01

Keep Your Voice Down, Always

Temples are quiet places by design. Loud conversation in the halls or garden, even in the daytime, stands out immediately and disturbs monks going about their training.

02

Be on Time for Meals and Curfew

Dinner, the morning service, and lights-out all run on a fixed schedule set for the whole temple, not just you. Arriving late disrupts everyone, not only your own evening.

03

Don't Assume Wi-Fi or Late Check-In

Many temples have limited or no Wi-Fi, and front doors may lock early. Confirm both before you plan a late dinner in town.

A Few Final Tips From a Guide案内人からの助言

If this is your first temple stay, start at Koyasan — the infrastructure for foreign guests is simply the most forgiving anywhere in Japan, and the Okunoin night walk alone is worth the trip. Save Eiheiji for a second visit, once you know you want the fuller monastic version of this experience. And whichever temple you choose, pack a flashlight, dress warmer than you expect, and set your alarm for the morning service even if you think you'll skip it — almost nobody regrets going.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is a shukubo?
Shukubo is temple lodging — a room inside a working Buddhist temple that welcomes overnight guests, originally built for monks and pilgrims and now open to travelers of any faith or none.
How much does a temple stay in Japan cost?
Most temple stays run from about ¥4,500 to ¥15,000 per person per night. Simple rooms with breakfast only sit at the lower end; Koyasan and larger temples with dinner and breakfast included tend to run ¥13,000–¥15,000.
Do I need to speak Japanese to book a temple stay?
No. A growing number of temples — including Koyasan's shukubo, Chishakuin in Kyoto, and Shunkoin — have English-language websites and English-speaking staff. Others are Japanese-only, so it is worth checking before you book.
Do I have to join morning prayers or meditation?
Almost never. Morning services, zazen meditation, and sutra copying are offered as optional experiences at most temples, not requirements. You can usually choose to sleep in instead.
What is shojin ryori?
Shojin ryori is Buddhist vegetarian cuisine served at most temple stays, prepared without meat, fish, or alcohol, rooted in the principle of not taking life. It is standard at nearly every shukubo dinner.
Which temple stay is best for a first-timer?
Koyasan is the easiest first temple stay for English-speaking travelers: dozens of temples to choose from, well-established tourist infrastructure, and the unforgettable Okunoin night walk. Kyoto's Shunkoin and Chishakuin are good alternatives if you want to stay in the city.
Is Eiheiji a good choice for a casual traveler?
Not really. Eiheiji, the great Soto Zen training monastery in Fukui, keeps a strict monastic schedule and requires reservations by mail or fax well in advance. It suits visitors who specifically want a rigorous Zen training experience, not a relaxed first temple stay.

Of everything a visitor can add to a Japan itinerary, one night in a shukubo is consistently among the most remembered — not because it is luxurious, but because it isn't.
