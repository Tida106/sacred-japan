Hakone Shrine: The Dragon That Became a God

箱根神社

A nine-headed dragon tamed by a monk, a fugitive shogun who became Japan's most powerful man, and a torii gate built in 1952 to commemorate a peace treaty — in a volcanic lake 3,100 years old.

Every travel photograph of Japan has its icons: the red gates of Fushimi Inari, the golden pavilion reflected in its pond, the deer of Nara. The image of a red torii gate rising from a mountain lake with Mount Fuji in the background belongs to Hakone Shrine, and it has become, through the logic of photography and social media, one of the most reproduced images of Japan in the world. Most visitors come for that image, and leave with it. The photograph is not wrong. But the lake it depicts is a volcanic crater 3,100 years old, the gate was built in 1952 to mark the end of the Allied occupation of Japan, and the creature supposedly living in the water below was terrorizing villages with demands for human sacrifice until a Buddhist monk sat down at the lake's edge and refused to move until it surrendered.

The Dragon of Lake Ashi竜神の湖

Lake Ashi fills the caldera of the Hakone volcano, formed roughly 3,100 years ago. Its waters are still and clear at the surface; its bottom drops to 43 meters in places. Long before any shrine stood on its banks, the communities living around the lake explained the volcanic activity of the surrounding mountains — the steam vents, the sulfurous springs, the sudden storms — through the presence of a nine-headed dragon named Kuzuryu, said to inhabit the lake and make its destructive will known through weather and disaster. The price of the dragon's restraint was an annual tribute: a young woman from the village, offered to the water.

This arrangement ended, according to shrine tradition, in 757 CE, when a Buddhist priest named Mangan Shonin arrived at the lake. Mangan had been conducting ascetic practice in the Hakone mountains — the name "Mangan" refers to the reading of a thousand Buddhist sutras, which he had completed — and had received a divine commission to deal with the creature in the water. He placed a stone slab in the shallows of the lake and sat on it, meditating, for three days and three nights. Kuzuryu, faced with this kind of sustained spiritual attention, eventually conceded. It gave up its practice of demanding sacrifices and agreed to become the lake's guardian deity instead, protecting the region rather than menacing it. Mangan built a sanctuary on the lakeside to enshrine the transformed dragon, and the religious presence at Lake Ashi began.

The dragon gave up its sacrifices and became a guardian — Hakone Shrine was built not to contain a monster, but to honor the one that agreed to protect.

The dragon is not merely a founding legend. Kuzuryu no Okami — the Great God of the Nine-Headed Dragon — is actively enshrined today at Kuzuryu Shrine, both in a sub-shrine within Hakone Shrine's main precinct and in a separate hongu (main shrine) in the forest by the lake, accessible via a longer trail. The purification fountain near the main hall, the Ryujin Suisha, flows through nine dragon heads and is fed by a natural spring; it is the closest the shrine's physical infrastructure gets to representing the deity below the water.

Yoritomo and the Samurai Tradition将軍の庇護

Hakone Shrine's influence spread beyond the lake communities in the late 12th century through an accident of military history. In 1180, Minamoto no Yoritomo — then a young rebel on the run after a catastrophic defeat against the ruling Taira clan at the Battle of Ishibashiyama — fled through the Hakone mountains and reportedly took shelter under the shrine's protection. He survived. He went on to destroy the Taira, establish the Kamakura shogunate, and become the most powerful man in Japan.

Yoritomo did not forget where he had sheltered. He became a devoted patron of Hakone Shrine, making regular pilgrimages and donating land and resources. His successors continued the tradition, and by the Kamakura period the shrine had acquired a strong association with the samurai class and with prayers for victory in battle, safe travel through mountain passes, and protection from harm. The Tokaido — the great coastal highway connecting Edo to Kyoto — passed through the Hakone area, and for centuries travelers making the difficult crossing of the Hakone Pass stopped to pray at the shrine before and after.

The same Yoritomo who sheltered here also founded Tsurugaoka Hachimangu in Kamakura — the shrine that became the spiritual headquarters of his shogunate. The two shrines share a founding patron separated by a single dramatic year.

The Peace Torii and Its History平和の鳥居

The image that has made Hakone Shrine globally recognized was not built until 1952. The Heiwa no Torii — the Torii of Peace — was erected on the lakeside that year to commemorate the Treaty of San Francisco, signed in September 1951, which formally ended the Allied occupation of Japan and restored Japanese sovereignty. It stands in shallow water at the lake's edge, its feet in the lake, its crossbeam overhead, framing whatever is beyond it: cedar forest, water, and on clear days, Mount Fuji.

The torii is approximately 15 meters tall. Its vermilion lacquer is maintained and periodically renewed. The alignment with Mount Fuji is not accidental — the gate was positioned with the mountain's profile in mind, and the view is most reliably clear in winter, when cold, dry air tends to push the cloud cover away from the mountain's cone. The gate has been photographed from boats, from the shore, from aerial perspectives, and from inside the water by people willing to wade; all of these photographs look essentially like Japan.

The Shrine Complex神社の境内

Behind the lakeside torii, a path through ancient cedar forest — some trees estimated at over 800 years old — leads uphill through a series of smaller torii gates to the main shrine buildings. The current main hall dates from a 1936 reconstruction after fire, in the gongen-zukuri architectural style that blends Buddhist and Shinto elements; behind it, a grove of himeshara trees (Stewartia monadelpha) is designated a Natural Monument of Kanagawa Prefecture. The shrine treasure house displays five objects classified as Important Cultural Properties.

At the summit of Mount Komagatake, accessible by ropeway from the Hakone Komagatake Ropeway or via hiking trails, stands Hakone Mototsumiya — the original mountain shrine that preceded the lakeside complex, and the place where worship of the Hakone deities is considered to have first been established before Mangan moved the focus to the lake. Clear days from the summit offer views of Lake Ashi, Mount Fuji, and the Pacific Ocean simultaneously.

The traditional pilgrimage circuit of Hakone — the Sansha Mairi, or Three Shrine Visit — combines Hakone Shrine, Kuzuryu Shrine (at the lake's forest hongu), and Hakone Mototsumiya, each representing a different aspect of the region's sacred geography and a different phase of its religious history.

Visiting Today参拝案内

Hakone Shrine is free to enter. The lakeside torii area is accessible at any hour; the main shrine precinct and paths are open from early morning. From Tokyo, the most direct route is the Romancecar limited express from Shinjuku to Hakone-Yumoto (approximately 85 minutes), followed by bus to the Hakonejinja-iriguchi stop (about 30 minutes). A Hakone day trip combining the shrine, a Lake Ashi boat cruise, and the Hakone Open-Air Museum is a standard circuit achievable in a full day from Tokyo.

Getting there
Take the Odakyu Romancecar limited express from Shinjuku Station to Hakone-Yumoto (approximately 85 minutes), then board a bus to the Hakonejinja-iriguchi stop (about 30 minutes). The shrine is a short walk from the bus stop. The Hakone Free Pass, available from Odakyu at Shinjuku or Odawara stations, covers buses, the Hakone Tozan Railway, cable cars, and the Lake Ashi sightseeing boats.
Admission
Entry to the shrine grounds and the lakeside torii is free. The treasure house may have a small admission fee. The Lake Ashi sightseeing boats (Hakone Sightseeing Cruise) are covered by the Hakone Free Pass.
Hours
The lakeside torii area is open 24 hours. The main shrine precinct is accessible from early morning; the shrine office operates approximately 8:00–17:00.
Best time for Mount Fuji views
Winter (December through February) offers the clearest views of Mount Fuji from across the lake — cold, dry air reduces the cloud cover that frequently obscures the mountain in warmer months. Early morning is most reliable before atmospheric haze builds. In summer, Fuji is often invisible.
What to book in advance
No advance booking is required for the shrine itself. For guided day trips from Tokyo that combine the shrine, a Lake Ashi cruise, the Open-Air Museum, and Hakone's hot spring culture, browse Hakone day trip tours from Tokyo through Viator.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is the story behind Hakone Shrine's torii gate in the lake?
The Heiwa no Torii (Torii of Peace) was built in 1952 to commemorate the Treaty of San Francisco, which ended the Allied occupation of Japan in 1951. It was positioned to align with Mount Fuji on clear days. The shrine itself was founded in 757 CE.
What is Kuzuryu?
Kuzuryu is a nine-headed dragon said to have inhabited Lake Ashi and terrorized the surrounding villages before being subdued by the Buddhist priest Mangan Shonin in 757 CE. After being subdued, the dragon became the lake's guardian deity and is actively enshrined at Kuzuryu Shrine within Hakone Shrine's precinct.
Who built Hakone Shrine?
The shrine was founded in 757 CE by the Buddhist priest Mangan Shonin after he subdued the dragon of Lake Ashi through three days and nights of meditation. The shrine's fame spread nationally after Minamoto no Yoritomo, founder of the Kamakura shogunate, became a devoted patron following his survival of the Battle of Ishibashiyama in 1180.
How do I get to Hakone Shrine from Tokyo?
Take the Odakyu Romancecar limited express from Shinjuku to Hakone-Yumoto (approximately 85 minutes), then a bus to the Hakonejinja-iriguchi stop (about 30 minutes). The Hakone Free Pass covers most transportation in the area including buses, the Hakone Tozan Railway, cable cars, and the Lake Ashi sightseeing boats.
What is the best time to see Mount Fuji from Hakone Shrine?
Winter (December through February) offers the clearest views, as cold, dry air reduces cloud cover over Mount Fuji. Early morning is most reliable before atmospheric haze develops. The mountain is frequently obscured in summer due to humidity.
What is the Sansha Mairi pilgrimage circuit?
The Sansha Mairi (Three Shrine Visit) is the traditional Hakone pilgrimage circuit combining Hakone Shrine (the main lakeside shrine), the forest hongu of Kuzuryu Shrine (a longer walk along Lake Ashi), and Hakone Mototsumiya on the summit of Mount Komagatake (accessible by ropeway). Together they cover the three major sacred sites of the Hakone area.

The Dragon Below the Waterむすびに

The photograph everyone takes at Hakone Shrine is real — the lake is that still, the torii is that red, and on winter mornings Mount Fuji does appear exactly where you hope it will. But behind the photograph is a 3,100-year-old volcanic crater, a dragon that traded sacrifice for guardianship, a shogun who said his prayers here before building Japan's first warrior government, and a postwar gate erected so that a country might remember the year it got its sovereignty back. Very few images carry this much history inside them.

For more on the samurai tradition that shaped Hakone, see our guide to Tsurugaoka Hachimangu in Kamakura — founded by the same Yoritomo who sheltered here in 1180.
