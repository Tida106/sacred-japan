Sake and Shrines: Why Japan Offers Sake to the Gods

神様とお酒のはなし

Walk through a big shrine in Japan and sooner or later you will meet a wall of straw-wrapped sake barrels. Here is why they are there, and where to follow the trail.

As a licensed guide, I have a favorite moment on shrine tours. We reach the big hall, and my guests spot a tall stack of straw-wrapped barrels with brewery names painted on them. “Is that a bar?” someone always asks. It is not. It is an offering, and it says a lot about how Japan thinks about sake.

In Shinto, sake is not just a drink. It is a gift to the kami, the gods and spirits of the land, and then it is shared with the people. Once you know that, you start to see sake everywhere: in rituals, in festivals, and even in the green ball hanging from a brewery door. This guide follows that trail.

Omiki: Sake Shared with the Kamiお神酒

Sake offered at a shrine is called miki, or omiki with the polite prefix. According to Japan's tourism ministry database, it is offered to the kami regularly and shared with worshippers after rituals and festivals. Sake is seen as a way to bridge the gap between people and the kami.

That sharing is the point. If you attend a ceremony, you may be handed a small cup at the end, and drinking it is a way of taking part in the blessing. I always tell guests to accept it with both hands and sip a little, even if they do not usually drink.

The Barrels at Meiji Jingu明治神宮の酒樽

The easiest place to see the barrels is Meiji Jingu in Tokyo. Each year, members of a national association of sake brewers donate straw-covered barrels to honor Emperor Meiji and Empress Shoken, who are enshrined there. The barrels are empty and decorative, and they are offered to the kami along with bottled sake.

Look beside them and you will find barrels of wine. The pairing reflects the Meiji period, when foreign influences were blended with Japanese tradition. I like to stop here because it shows, in one photo, how old customs and new ones meet.

📷 The guide's tip: Read the names on the barrels. Each one is a brewery, so the stack is a little map of Japan's sake regions. For the full shrine story, see our Meiji Jingu guide.

Omiwa Shrine and the Cedar Ball大神神社と杉玉

If sake has a spiritual home, many people point to Omiwa Shrine at the foot of Mount Miwa in Nara. It is worshipped as a god of sake brewing. The shrine has no main hall, because the mountain itself is the object of worship. In the old legend recorded in the Kojiki, the deity Omononushi is worshipped at Mount Miwa.

Every year on November 14, brewers and master brewers from across the country gather here to pray for the safe making of their new sake. A large cedar ball hanging at the entrance is replaced with a new one that day, and participating brewers take home a small ball made from the shrine's cedar leaves to hang at their own gates.

That is the origin of the sugidama, or sakabayashi, the ball of cedar leaf tips you see hanging from sake breweries and some pubs. It is said to have started at Omiwa. It is bright green when it first goes up, usually around February or March when the new sake is ready, and it slowly turns brown as the sake matures. When you see a brown one, you can guess the brewery has been making sake for a while.

🌿 The guide's tip: Look up when you walk past an old brewery or a sake bar. The color of the cedar ball tells you roughly where we are in the brewing year.

Matsunoo Taisha, Kyoto松尾大社

In western Kyoto, Matsunoo Taisha is one of the city's oldest shrines. Its own English page says it was founded in 701, and that a lord of the Hata clan began worship after seeing a tortoise in the stream at the foot of a waterfall. The main hall you see today dates from 1397, according to a tourism article I checked.

The shrine is visited by sake brewers who pray for the success of their business. Breweries from across Japan have presented barrels, which are said to be stacked in front of the portable-shrine storehouse. Behind the main building is a spring called Kame-no-I, the Tortoise Well, and visitors come for its water, which is believed to bring good health and long life.

I like Matsunoo for a quiet half day: it is far from the crowds of central Kyoto, and the tortoise motifs are everywhere once you start looking.

Fushimi and Nada: Where to Taste伏見と灘

After the shrines, you can follow the sake to the source. Two districts are the classics, and both are easy to reach from where most visitors stay.

Fushimi, Kyoto. Fushimi is known for its fine water. Gekkeikan, which Time Out says has made sake since 1637, runs a museum with a small brewery next door. The same article notes that tours are in Japanese with bilingual signs and advises booking at least a day ahead, so please check the official page. Fushimi is also the home of Fushimi Inari, so the two make a good pair; see our Fushimi Inari guide.

Nada, between Kobe and Nishinomiya. Nada is said to produce about 30 percent of Japan's sake, with roughly 40 breweries across five villages. Its breweries use the mineral-rich miyamizu water from Mount Rokko, credited with the region's characteristic dry sake. Hakutsuru has a free museum a short walk from Hanshin Sumiyoshi Station.

Manners and Tips楽しむコツ

  • Drink at the brewery, not the shrine. Tasting rooms and restaurants are the place for sake. On shrine grounds, join in only when a cup is offered at a ritual.
  • Do not touch the barrels. They are offerings, so photograph them from the path.
  • Try a tasting machine. Tasting halls such as Ponshukan at Echigo-Yuzawa Station in Niigata let you buy a medal and pour a small cup from a machine, a fun way to compare many sakes.
  • Mind the age. The legal drinking age in Japan is 20.
  • Go in the cold months. New sake season runs through winter, and green cedar balls go up around February or March.
  • Check before you go. Tour languages, booking rules and opening days change, so look at each official page.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Why is sake offered to the gods in Shinto?
Sake is offered to the kami as a gift and then shared with worshippers after rituals and festivals. Shrine sake is called miki or omiki, and it is seen as a way to bridge the gap between people and the kami.
What is the cedar ball hanging outside some sake breweries?
It is called a sugidama or sakabayashi, a ball of cedar leaf tips hung from the eaves. It is said to have started at Omiwa Shrine in Nara, and it is usually hung in late winter to show that the new sake is ready. It starts bright green and slowly turns brown as the sake matures.
Which shrine is linked to sake brewers?
Omiwa Shrine in Nara is worshipped as a god of sake brewing, and brewers from across Japan gather there each year on November 14. In Kyoto, Matsunoo Taisha is also visited by sake brewers, who pray for success.
Can I drink sake at a shrine?
Drinking on the grounds is generally not welcome, so enjoy it at a brewery tasting room or a restaurant instead. Some shrines serve a sip of omiki at festivals or after a ritual, and you can join in when it is offered to you. The legal drinking age in Japan is 20.
Where can I visit sake breweries near famous shrines?
The Fushimi district in Kyoto and the Nada area between Kobe and Nishinomiya are the two best-known choices. Gekkeikan in Fushimi has a sake museum, and Nada is said to produce about 30 percent of Japan's sake. Check each brewery's official page for tour languages and booking rules.

One Cup for the Godsむすびに

Next time you pass a stack of barrels at a shrine, think of it as a thank-you note from a brewery to the gods. Then follow it to Fushimi or Nada and taste what the offering was made from. Prayers first, sake after, and the order feels just right. If you have a sweet tooth too, our guide to shrine and temple sweets is the perfect partner.
