Omiya Hikawa Shrine: The Shrine That Named a City

武蔵一宮 氷川神社

Most shrines lend their name to a neighborhood, if anything. Omiya Hikawa Shrine lent its name to an entire city — a place of worship, by some accounts, for nearly 2,500 years, and the head of 280 shrines stretching across the Kanto plain.

Most shrines lend their name to a neighborhood, if anything. Omiya Hikawa Shrine lent its name to an entire city. "Omiya" literally means "great shrine," and the Saitama City ward that carries the name today exists because of the shrine at its center — a place worshippers have been visiting, by some accounts, for nearly 2,500 years.

Born From a Swamp and a Serpent沼と蛇神の起源

According to shrine tradition, Omiya Hikawa Shrine's origins trace back to around 473 BCE, when officials from the Izumo clan — administrators sent from what is now Shimane Prefecture to govern the newly designated Musashi Province — brought with them the worship of Susanoo-no-Mikoto, the storm god and estranged brother of the sun goddess Amaterasu. Susanoo is best known in Japanese mythology for slaying Yamata no Orochi, an eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent that had been terrorizing a village and demanding the sacrifice of its daughters. After killing the creature, Susanoo married the last intended victim, Inadahime, and the two are enshrined here together alongside their descendant Onamuchi (also known as Okuninushi), the god of nation-building and matchmaking.

The shrine's physical location reflects this watery mythology directly. The grounds sit on a low promontory that once extended into Minuma, a vast swamp that covered much of the area until the mid-Edo period. Within the precincts today, a small body of water called Ja-no-ike — the Snake Pond — is considered the shrine's literal birthplace: a natural spring that, in Japanese folk belief, would have been seen as the dwelling place of a water deity, with snakes traditionally regarded as the water god's living incarnation.

The pairing of a serpent-slaying god with a spring guarded by serpent symbolism is not coincidence so much as layered mythology — a single site where the legend of Susanoo and the practical religion of swamp-edge water worship appear to have merged into one.

Musashi's Top Shrine, Repeatedly Endorsed by Power武蔵国の筆頭神社

Hikawa Shrine first enters the written historical record in 901, in the Nihon Sandai Jitsuroku chronicle, and was already classified as a "myojin taisha" — a shrine of major rank — under the Heian-period Engishiki system. During the Heian period, the warrior Taira no Sadamori is said to have prayed here for victory during the Tengyo Rebellion against his cousin Taira no Masakado — in the very years Masakado's revolt was being suppressed elsewhere in the Kanto region. In 1180, Minamoto no Yoritomo — about to found the Kamakura shogunate — ordered his retainer Doi Sanehira to rebuild the shrine and made substantial donations of his own, cementing its status among the region's most important religious sites.

The shrine's fortunes rose again during the Edo period, when it sat within Omiya-juku, a prosperous post town on the Nakasendo, one of the two great highways linking Edo to Kyoto. But its single most consequential moment of imperial favor came in 1868, when Emperor Meiji, traveling the Nakasendo as he relocated the imperial capital from Kyoto to Tokyo, stopped specifically to worship at Hikawa Shrine. He returned in 1870 and formally designated it a chokusaisha — a shrine receiving imperial offerings directly from the throne — before it was ranked Kanpei-taisha, the highest tier of state-recognized shrines, effectively placing it above every other shrine in the Kanto region. It is this elevation, layered atop a name the area had carried since antiquity, that cemented Omiya's modern identity: a city named, quite literally, for its great shrine.

A Torii Gate's Second Life明治神宮からの鳥居

The shrine's current main buildings date to a government-funded reconstruction completed in 1940. One of the more unusual artifacts on the grounds today did not start out at Hikawa Shrine at all. In 1966, a large wooden torii gate at Meiji Jingu in Tokyo was damaged by lightning. Rather than scrap the timber, Meiji Jingu had the gate repaired — and in 1976, donated it to Omiya Hikawa Shrine, where it now stands as the shrine's Ni-no-Torii (second gate), reportedly the largest wooden torii in the Kanto region at roughly 13 meters tall.

It is a quiet, literal link between two of the region's most prominent shrines, separated by a few prefectures but joined by a single piece of repaired cedar. Visitors who have walked the forest path at Meiji Jingu and the sando at Omiya are standing, without knowing it, beneath the same wood.

The Longest Approach in Japan日本最長の参道

Few shrines in Japan can match Hikawa Shrine's sando, or approach path: a roughly 2-kilometer walk running in a straight north-south line from the old Nakasendo highway to the shrine itself, lined with some 650 trees — elms, chinquapins, and hackberries among them — passing through three successive torii gates before reaching a red bridge across Kamiike Pond and the vermilion two-story Romon gate that marks the entrance to the inner shrine grounds.

It is widely cited as the longest shrine approach in the country, and walking its full length, beneath a canopy that turns brilliant red and gold in autumn, has become as much a part of visiting Hikawa Shrine as the worship hall at the end of it. The path follows the same general route that pilgrims, post-town travelers, and Edo-era merchants would have walked for centuries — a corridor of living trees laid over an older corridor of religious purpose.

Head of 280 Shrines二八〇社の総本社

Omiya Hikawa Shrine is the head of an estimated 280 Hikawa shrines scattered primarily across the Kanto region, sharing its name, its enshrined deities, and a common thread of Susanoo worship traced back to this single site. The network reflects a pattern of religious expansion radiating outward from this original Musashi Province location as the Izumo clan's administrative and religious influence spread during the early centuries of Japanese state formation.

Among its better-known offshoots is Akasaka Hikawa Shrine in central Tokyo — a quiet, tree-lined shrine in the middle of one of the city's busiest districts, a small reminder, for visitors who encounter it first, of how far this particular branch of Shinto worship has spread from its origin point in what is now Saitama.

Visiting Today: Practical Guide実践ガイド

Getting there
The shrine is roughly a 20-minute walk from the East Exit of JR Omiya Station — approximately 30 minutes from central Tokyo by train. Alternatively, Kita-Omiya Station on the Tobu Noda Line puts you about 10 minutes away on foot, a quieter approach that brings you in from the north end of the sando.
Hours and cost
There is no admission fee to enter the shrine grounds. The grounds are generally open from early morning to evening; the inner shrine hours follow standard seasonal adjustments. During New Year's, access is around the clock for the first days of January.
The approach path
Walking the full 2-kilometer sando from the Nakasendo end adds roughly 25–30 minutes each way but is one of the best forested shrine walks near Tokyo. The path is particularly beautiful in autumn (late October to mid-November), when the canopy turns red and gold, and in early spring when cherry blossoms open in the adjacent Omiya Park.
Annual festivals
The Setsubun bean-throwing festival falls on February 3rd. The grand Reisai festival is August 1st, followed by Jinkosai the next day. The Oyu Festival (also known locally as a toka-ichi market) is held December 10th. In May, the shrine grounds host Omiya Takigi Noh — torch-lit outdoor Noh theater, one of the most atmospheric seasonal events in the region. See our Japan Festivals Guide for the broader seasonal calendar.
Combining your visit
The shrine borders Omiya Park, famous for cherry blossoms in spring. A short walk away are the Railway Museum and the Omiya Bonsai Art Museum — the world's first public museum dedicated entirely to bonsai — making the area a natural half-day or full-day combination for visitors based in Tokyo.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Omiya Hikawa Shrine free to visit?
Yes, there is no admission fee to enter the shrine grounds.
Why is the area called "Omiya"?
"Omiya" means "great shrine" in Japanese, and the name derives directly from Hikawa Shrine itself, which has stood at the center of the area since ancient times and was formally elevated to the rank of imperial shrine in the late 19th century.
What god is worshipped at Omiya Hikawa Shrine?
The shrine enshrines Susanoo-no-Mikoto, the storm god known for defeating the serpent Yamata no Orochi; his wife Inadahime; and their descendant Onamuchi (Okuninushi), associated with nation-building and matchmaking.
Is Omiya Hikawa Shrine related to other Hikawa shrines?
Yes. It is considered the head shrine of an estimated 280 Hikawa shrines across the Kanto region, including Akasaka Hikawa Shrine in central Tokyo. All share its name, its enshrined deities, and a common thread of Susanoo worship traced back to this original site in what is now Saitama.
How long is the approach to the shrine?
The sando (approach path) runs approximately 2 kilometers from the old Nakasendo highway to the shrine itself, often cited as the longest shrine approach in Japan. Walking the full length adds roughly 25–30 minutes each way and passes through three successive torii gates beneath a canopy of elms, chinquapins, and hackberries.
How do you get to Omiya Hikawa Shrine?
It's about a 20-minute walk from the East Exit of JR Omiya Station, roughly 30 minutes from central Tokyo by train, or a 10-minute walk from Kita-Omiya Station on the Tobu Noda Line.

A Shrine That History Never Lost Track Ofむすびに

What distinguishes Omiya Hikawa Shrine from many of Japan's ancient sacred sites is the continuity of its importance: founded in an era before writing, already ranked among the nation's major shrines by the Heian period, rebuilt by the Kamakura shogunate, elevated above the Kanto's entire shrine hierarchy by Meiji-era imperial decree, and still drawing over two million visitors every New Year's. The swamp that once surrounded the grounds has long since been drained, but the Snake Pond remains. The Nakasendo highway has been replaced by train tracks and prefectural roads, but the sando path still runs its full two kilometers in an unbroken line. And the torii gate that lightning struck at Meiji Jingu in 1966 still stands, repaired and relocated, marking the approach to a shrine that was here long before either of its donor's emperors were born.

For visitors based in Tokyo, the 30-minute train journey to Omiya is one of the easiest half-days in the Kanto region — a forested walk, one of Japan's most storied shrines, and a city that owes its very name to what stands at its center.
