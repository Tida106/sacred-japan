Tokyo Daijingu: Tokyo's Ise Shrine, Japan's First Shinto Wedding and Love Charms

東京大神宮

Tokyo's Ise Shrine, five minutes from Iidabashi Station.

As a licensed guide, I often tell visitors that Tokyo has a small copy of Japan's holiest shrine, and that it is tucked behind office buildings five minutes from a train station. Tokyo Daijingu was founded so that people in the capital could pray to the deities of the Grand Shrine of Ise without making the long journey. It is still called O-Ise-sama, “Tokyo's Ise Shrine,” and today it is just as well known as a place to pray for good relationships.

This guide covers the history, the deities, the shrine's claim to have begun Shinto weddings for the public, a walk through the precinct in photos, and the practical details.

How It Began由緒

According to the shrine's own history, a new shrine was built in Tokyo in 1880 with the approval of Emperor Meiji, so that people in Tokyo could worship the deities enshrined at Ise from afar (official site). At first it was called Hibiya Daijingu, after the district where it stood. After the Great Kanto Earthquake of 1923 it moved to its present site in Iidabashi, in 1928, and was renamed Iidabashi Daijingu. It took the name Tokyo Daijingu after the war; GLTJP gives 1946 for the change, and I have not seen that year on the official English page, so I treat it as a single-source date.

Tokyo Daijingu is counted among the Five Major Shrines of Tokyo (GLTJP, Tsunagu Japan), together with places such as Meiji Jingu. For the wider picture of Ise itself, see our Ise Grand Shrine guide.

The Deities and the Matchmaking御祭神

The enshrined deities are those of Ise: Amaterasu-Sume-Okami and Toyouke-no-Okami, together with Yamatohime-no-Mikoto, whom the official site calls the founder of the Inner Shrine of Ise. The shrine also honors the three deities of creation and growth, Ame-no-Minakanushi, Takamimusubi and Kamimusubi.

That last group explains the matchmaking reputation. Visit Chiyoda says these deities oversee musubi, the binding together of all things, so the shrine is known for blessings of good relationships, and people come from all over Japan to pray for them. In my own tours, I add that visitors pray here for many kinds of connection: partners, but also work and friends, as GLTJP notes.

Japan's First Shinto Wedding神前結婚式

The shrine is known as the first to hold Shinto wedding ceremonies in Japan. The story, as Walker Plus and GLTJP tell it, begins in 1900 (Meiji 33), when the wedding of the Crown Prince, later Emperor Taisho, was held before the deities in the Kashikodokoro sanctuary of the Imperial Palace. To commemorate it, Tokyo Daijingu began ceremonies of the same kind for the general public. Wikipedia and GO TOKYO date the first wedding at the shrine to 1901, so you will see both years.

Weddings are still held here today. Walker Plus and a wedding venue listing mention a celebratory dance performed by shrine maidens, the Toyohogi no Mai, said to be unique to the shrine. That detail comes from those two sources only, so I mention it as something to look for if you see a ceremony, and not as a fixed feature. If you see a bridal party, please keep your distance.

A Walk Through the Precinct境内

I will take you through in the order a visitor walks. It begins at the street.

The precinct begins at a plain wooden torii and a tall stone marker carved with the shrine's name, at the top of a short flight of steps. Behind the torii, you can already see a gate and, through it, the purple curtain of the hall. A teal banner on the left appears to refer to Ise Jingu, as I read it.

At the top of the steps stands a gate with vermilion pillars and white curtains, with red offering banners on its right. The banner on its left reads, as I read it, something like “Let us visit Ise Jingu, the home of the heart.” A small sign in front of the steps reminds visitors to purify themselves and then worship.

Purify your hands and mouth here before you go on. A bronze dragon sits at the back of the basin. The white banner on the right urges visitors to enshrine an Ise Jingu talisman, and the sign beside it appears to refer to the 62nd rebuilding of Ise Jingu in autumn 2013, so this photo seems to date from around that time. Traffic cones and blue sheets show the shrine was working on something nearby; I would not expect them now.

Here is a detail I enjoy: there are no ladles. Water runs down a row of wooden channels, and a sign gives the steps. As I read it, you wash both hands under the running water, cup water in your hand to rinse your mouth, and wash both hands again. Compare this with the routine in our shrine etiquette guide.

From inside the gate the precinct opens out: a paved path runs across a garden of dark pebbles and stepping stones to the hall, with lanterns on either side. The courtyard is small, which is part of why it feels so intimate and why it fills up quickly.

This is the approach as you walk up the path: lanterns left and right, a purple curtain at the front of the hall, and crossed finials on the ridge. The chrysanthemum crests on the curtain are the shrine's most visible emblem.

At the hall, visitors pray in front of three offering boxes under the purple curtain. The usual routine is a coin, two bows, two claps, a silent prayer and one bow; the shrine usually has a leaflet in English describing how to pray (Wanderlog reviewer, single source), so ask at the office if you are unsure.

Look up at the roof. Crossed finials (chigi) rise at the ends of the ridge, and a row of logs capped in gold (katsuogi) runs along it. These are roof details that shrine buildings are often identified by; our shrine architecture guide explains what they are.

This is the busy side of the hall, where the amulet window is, under a red awning. On a good-weather day the area fills with visitors queuing for charms and fortunes. It is the reason I recommend a weekday morning.

Through the glass doors you can see the interior: bamboo blinds with tassels, white curtains with crests, lanterns, and rows of low tables. Shinto weddings and rituals take place in a hall like this, though I cannot tell from the photo which ceremony it is set up for.

Fortunes, Charms and Emaおみくじ・お守り・絵馬

The shrine is known for its fortune slips and charms. GO TOKYO says you can draw a love fortune in English, and Japan Travel reports that the staff had boxes of English omikuji ready. Tsunagu Japan notes that the shrine has many types of fortune slip, some even by blood type. Many charms are themed around love and relationships; one travel site counts over 40, including a lily-of-the-valley design (Magical Trip, single source), so I would browse the window and see what is on offer that day. Designs and prices change.

Beside the hall, a wooden frame is hung with ema, the wooden wishing plaques. The ones here are printed with colorful illustrations, and GO TOKYO calls them colorful and quirky. Visitors stop to read what others have written. If you write one, keep your personal details off it; the plaques are on public display.

Visiting Tokyo Daijinguアクセス

  • Address: 2-4-1 Fujimi, Chiyoda City, Tokyo.
  • Hours: gate 6:00 to 21:00; charms 8:00 to 19:00; goshuin 9:00 to 17:00 (Visit Chiyoda, GO TOKYO). Please check the official site.
  • Access: about 5 minutes from Iidabashi Station: JR West Exit, Metro exit B2a (Yurakucho and Namboku Lines) or exit A4 (Tozai and Oedo Lines) (Visit Chiyoda).
  • Cost: I know of no entrance fee. Fortunes, charms and goshuin are extra, in cash.
  • Best time: a weekday morning; New Year and weekends are crowded.
  • Pair it with: Meiji Jingu for a larger shrine, or the Love Live! pilgrimage at Kanda Myojin, also in Chiyoda.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What kind of shrine is Tokyo Daijingu?
It was founded in 1880 with the approval of Emperor Meiji so that people in Tokyo could worship the deities of the Grand Shrine of Ise from afar (official site). That is why it is affectionately called Tokyo's Ise Shrine. It is also known for matchmaking, and for being the shrine that began Shinto weddings for the public.
What are the opening hours?
The gate is open 6:00 to 21:00, charms and amulets are given out 8:00 to 19:00, and goshuin (shrine stamps) are received 9:00 to 17:00 (Visit Chiyoda, GO TOKYO). Hours can change, so check the shrine's official site before you go.
Is there an admission fee?
I know of no entrance fee for the grounds. Charms, fortunes and goshuin are paid for separately, and GO TOKYO lists payment as cash, so bring coins.
Are the love fortunes available in English?
Yes. GO TOKYO says omikuji fortunes are available in English, and Japan Travel and Tsunagu Japan report the same. One writer paid 200 yen for a fortune (Japaholic), but prices and designs change, so ask at the office.
How do I get there?
About five minutes on foot from Iidabashi Station: the JR West Exit, Tokyo Metro Yurakucho or Namboku Line exit B2a, or Tokyo Metro Tozai Line or Toei Oedo Line exit A4 (Visit Chiyoda, GO TOKYO). The address is 2-4-1 Fujimi, Chiyoda City.
When is the best time to visit?
Weekday mornings are my advice, because the shrine is popular with visitors seeking good relationships and the courtyard can fill up, as in the photos above. Expect crowds at New Year and on weekends.

Before You Goむすびに

Go in on a quiet morning, purify at the water pavilion, and look up at the roof before you leave. A small shrine in the middle of the city can still feel like a long journey made short.
