Fushimi Inari Taisha

伏見稲荷大社

The Mountain That Built Itself Gate by Gate

The photographs are accurate and they are also misleading. Yes, the tunnel of vermilion gates really does look like that — a corridor of red that seems to stretch toward infinity, lit strangely even in midday shade. But the photographs cannot tell you that you are looking at a mountain that essentially built itself through a collective act of prayer spanning more than a thousand years, or that every gate you walk through has a donor's name and a date inscribed on the back, or that the foxes sitting at every altar are white because that is the color of something that cannot normally be seen. Understanding those things does not make the gates less impressive. It makes them considerably more so.

Founded Before Kyoto Was the Capital京都より古い歴史

Fushimi Inari Taisha (伏見稲荷大社) was formally founded in 711 CE by the Hata clan, an influential immigrant group from the Korean Peninsula. That date matters: Fushimi Inari has ancient origins, predating the capital's move to Kyoto in 794. The shrine was already an established place of worship for generations before the city around it existed.

The Hata clan were agricultural specialists — engineers, in effect, who had introduced advanced farming and weaving techniques to the region. Their technical expertise in agriculture and their close ties to the Imperial Court allowed the Inari deity to transition from a private clan tutelary to a major national protector of the harvest and the state.

The founding legend has two versions. In one, a Hata clan elder named Irogu was practicing archery using rice cakes as targets. One arrow struck clean through a cake, which transformed into a white dove; following the bird, Irogu found rice growing on the mountain's summit and understood it as a divine sign. In the other, Empress Genmei instructed the Hata clan to enshrine three local deities on three peaks of Mount Inari on the first Day of the Horse in the second month of 711, following a series of bad harvests across the region.

Whether the founding was a private vision or an imperial command, the result was the same: a shrine at the summit of a 233-meter mountain that would become the most important center of Inari worship in Japan.

The earliest structures were built in 711 on the Inariyama hill, but the shrine was re-located in 816 on the request of the monk Kukai. Kukai — the founder of Shingon Buddhism in Japan, later known as Kobo Daishi — reportedly encountered an old man carrying rice on Mount Inari and understood it as a meeting with the deity himself. He made Inari the guardian of To-ji temple in Kyoto, the center of his new school of Buddhism, and in doing so brought Inari worship into the heart of the court. The main shrine structure was built in 1499.

What Inari Actually Is稲荷とは何か

Dedicated to Inari, the deity of a good harvest and success in business, Fushimi Inari Taisha is the head of all of Japan's Inari shrines. There are five deities formally enshrined here, though Inari Okami (稲荷大神) is understood as the primary presence — a deity whose name derives from the word for rice seedling and whose domain has expanded, over thirteen centuries, from agriculture to commerce, industry, and general prosperity.

During the Edo Period, Inari took on an association with commerce and thus came to represent wealth and prosperity. This developed further as Japan's economy grew and life became more commercialized so that, by the Meiji Period, Inari was considered the kami of finance and industry. This flexibility is central to Inari's enduring popularity: worshippers today may pray for a good harvest, for business success, for traffic safety, or for any personal wish. The deity adapts.

The Foxes稲荷の狐

The white fox statues standing sentinel at every altar and sub-shrine throughout the mountain are not Inari. They are Inari's messengers. Fox statues (kitsune, 狐) found throughout the shrine grounds are believed to be messengers of Inari. Many hold symbolic objects in their mouths, such as keys (to rice granaries) or scrolls.

The foxes are rendered in white specifically because foxes in Japanese folk belief are ordinarily invisible to human eyes — creatures that exist between the ordinary world and the divine one. A white fox statue is a representation of something that cannot, in principle, be depicted accurately.

Look closely at the pairs of foxes at each altar: they are never identical. One may carry a key, another a jewel, another a scroll. Some clutch a sheaf of rice. Each object encodes a specific aspect of Inari's protection:

  • A key (鍵) — the key to the rice granary
  • A scroll (巻物) — wisdom and divine knowledge
  • A jewel (宝珠) — divine spirit, the ability to grant wishes
  • A sheaf of rice (稲) — the harvest itself

The Gates鳥居の由来

To express gratitude to the deity of the shrine, visitors traditionally donate a torii gate whenever their wish comes true or when making a prayer for prosperity. This tradition has been carried out over several centuries now, and as a result the shrine has accumulated countless gates.

During the Edo period, merchants and businesses began donating torii gates to the shrine as a gesture of gratitude or prayer for prosperity. Over time, this tradition created the thousands of vermilion gates that now line the mountain trails. Today there are roughly 10,000 torii on the mountain in total. The densest section — the Senbon Torii (千本鳥居), "thousand gates" — consists of approximately 800 gates placed so closely together they form a tunnel, beginning just behind the main hall.

On the back of every torii gate you will see the name of the donator and the date they made their contribution. Depending on the size, it can cost between 180,000 to 1,300,000 yen for each gate. The donor names range from major corporations to small family businesses to individuals. Reading the backs of the gates as you walk is a slow-motion record of commercial prayer spanning the entire modern period of Japanese history.

The two-story entry gate (romon, 楼門) was built by Toyotomi Hideyoshi in 1589 — a donation from one of the most powerful men in Japan at the moment of his greatest ambition, made to a shrine his family had long venerated.

The Mountain稲荷山

The shrine complex does not end at the main buildings. Behind the romon gate and the honden, the entire mountain is sacred ground. A network of trails leads through sub-shrines, rock altars called otsuka (お塚), and smaller gate tunnels for roughly four kilometers to the summit at 233 meters. The full hike takes about two to three hours.

About halfway up, the Yotsutsuji (四ツ辻) intersection offers the mountain's best views over southern Kyoto, along with benches, vending machines, and small teahouses. Many visitors turn back here. Above Yotsutsuji, the gates thin out, the forest closes in, and the crowds largely disappear. The upper mountain has no dramatic view at the summit — only trees, moss-covered altars, incense, and a quiet that the photographs never prepare you for.

The shrine is open 24 hours, which makes it genuinely possible to visit at dawn or after dark. Early morning — particularly before 8am — is when the light through the gates is most striking and the approach least crowded.

Visiting Today参拝の実際

Fushimi Inari Taisha is free to enter and open at all hours. It is located directly in front of JR Inari Station on the JR Nara Line, five minutes from Kyoto Station (¥150). Keihan Fushimi-Inari Station is a short walk away for visitors coming from Gion or eastern Kyoto.

If you want a deeper understanding of the mountain while you walk it — the history behind each sub-shrine, the meaning of the fox objects, and the correct etiquette at each altar — guided tours of Fushimi Inari are available from Kyoto Station and typically run two to three hours.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Fushimi Inari Taisha free to visit?
Yes, entry to the shrine grounds and mountain trails is completely free. There is no admission fee at any point on the mountain.
How many torii gates are there at Fushimi Inari?
Approximately 10,000 torii gates line the trails across the mountain. The densest section, Senbon Torii, contains around 800 gates placed in close parallel rows to form a tunnel just behind the main hall.
Who donated the torii gates?
Each gate was donated by an individual or company in gratitude for, or in prayer for, prosperity — a tradition that began spreading during the Edo period (1603–1868). The donor's name and the date of donation are inscribed on the back of each gate.
What do the fox statues represent?
The foxes (kitsune) are Inari's divine messengers, not the deity itself. They are depicted in white because foxes in Japanese folk belief are normally invisible to human eyes. Many carry symbolic objects: a key represents the rice granary; a scroll represents wisdom; a jewel represents divine spirit.
How long does it take to hike Mount Inari?
The full circuit to the summit (233 meters) and back takes approximately two to three hours. The Yotsutsuji viewpoint, roughly halfway up, makes a satisfying one-hour round trip for visitors who prefer a shorter walk.
When was Fushimi Inari Taisha founded?
The shrine was formally founded in 711 CE — more than 80 years before Kyoto became Japan's capital — by the Hata clan, an immigrant group that had brought advanced agricultural techniques to the region.
