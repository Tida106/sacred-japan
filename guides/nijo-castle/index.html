Nijo Castle: Nightingale Floors and the Last Shogun

二条城

The shogun's palace in Kyoto, where the floors sing and Japan's last shogun gave up his power.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I tell guests that Nijo Castle is the castle that is not really a castle. There is no tall keep to climb. What you walk through is a shogun's palace, and in the Ninomaru Palace the floors chirp underfoot, the walls are covered in gold-leaf paintings, and in 1867 the last shogun announced that he was giving power back to the emperor.

This guide covers who built it, what to look for inside, what happened in 1867, the Honmaru Palace and gardens, and the practical details, including a ticket price change that has been reported.

Ieyasu's Kyoto Residence歴史

According to japan-guide, Tokugawa Ieyasu, the first shogun of the Edo period, built Nijo Castle in 1603 as his residence in Kyoto. His grandson Iemitsu finished the palace buildings in 1626 and added a five-story keep. Wikipedia describes two rings of walls and moats around about 27.5 hectares, with the Honmaru (inner ward) inside and the Ninomaru (outer ward) around it.

The place is deliberately grand. Kyoto was the emperor's city, and the Tokugawa wanted a base there that showed who held real power. I find that helps guests make sense of the scale of the gates and the gold. The keep itself burned after a lightning strike in 1750, according to Wikipedia, which is why you will not find one today.

The defenses are still easy to read on the ground. In this rainy April 2011 photo, a still moat reflects pines, and the stone walls on the right rise at a steep slope, built from large, tightly fitted blocks. It is a quiet, gray view, and a good reminder that the "palace" at Nijo sits inside a fortification.

Inside the Ninomaru Palace二の丸御殿

The Ninomaru Palace is the main reason to come. Wikipedia describes six connected buildings, mostly of hinoki cypress, with gold leaf, carvings and wall paintings by the Kano school, and notes that all six are National Treasures. japan-guide adds the tatami rooms, decorated ceilings and painted sliding doors. You walk through in your socks along the corridors.

Then there are the famous nightingale floors. The corridors chirp as you walk on them. The story most guides tell is that they were an alarm against intruders. Wikipedia says the sound actually comes from clamps rubbing against nails in the beams under the floor. Either way, the sound is lovely, and I tell guests to walk slowly and listen. Photography inside the palace is not allowed, so the memory has to do the work.

Look up before you go in. The end of the roof is a triangle of lattice-work wood, framed in green-patinated metal with round chrysanthemum-shaped fittings and carved scrolls. It is the kind of detail that is easy to miss when you are watching your feet on the corridors, and it shows the same instinct as the gold inside: even the roofline was meant to impress.

Not everything at Nijo is gold leaf. This April 2009 photo, which Wikimedia Commons places in the palace kitchens, shows a tall ikebana arrangement of blossoming branches, pine and bamboo set against dark timber, with three koto laid out on a red carpet in front. It is a good picture of the other side of the castle: the plain, dark working buildings are the backdrop, and the color comes from whatever is placed in them. Photography rules inside the palace buildings can differ by area and event, so follow the signs on the day.

This is what the gold-leaf rooms look like. Wikimedia Commons describes the photo as a room inside the Ninomaru Palace, taken in April 2018. Every wall is a gold ground with pines painted in blue-green and brown, white birds that look like cranes fly across the lower panels at the far end, and the ceiling is a grid of coffered panels, each one decorated. The tatami floor runs the whole length. The extra lights along the floor suggest a special arrangement on that day, so please do not count on photographing inside: the usual rule is no photography in the palace, and the signs on the day decide.

1867: The Room Where the Shogunate Ended大政奉還

The most important thing that ever happened at Nijo is a decision, not a battle. Wikipedia says that in 1867 Tokugawa Yoshinobu, the last shogun, declared the return of political authority to the Imperial Court in the Ninomaru Palace. That ended about 260 years of Tokugawa rule, and it is why the same castle that opened in 1603 as a symbol of shogunal power is also where the era closed.

After that, japan-guide says the castle was used for a time as an imperial palace. Wikipedia says it was donated to the city of Kyoto in 1939 and opened to the public in 1940. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994, according to japan-guide.

Honmaru and the Gardens本丸と庭園

The Honmaru is the inner ward. The stone base of the former keep is still there, and japan-guide says it offers views over the grounds. The Honmaru Palace, built in 1893 and 1894 according to Wikipedia, is a later building that was moved from an imperial residence in Kyoto. japan-guide says it reopened to the public in September 2024, and that it needs an advance reservation and a separate fee.

This photo, taken from above in August 2013, shows how different the Honmaru Palace looks from the Ninomaru. Wikimedia Commons describes it as one of the buildings of the Honmaru Palace. The roofs are layered and gray-tiled, the lower walls are dark timber, and the upper story is paneled in plain wood, with a gabled end carrying a small carved ornament. There is no gold in sight, and the restrained look is exactly why I like to compare the two palaces on the same visit. Note that the picture is more than a decade old, so details may have changed since the palace reopened in 2024.

The Ninomaru Garden has a large pond, ornamental stones and pines, and is the view you see from the palace rooms. In the north of the grounds, Wikipedia lists Seiryu-en, a garden built in 1965 with two tea houses and more than a thousand stones. japan-guide lists the seasons: plum blossoms from late February to early March, cherry blossoms from late March through April, and autumn leaves in the second half of November.

This is the view the garden was built to give. The pond is ringed with boulders and pines, a small waterfall sits at the far end, and the long tiled roof of the Ninomaru Palace comes in from the right. The Commons description lists the garden as a Special Place of Scenic Beauty. Walk the path beside the palace and the same scene keeps changing, which is why I suggest leaving time for the garden after the interior.

The same pond in September looks completely different. In this 2015 photo the pines and broadleaf trees are full and deep green, the water reflects them almost like a mirror, and a few visitors walk along the path beside the palace on the right, which gives a good sense of scale. If you cannot come in spring or autumn, the garden still works in summer, mostly as a study in greens and stone.

Quieter corners reward a slower walk. This rainy-day photo looks down on a bed of bright green moss, with a maple whose leaves have turned orange and red, a pale path curving through, and a stone retaining wall at the right edge. Commons dates the photo to March 2014, so I would treat it as a mood picture of the garden in wet weather rather than a forecast of November color. For the autumn timing, rely on the second half of November mentioned above.

This is the color people come for. A single maple blazes crimson and orange in front of a pale stone wall and the moat, with pines framing it on the left. Commons dates the photo to December 1, 2008, which is a useful reminder that Kyoto's leaves often peak late, so a visit in the second half of November or early December can still catch them, though the timing shifts from year to year.

In early April the grounds turn white. These cherry trees stand over an open lawn and a paved path, and behind them you can see the pink of weeping cherries, so different varieties bloom in the same area. The photo shows ropes and lights set up along the lawn, a sign that the castle is prepared for visitors in blossom season. Dates in the guide above are only a rough window, since the peak moves with each year's weather.

Visiting Nijo Castleアクセス

  • Getting there: japan-guide says Nijojo-mae Station on the Tozai Subway Line is a few steps from the entrance. From Kyoto Station it takes about 15 minutes, changing at Karasuma-Oike. City buses 9 and 50 from Kyoto Station take 15 to 20 minutes.
  • Hours: japan-guide lists 8:45 to 17:00 for the grounds, with last entry at 16:00. The Ninomaru Palace closes earlier, at 16:10. The palaces have extra closing days, including some Tuesdays in January, July, August and December, so check the calendar before you go.
  • Tickets: japan-guide lists 800 yen for the grounds and 500 yen more for the Ninomaru Palace, which makes the 1,300 yen set ticket. Honmaru Palace is an extra 1,000 yen and needs an advance reservation. A Kyoto newspaper reported on October 1, 2026 that the city plans a two-tier price from fiscal 2027, with 1,000 yen for residents and 2,000 yen for non-residents on the set ticket. It was described as a plan, not a confirmed date.
  • Time needed: about two hours, or more in cherry blossom and autumn leaf seasons.
  • Tip: japan-guide says audio guides can be rented just inside the east entrance, which helps with the palace rooms.
  • Check first: hours, closing days and prices can change. Please confirm on the official Nijo Castle website before you go.

Making It a Day in Kyoto滞在

Nijo is easy to pair with a morning in central Kyoto, since it sits on the subway line. If you are visiting in the second half of November, it is also a good place to see autumn leaves without leaving the city.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How much does Nijo Castle cost?
japan-guide lists 800 yen for the grounds, plus 500 yen for the Ninomaru Palace, which makes the 1,300 yen set ticket. Honmaru Palace needs an advance reservation and an extra 1,000 yen. A Kyoto newspaper reported in October 2026 that the city plans a two-tier price from fiscal 2027, with 2,000 yen for non-residents on the set ticket. Please confirm on the official site, because prices change.
What are the nightingale floors?
The corridor floors in the Ninomaru Palace chirp when you walk on them. They are often described as a burglar alarm, but Wikipedia says the sound comes from clamps rubbing against nails in the beams that support the floor.
What happened at Nijo Castle in 1867?
Wikipedia says Tokugawa Yoshinobu, the last shogun, announced the return of political authority to the Imperial Court in the Ninomaru Palace. The Tokugawa shogunate ended soon after.
How do I get to Nijo Castle?
japan-guide says Nijojo-mae Station on the Tozai Subway Line is a few steps from the entrance. From Kyoto Station it takes about 15 minutes, changing at Karasuma-Oike. City buses 9 and 50 from Kyoto Station take 15 to 20 minutes.
How long do I need at Nijo Castle?
I plan about two hours: roughly an hour inside the Ninomaru Palace and the rest for the gardens and Honmaru. Allow more if you add Honmaru Palace or visit in cherry blossom or autumn leaf season.

A Palace Built to Impressむすびに

Nijo is the castle I recommend to guests who want to understand how the shoguns used beauty as power. It has no tall keep, but it has gold, singing floors, and the room where it all ended.
