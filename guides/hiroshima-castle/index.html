Hiroshima Castle: The Carp Castle, the 1945 Blast and a 1958 Keep

広島城

A castle founded on a river delta, destroyed in 1945, rebuilt in 1958, with survivors in the grounds.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I tell visitors that Hiroshima Castle is where the city started. Before there was a Hiroshima, there was a lord, a river delta and a castle on flat ground, and the town grew around it. Most people come to this city for the Peace Memorial Park, which is right, and the castle is the quieter part of the same story, with a keep that was rebuilt in 1958 after the bomb.

This guide covers who built the castle, who held it, what is known about 6 August 1945, what you can actually see now, and the practical details. Where my sources disagree, I say so.

Mori, Fukushima and Asano築城と城主

Mori Terumoto built the castle on flat land in the delta of the Ota River. The sources give different dates for the start, from 1588 (GLTJP) to 1589 (MLIT and Wikipedia) and 1590 (Japan Experience), and they agree it was finished in 1599. GLTJP says Terumoto moved here from a mountain stronghold. Wikipedia and MLIT give the castle the nickname Rijo, the Carp Castle, and MLIT says it became the base for the growth of Hiroshima.

After Sekigahara in 1600, Terumoto lost the castle. Wikipedia says Fukushima Masanori held it from 1600 to 1619, and Japan Experience says he lost it after failing to report repairs made following a flood in 1619. The Asano family then governed from here, starting with Asano Nagaakira, until the end of Tokugawa rule, which MLIT counts as twelve generations. Japan Experience adds that the Mori moved to Hagi.

In the Meiji period the castle became a military facility. Wikipedia says the Imperial General Headquarters was based here during the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894 to 1895. Wikipedia and MLIT both say the original keep was designated a National Treasure in 1931.

One figure is worth a caution. GLTJP says the castle had 88 turrets, which it describes as the highest number in Japan. I found that in one source only, so treat it as a claim to check.

6 August 1945原爆

MLIT says troops were stationed at the castle near the end of the war, and that the atomic bomb leveled it on 6 August 1945, probably because the blast destroyed the lower pillars and the rest of the structure collapsed. Wikipedia says it was long believed that the whole tower was blown away, and that evidence from 2010 points to the collapse instead. I would present the cause as the best current reading, not as settled.

Hiroshima is a city where visitors come for this history, and I think the castle works best when you see it as one layer of it. The Peace Memorial Park is the place for the bombing itself, and I would not try to make the castle carry that weight.

The 1958 Keep再建天守

The present keep was completed in 1958. Wikipedia and MLIT call it a concrete replica of the original wooden keep, while GLTJP says it has a wooden top floor and reinforced concrete lower floors. Wikipedia says it is five stories, 26.6 meters above a 12.4 meter stone base, and GLTJP gives about 40 meters including the base and the shachihoko.

This is the view most visitors get: the keep seen straight on from the Honmaru, with its stacked gables and dark wooden boards, and the entrance low at the center where the ticket line forms. The red leaves on the right give the date away as late autumn. The three people walking toward the door also help show the scale, since the keep looks large but is only five stories.

Inside it is a history museum. GLTJP describes the layout floor by floor: the first floor has exhibits on the castle structure, a hands-on area and a shop, the second floor covers daily life and culture with restored folding screens, the third has armor and swords, the fourth has main keep exhibits and special exhibitions, and the fifth is an observation room with views of the city. Exhibits change, so check what is on when you go.

From a wider angle you can see the covered walkway that leads into the keep, with its red-brown posts, and the signboard beside it. I would not read much into the walkway as history, because it is a visitor facility added in front of a replica. Its main use is to show you where the museum begins.

This tall frame, taken from the square in front, shows how the roofs and gables step upward. Look at the large gable over the entrance and the smaller ones above it, which give the keep its layered look. The stone bench in the foreground is a good reminder that the square is a place to sit and take it in before you go up.

I could not confirm the exact spot from the Commons page, but the height suggests an upper level of the keep or a nearby high point. It gives a sense of where the castle sits now: a green island of trees and water in the middle of a modern city, with the broadcasting tower and apartment blocks pressing close. Please treat this as an illustration of the setting rather than a marked viewpoint.

Ninomaru, Moat and Survivors二の丸と被爆樹木

Wikipedia says the castle had three concentric moats, and that the two outer ones were filled in during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Japan Experience says only the inner moat survives. Inside it stands the Hiroshima Gokoku Shrine, which Wikipedia says was moved there after 1945.

Across the moat, the keep rises above a thick screen of trees, and the stone facing of the bank runs along the waterline. This is the best kind of day for the walk around the moat, when the water is calm and the sky is clear. The only unattractive detail is a pair of cranes at the right edge, which is a reminder that the city around the castle is always changing.

GLTJP describes the Ninomaru as an outpost guarding the Naka Gomon gate, with military buildings rather than a palace: the Omote Gomon gate, a Hira Yagura, a long Tamon Yagura of 68 meters, a Taiko Yagura, a guardhouse, a stable and a well. It says everything but the stone walls was lost in 1945 and that restoration ran from 1989 to 1994, with the gate rebuilt in 1991. Wikipedia says a gate and three turrets were rebuilt in wood in 1994. The same source puts the stone walls of the Honmaru and Ninomaru at 31,781 stones, with 214 carved marks confirmed on the north side, which is a single-source figure.

On an overcast day the castle looks quieter and darker, and the moat is carpeted with floating green plants. The broadcasting tower and apartment block on the left are reflected in the water beside the trees. I like this picture because it shows the castle as part of the working city, not as a postcard, which is how many residents see it.

Commons titles this the Main Gate of Hiroshima Castle, and it shows the reconstructed gate with its wooden bridge, the tower-like corner structure on the stone base and the long walls running away to the left. This is the entrance side of the Ninomaru that I described above. The stone corner is worth a look, because it is the part that is original, and the timber above it was rebuilt in the 1990s. Commons marks the photo as a Quality image.

Some things are older than the keep you see. Wikipedia says a concrete bunker in the Honmaru was used for the first radio broadcast after the bombing, and it lists a eucalyptus and a willow about 740 meters from the hypocenter and a holly about 935 meters away that survived. GLTJP counts four such trees at the castle and gives slightly different distances, 740 meters for the eucalyptus, 770 for the willow and 910 for two hollies, while MLIT says three trees survived. I would read the numbers as approximate and the trees as real.

Hiroshima Gokoku Shrine 広島護国神社

The shrine inside the inner moat is a working shrine, and it is worth a short stop if you are already at the castle. Gokoku shrines honor those who died in service of the country, so I would keep voices low and photos respectful. The pictures below show the approach, the main hall and some of the details that visitors notice.

Commons describes this simply as the torii of Hiroshima Gokoku Shrine. It is a large granite torii, with the shrine's name on the plaque at the top, and the main hall sits straight ahead at the end of the paved approach. The signboards and the guardian lion-dog statues on either side are the usual small details of a shrine front.

This shows how close the shrine sits to the modern city: through the torii you can see a delivery truck and a tall tower block. The stall at the left sells omikuji fortune slips, which suggests a New Year period. The vertical inscription on the right pillar appears to read as a commemoration of an enthronement ceremony in the second year of Heisei, but I could not read it with full confidence. It may be the same torii as in the previous picture, seen from the other side, but I cannot confirm that.

The water pavilion has a green copper roof and two thick pillars, and the basin is where visitors rinse their hands before approaching the hall. Behind it you can see tents being set up with red-and-white curtains and banners that read as the first shrine visit of the year. A sign on the pavilion appears to say that the night-time water supply is stopped, so please take it as a picture from a particular day.

Worshippers stand in front of the hall, while in the foreground two workers rake the gravel, which gives a sense of how much daily care a shrine needs. The large plaque at the front reads 甲辰, the sexagenary cycle name for 2024, the year of the dragon, and a dragon image hangs above the offering area. A bronze carp stands at each end of the platform.

This wide view down the paved path shows the hall in a calmer season. Two shrine maidens in red and white and a priest are sweeping in front of it, with colored streamers hanging from the eaves. Commons describes it as Hiroshima Gokoku-jinja. The two pictures of the hall show how different the same place can look in winter and in summer.

The carp that leaps up out of the waves is one of the shrine's best-known sights. The Japanese sign behind it appears to explain the old legend of a carp that climbs a waterfall at the Dragon Gate and becomes a dragon, and that fits the dragon year plaque in the earlier picture. The English notice at the bottom warns that the bronze can be hot when it is sunny, which is a practical tip.

Visiting Hiroshima Castleアクセス

  • Admission: About 370 yen for adults (GLTJP), or 360 yen (Japan Experience). GLTJP gives 180 yen for ages 65 and over and high school students, and free for junior high students and younger. Please confirm on the official site.
  • Hours (keep): 9:00 to 18:00 from March to November, last entry 17:30, and 9:00 to 17:00 from December to February, last entry 16:30 (GLTJP). Hours may change by season and for events.
  • Closed: 29 to 31 December (GLTJP).
  • Ninomaru: Free (GLTJP). 9:00 to 17:30 from April to September and 9:00 to 16:30 from October to March, closed 29 December to 2 January.
  • Getting there: Tram line 1, 2 or 6 to Kamiyacho-higashi or Kamiyacho-nishi, then about 15 minutes on foot, or a bus from the south exit of Hiroshima Station and about 8 minutes on foot. About 25 minutes on foot from the station (GLTJP).
  • Nearby: Shukkeien Garden is about 10 minutes on foot to the east. The Peace Museum and Atomic Bomb Dome are to the south in Peace Park (Japan Experience).

Making It a Trip滞在

GLTJP says the best time for cherry blossoms is late March to early April, with evening lighting on the blossoms and the castle during the season. For another castle that burned in the war and was rebuilt in concrete, see our Okayama Castle guide, and for a white castle that came through intact, our Himeji Castle guide. If you are going on to Miyajima, see our Itsukushima Shrine guide.

Commons titles this Cherry Blossoms From Hiroshima Castle, and its location data puts it at the castle, so it appears to look down over the moat from a height. Blossoming branches frame the water in the foreground, and a second row of cherry trees runs along the far bank above the stone facing. The surface of the moat looks speckled by rain, which gives the scene a soft, quiet mood. The photo carries a small watermark in the corner. It was imported to Commons from 500px, and the flowering date will shift from year to year, so please check the local forecast.

The keep is lit from below, so the white plaster and the roof edges glow against the sky and the gold streaks in the water double the effect. Commons notes the picture came from Panoramio, and the photographer's watermark is on the image. If you are staying in the city, a walk here after dinner gives you a very different castle from the daytime one. Lighting hours can change, so please check locally.

Seen through leafless branches, the lit keep looks like a glowing lantern in the dark. The photographer's comment on Commons gives a date of 20 January 2009 and a time of around 9 p.m. The bare trees are a reminder that winter lighting is quite different from the cherry blossom season, when the trees around the castle are in flower.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Hiroshima Castle original?
No. Wikipedia and a Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) multilingual sign database both say the original keep was destroyed in the atomic bombing of 6 August 1945, and that the present keep was completed in 1958. Wikipedia and MLIT call it a concrete replica, while GLTJP says the top floor is wood and the lower floors are reinforced concrete. The stone walls, moat and a few trees are what remain from before the bomb.
What happened to the castle on 6 August 1945?
Troops were stationed at the castle at the end of the war, and MLIT says the atomic bomb leveled it, probably because the blast destroyed the lower pillars and the rest collapsed. Wikipedia says it was long believed the whole tower was blown away, and that evidence from 2010 points to the same collapse. Please treat the exact mechanism as something historians still discuss.
How much does it cost, and when is it open?
GLTJP gives 370 yen for adults, 180 yen for ages 65 and over and high school students, and free for junior high students and younger. Japan Experience gives 360 yen. GLTJP lists 9:00 to 18:00 from March to November (last entry 17:30) and 9:00 to 17:00 from December to February (last entry 16:30), closed 29 to 31 December. Please confirm on the official site, https://www.rijo-castle.jp/.
Is the Ninomaru free?
GLTJP says the Ninomaru, the outer enclosure with the rebuilt gate and turrets, is free. It lists 9:00 to 17:30 from April to September (last entry 17:00) and 9:00 to 16:30 from October to March (last entry 16:00), closed 29 December to 2 January. Confirm the hours on site or on the official page.
How do I get there from Hiroshima Station?
GLTJP says to take Hiroshima Electric Railway line 1, 2 or 6 to Kamiyacho-higashi or Kamiyacho-nishi and walk about 15 minutes, or to take a bus from the south exit and walk about 8 minutes. It gives about 25 minutes on foot from the station. Japan Experience gives Kamiyacho as the nearest tram stop.
Can I combine it with the Peace Memorial Park?
Yes. Japan Experience places the castle west of Hiroshima Station, with Shukkeien Garden about 10 minutes on foot to the east, and the Peace Museum and Atomic Bomb Dome to the south in Peace Park. I would treat the park as a visit of its own, and the castle as the older layer of the city's story.

The Older Layer of the Cityむすびに

Hiroshima Castle is worth an hour or two for the story of how the city began, the Ninomaru turrets, the moat and the few trees that were there in 1945, and not for original rooms. Go knowing the keep is a 1958 rebuild, and spend the rest of the day at the Peace Memorial Park.
