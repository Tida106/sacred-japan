Kumano Kodo

熊野古道

Japan's Answer to the Camino de Santiago

There is a certificate that only a few thousand people in the world hold. To earn it, you must complete two of history's most ancient pilgrimage routes — one threading through the cedar mountains of Japan's Kii Peninsula, the other crossing northern Spain to the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela. The Kumano Kodo (熊野古道) and the Camino de Santiago are the only two pilgrimage routes on earth to have been designated UNESCO World Heritage Sites. They have been formally recognized as sister routes since 1998, and a joint credential program called the Dual Pilgrim has been in place since 2015. The symbol on the Dual Pilgrim badge combines the Camino's scallop shell with the Kumano's three-legged crow.

The parallel is genuine and not merely promotional. Both routes emerged around the 10th century. Both were walked by people of every social class, from emperors to peasants. Both experienced centuries of intense pilgrimage followed by decline, and both were revived in the late 20th century. The differences are significant too — the Kumano is steeper, shorter, and quieter; the Camino longer, more social, and on gentler terrain — but the underlying structure of a long walk through sacred geography toward a destination that matters is the same.

What the Kumano Kodo Is熊野古道とは

The Kumano Kodo is not a single path. It is a network of ancient routes crossing the Kii Peninsula — the largest peninsula in Japan, a mass of mountain ranges, deep cedar forest, hot spring valleys, and Pacific coastline occupying the southern part of Wakayama, Mie, and Nara Prefectures. All routes converge on the same destination: the Kumano Sanzan (熊野三山), the Three Grand Shrines of Kumano — Kumano Hongu Taisha (熊野本宮大社), Kumano Nachi Taisha (熊野那智大社), and Kumano Hayatama Taisha (熊野速玉大社).

The region's sacred character is older than the shrines themselves. The forests and mountains of Kumano appear in the Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan, 720 CE) as places of nature worship, associated with purification and the boundary between the living and the dead. The specific pilgrimage routes began forming during the Heian period (794–1185), when aristocrats and imperial family members started making regular journeys to the three shrines. By the Kamakura period (1185–1333), samurai were making the journey. By the Muromachi period (1333–1573), commoners were joining in such numbers that the processions were described as an "ant's pilgrimage" — an endless, slow-moving column of ordinary people moving through the mountains toward Kumano.

At its peak, Kumano drew as many as 800 pilgrims a night to inns near the shrines. The routes are estimated to have carried millions of individual pilgrimages across the centuries — a sustained intensity of sacred travel that has few parallels anywhere in the world.

The Syncretism That Made It Work神仏習合の聖地

One reason for Kumano's extraordinary cross-class, cross-sect popularity was its theological flexibility. The Kumano region developed a form of worship called Kumano Shinto (熊野神道) that blended native Shinto nature reverence with Buddhist cosmology — the three grand shrines came to be understood as manifestations of Buddhist figures simultaneously with their Shinto identities, a practice called honji-suijaku (本地垂迹). Kumano Hongu Taisha's principal deity was identified with Amida Buddha; Kumano Nachi Taisha's with the bodhisattva Senju Kannon; Kumano Hayatama Taisha's with the bodhisattva Yakushi Nyorai. This meant the pilgrimage could accommodate Buddhist monks, Shinto devotees, aristocrats seeking imperial blessing, and peasants hoping for healing and salvation, all within the same landscape and the same ritual framework.

Shugendo (修験道) practitioners — mountain ascetics who combined Buddhist and Shinto practices with demanding physical austerity in natural landscapes — also used the Kumano routes as training grounds and served as guides for pilgrims. Their presence along the paths helped make the mountains navigable and gave the pilgrimage a professional infrastructure across centuries.

The Meiji Restoration's forced separation of Shinto and Buddhism in 1868 disrupted this synthesis severely, and the "Shrine Combining Order" of 1906 drastically reduced the number of smaller shrines and oji (王子) (pilgrimage waypoint shrines) along the route. An early ecologist named Minakata Kumagusu fought to preserve the ecosystems of the region against these reductions — his work anticipates modern conservation thinking by several decades.

The Main Routes主要ルート

Nakahechi ／ 中辺路 — The Imperial Route

The most walked section today, running approximately 70 kilometers from Takijiri-oji (滝尻王子) — a small shrine at the trailhead where pilgrims have begun their journey since the Heian period — through mountain forest to Kumano Hongu Taisha. The route passes through cedar groves, over mountain passes, through tiny villages with guesthouses that exist because of pilgrimage traffic, and past hundreds of oji shrines where pilgrims historically stopped to pray. Stone-paved sections date from medieval reconstruction efforts. The full Nakahechi takes four to five days at a walking pace.

Kohechi ／ 小辺路 — The Mountain Route

Runs 70 kilometers connecting Koyasan — the mountain monastery of Shingon Buddhism — to the Kumano Sanzan, crossing three mountain passes above 1,000 meters. It is the most physically demanding of the main routes and the least walked. For pilgrims who have already visited Koyasan, it creates a logical connection between the two major pilgrimage centers of the Kii Peninsula.

Ohechi ／ 大辺路 — The Coastal Route

Follows the Pacific coastline from Tanabe south, offering sea views and beach landscapes that make it distinct from the interior forest routes. Writers and artists have historically gravitated toward the Ohechi for its scenery.

The Three Grand Shrines熊野三山

Kumano Hongu Taisha is considered the heart of the pilgrimage — its equivalent in the Camino framework would be the Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela. The current shrine stands on a hillside above the Kumano River; its predecessor occupied a sandbar at the river's confluence until a flood in 1889 destroyed most of it. The largest wooden torii gate in Japan — 33.9 meters tall — now stands on the original river site, marking where the old shrine was and visible from considerable distance.

Kumano Nachi Taisha shares its precinct with Seiganto-ji (青岸渡寺), a Buddhist temple, and overlooks Japan's highest single-drop waterfall, Nachi-no-Otaki (那智の滝), at 133 meters. The combination of shrine, temple, and waterfall in a single view — all functioning simultaneously for well over a thousand years — is one of the most visually striking religious sites in Japan.

Kumano Hayatama Taisha sits at the river mouth near the Pacific coast town of Shingu. Its grounds contain what is claimed to be a 1,000-year-old nagi tree (a type of podocarpus), whose leaves pilgrims traditionally carried as protection during the journey.

Visiting Today歩き方・アクセス

The Kumano Kodo is walkable year-round, with spring (April–May) and autumn (October–November) offering the most comfortable temperatures and best foliage. Summer is hot and humid but the waterfalls are at their fullest. Winter brings cold but emptier trails.

For those who want to walk with an expert who can explain the history of the oji shrines, the significance of the stone-paved sections, and the meaning of arrival at Kumano Hongu Taisha, guided walks on the Kumano Kodo range from single-day sections to multi-day full-route experiences with accommodation included.

BY TRAIN

Take an express train from Osaka to Kii-Tanabe (approximately 2 hours on the Kuroshio Limited Express). The Tanabe City Kumano Tourism Bureau, located next to the station, provides maps, pilgrim credentials (the stamp passport for the oji shrines), and route planning assistance for both independent walkers and guided arrangements.

TRAILHEAD

The Nakahechi trailhead at Takijiri-oji is accessible by bus from Kii-Tanabe (approximately 30 minutes). Accommodation along the route ranges from traditional guesthouses in small villages to ryokan near the shrines — book well in advance for autumn.

DAY WALKS

For those who cannot walk the full route, day sections of the Nakahechi are accessible by bus, making it possible to walk a meaningful portion — the Takijiri to Takahara section is one of the most rewarding — and return the same day to Kii-Tanabe or Osaka.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is the Dual Pilgrim program?
A formal recognition program for people who complete both the Kumano Kodo and the Camino de Santiago — the world's only two UNESCO World Heritage pilgrimage routes. Dual Pilgrims receive a special certificate and badge combining the Camino's scallop shell and the Kumano's three-legged crow (Yatagarasu, 八咫烏). Registration is available at Kumano Hongu Taisha and at the Santiago de Compostela pilgrim office.
How long does it take to walk the Kumano Kodo?
The most popular section, the Nakahechi from Takijiri-oji to Kumano Hongu Taisha, takes four to five days of walking at a comfortable pace. The full Kohechi connecting Koyasan to the Kumano Sanzan takes three to four days. Individual day sections are also walkable for those with limited time.
Do I need to be religious to walk the Kumano Kodo?
No. The pilgrimage has historically welcomed people of all backgrounds and intentions — aristocrats, warriors, commoners, and people simply seeking healing or beauty in the landscape. Today's walkers include religious pilgrims, hikers, and cultural travelers.
What are the Three Grand Shrines of Kumano?
Kumano Hongu Taisha (the pilgrimage's primary destination), Kumano Nachi Taisha (overlooking Japan's highest single-drop waterfall, Nachi-no-Otaki at 133 meters), and Kumano Hayatama Taisha (near the Pacific coast at Shingu). Together they form the Kumano Sanzan (熊野三山), the sacred center of the Kumano pilgrimage network.
How do I get to the Kumano Kodo?
Take an express train from Osaka to Kii-Tanabe (approximately two hours on the Kuroshio Limited Express), where the Tanabe City Kumano Tourism Bureau provides maps, credentials, and route information. The trailhead for the Nakahechi route, Takijiri-oji, is accessible by bus from Kii-Tanabe.
Is the Kumano Kodo harder than the Camino de Santiago?
The Kumano is shorter but steeper, with more rugged terrain and less infrastructure than the Camino Francés. The Nakahechi involves sustained climbs over mountain passes on root-covered paths. The Camino de Santiago is generally longer, on gentler terrain, with more frequent accommodation options along the route.
