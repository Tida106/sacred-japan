Takeda Castle Ruins: Japan's Machu Picchu, the Cloud Sea and the Winter Closure

竹田城跡

Stone walls on a 353-meter mountain in Asago, Hyogo: the 'castle in the sky' above the autumn cloud sea, closed from January to February.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I tell visitors that Takeda Castle is the one place where you go to see a castle that is not there. No tower, no hall, no gate survives. What remains is a long ridge of stone walls on a mountain in Asago, Hyogo, and on a cold autumn morning, with cloud filling the valley below, they look as if they are floating. That is why people call it “Japan’s Machu Picchu” and the “castle in the sky.”

This guide covers the history, the walls, when the clouds actually come, and the practical details, including a long winter closure. Where my sources disagree, I say so, and I mark claims I found in only one place.

From 1400s Fortress to 1600 Abandonment築城と廃城

The sources do not agree on the start. japan-guide says the castle was originally built in 1411. thegate12 says construction began in 1443 under Yamana Sozen and took 13 years. I would treat the founding as “the 15th century” and leave it there.

japan-guide says Toyotomi Hideyoshi conquered the castle during one of his western campaigns, and thegate12 says Akamatsu Hirohide finished the stone walls in 1590. That second detail is from one source only. Both sources say the castle was abandoned after the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, and thegate12 ties this to Akamatsu’s defeat.

After that it simply decayed for centuries. japan-guide says the ruins were restored in the 1970s and 1980s and opened to visitors. GLTJP says it is listed among Japan’s Top 100 Castles and was designated a “Lovers’ Sanctuary” in 2012.

Commons titles this only as Takeda Castle, taken on 7 May 2013. The carved characters read “Takeda-jo-seki” (Takeda Castle Ruins) with the word for historic site above and “Hyogo Prefecture” below, which is how a designated historic site is usually labelled. Behind it you can see the edge of a stone wall above the slope. It is a small thing, but it is a good reminder that the stone here is protected, so please stay on the paths.

The Walls on Mt. Kojo石垣

GLTJP puts the castle on Mt. Kojo (also called Mt. Torafusu) at 353.7 meters, and says the stone walls are almost entirely intact, following the natural terrain in a curved layout, with the keep foundation and entrances still visible. thegate12 gives the site as about 100 meters wide and 400 meters long. japan-guide describes a central fortress with three wings, on foundations that are largely intact.

What this means on the ground: you walk along the tops of the walls and between the foundations, so the shape of the castle is something you feel with your feet. Because no buildings stand, nothing blocks the view down to the town.

This view looks down from the keep platform onto the southern enclosures. The Japanese description on Commons, which I translate here, says that each gate passage has a turret base on both sides, with the gate between them under a long turret, and that the routes bend several times. You can see that zigzag in the stone: no passage runs straight. The tree with the reddish tint at the center is, I believe, a cherry not yet in bloom, which fits the April date.

Commons labels this view as the south enclosures, taken on 7 May 2013. Compare it with the April photo: the mountains are fully green and the stone is the same. The pines on the far platform and the road in the valley give a sense of how high this ridge stands above the town.

A visitor climbs a dirt path between rough stone walls toward a higher terrace. The photo was taken on 5 May 2011 at 15:47, and Commons gives only the general description of Takeda Castle. The walls are built of irregular stones fitted together rather than cut blocks, which is why they look so rugged. Because of the ridge’s shape, paths like this one run between walls rather than across open ground.

Commons says this was taken from the south enclosure and shows the keep platform; the Japanese caption calls it the tenshudai. A visitor stands on the wall edge at the left, and two more are on the top terrace, which helps with scale. The nearer walls are layered, with each terrace set back from the one below.

The description says this was taken from the third enclosure (the sannomaru). From here the keep platform looks like a stepped pyramid of stone, with leafless cherry trees on the terraces in mid-April. It gives a good idea of how the castle rises in layers up the ridge.

Taken on 5 May 2011 at 16:10, this shows the walls from a distance, with several tiny visitors on the top that show how large the stone work is. In spring the ridge is green, so the walls stand out against the trees. I do not know exactly where it was taken from, so please treat it as an example of the view from outside.

The Sea of Clouds雲海

japan-guide says the best season is October and November, around sunrise (roughly 6:00 to 6:30), and that a sea of clouds appears on about one morning in three on average. thegate12 says September to November; GLTJP says autumn through early winter. These are not guarantees, and cold, calm, clear mornings after a humid day are what people hope for.

The photo at the top of this page shows the kind of morning people come for: a sea of clouds filling the valley at sunrise, with the stone terraces and pines standing above it. Commons gives only the date, 5 December 2010 at 07:11, and does not say where it was taken. Conditions like these are the exception, not a promise.

The classic view is from Ritsuun-kyo, on the slope opposite the castle. japan-guide says the lower decks are a few steps from the parking lot, while viewpoints 1 and 2, which have the best view, are 10 to 40 minutes up a steep dirt trail. It lists a 300-yen environmental cooperation fee, and says there is no public transport there; by car it is about 10 minutes from the town center. Please wear shoes you trust on dirt in the dark.

One practical point: the ruins open early in the cloud season (5:00 in japan-guide and GLTJP, 4:00 in thegate12), so you can be on the walls near dawn. I would still choose one morning at Ritsuun-kyo for the famous photo and another inside the ruins, if you can spare both.

The source title on Commons says this was taken at Ritsuun-kyo, the viewpoint opposite the castle, on 16 November 2011 at 08:08. The cloud here is thin and moving, not a flat sea. The hillside below is turning yellow and orange, and the sun lights the walls. If you hope for the classic cloud-sea photo, remember that you may also get a morning like this one, which has its own beauty.

Commons gives this as the Takeda Castle site in winter with a sea of clouds, taken on 24 December 2011, and lists a camera position on the ruins. The walls and terraces have a thin cover of fresh snow and a few visitors are on the walls. This is the short winter-hours season in my sources (about 10:00 to 15:00 from early December, and closed from 4 January), so check the dates first if snow is what you are after.

Spring on the Walls春の桜

The ruins are not only a cloud-season destination. The photos below, all from Commons, show cherry trees growing on the terraces among the stone. I do not have a source for the number of trees or the usual peak dates, so I will only describe what the pictures show and say that the bloom changes each year.

On Commons the file is titled as the castle in full cherry bloom, taken on 13 April 2019. Cherry trees grow around the terraces and the paved path winds between them, with visitors strolling in the sun. The town and river are far below. This is one example of how things looked on one day, because the bloom varies from year to year.

Taken the same day, this shows the trees close up beside a rope-fenced stone wall, with visitors resting on benches in the shade. Small solar panels on stands are visible along the ropes. I do not know what they power, so I will not guess. The photo shows how the blossoms and the old stone share the same space.

The same photographer on the same day, now looking down the valley, with one pink tree in front and the grass still dry and golden. The valley floor is a long way below, which helps explain the 30 to 40 minute climb from the station.

Photographed on 17 April 2022, this shows cherry trees with a reddish tint and few open flowers. It is not full bloom, and orange cones mark off an area on the grass. Together with the 2019 photos it shows how much the timing can differ, so I would not plan a trip around one fixed date.

Visiting Takeda Castle Ruinsアクセス

  • Admission: 500 yen for adults; junior high and younger free (japan-guide, GLTJP, thegate12). Cash only (GLTJP, thegate12).
  • Hours (sources differ, please confirm officially): japan-guide gives March to May 8:00 to 18:00, June to mid-September 6:00 to 18:00, mid-September to early December 5:00 to 17:00, and early December to 3 January 10:00 to 15:00. GLTJP gives 8:00 to 17:00 in March to May, 5:00 to 17:00 from June to 6 December, and 10:00 to 15:00 from 7 December to 3 January. thegate12 gives different closing times and says 4:00 opening from September. Last entry is 30 minutes before closing.
  • Closed: 4 January through the end of February (all three sources). thegate12 writes “lat February,” which looks like a typo, so please confirm the reopening date.
  • On foot from JR Takeda Station: about 40 minutes up a steep trail (japan-guide, GLTJP), 30 minutes in thegate12. It can be slippery after rain.
  • Tenku Bus: from Takeda Station to the Takedajo-seki stop in about 20 minutes, 300 yen one way or 500 yen for a day pass, roughly one per hour, not in winter; then about 20 minutes on foot to the entrance (japan-guide).
  • Taxi: about 15 minutes and 2,000 yen to the trailhead, then about 20 minutes on foot (japan-guide).
  • Ritsuun-kyo: no public transport; about 10 minutes by car or 45 minutes on foot from the town center. The free Yamajiro no Sato parking lot is halfway up, and the bus from there to the trailhead is 200 yen one way (japan-guide).
  • Facilities: restrooms only at the admission booth; no smoking on the trails or in the ruins (GLTJP).
  • Address and phone: 169 Wadayama-cho Takeda, Asago-shi, Hyogo; 079-672-4003 (thegate12). Volunteer guides in Japanese are listed at 2,000 to 3,000 yen (thegate12, single source).

Making It a Trip滞在

The cloud season rewards staying in or near Asago the night before, because the best light is before 7 a.m. If you like the idea of ruins that are mostly stone, our Bitchu Matsuyama Castle guide covers the other famous castle that floats above clouds, and our Hagi Castle ruins guide covers a castle site that its own lords tore down.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

When is the best time to see the sea of clouds at Takeda Castle?
japan-guide gives October and November, around sunrise (roughly 6:00 to 6:30), and says a sea of clouds appears on about one morning in three on average. thegate12 gives September to November, and GLTJP says autumn through early winter. Nothing is guaranteed, so I suggest staying more than one night if the view is the point.
Is Takeda Castle open in winter?
Only partly. All three sources I used say the ruins are closed from 4 January through the end of February. Early December to 3 January is a short-hours season (10:00 to 15:00 in japan-guide and GLTJP; thegate12 gives slightly different times). Please check the official Asago site for the exact reopening date.
How much does it cost to enter Takeda Castle?
Adults pay 500 yen; junior high students and younger are free (japan-guide, GLTJP, thegate12). Credit cards are not accepted at the entrance (GLTJP, thegate12), so bring cash. japan-guide adds a 300-yen environmental cooperation fee for the Ritsuun-kyo viewpoint.
How do I get to Takeda Castle Ruins?
From JR Takeda Station you can walk up in about 40 minutes (japan-guide, GLTJP; thegate12 says 30). The Tenku Bus runs from the station to the Takedajo-seki stop in about 20 minutes (300 yen one way, roughly hourly, not in winter), followed by a walk of about 20 minutes to the entrance. A taxi takes about 15 minutes to the trailhead for around 2,000 yen (japan-guide).
Is the view from inside the ruins or from across the valley?
Both are worth it, but they are different. The famous cloud-sea photo is taken from Ritsuun-kyo, a viewpoint on the opposite slope, because from there you see the whole ridge and the walls. Inside the ruins you walk on the walls themselves and look out over the town.
Is anything of the castle still standing?
No buildings remain. What survives is the stone: GLTJP says the walls are almost entirely intact, following the contours of the mountain, and that the keep foundation and entrances are still visible.

A Castle in the Skyむすびに

Takeda Castle is worth the early alarm. Plan for a morning rather than an afternoon, check the dates before you commit to winter, and let the stone do the talking.

The buildings are gone. The walls are still holding up the sky.
