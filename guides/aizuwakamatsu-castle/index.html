Aizuwakamatsu Castle: The Red-Roofed Keep, the 1868 Siege and the Byakkotai

会津若松城

A rebuilt keep with its historic red roof tiles, besieged for a month in 1868, a short bus ride from Aizuwakamatsu Station.

As a licensed guide, I tell visitors that Tsuruga Castle in Aizuwakamatsu is the castle where the samurai era ended in practice. In the autumn of 1868 the lords of Aizu stayed loyal to the shogunate, and the imperial forces came for them. The castle was bombarded for about a month and then surrendered. Today it is the only keep in Japan I know of with a red roof, and a short walk from the keep you can look across to the hill where a group of teenage boys ended their lives.

This guide covers the history, the siege, what is inside the keep and the tea house, the cherry blossoms, and the practical details. Where my sources disagree, I say so, and I mark claims I found in only one place.

From Ashina to Matsudaira蘆名氏から松平氏へ

The sources give different starting points. Fun Japan and the Fukushima tourism page say the first fortification was built in 1384 by Ashina Naomori, and that the place was then called Kurokawa Castle. Date Masamune took it in 1589. Fun Japan says Gamo Ujisato received it in 1592 and renamed it Tsuruga; the Fukushima page says Date was summoned by Hideyoshi the following year, which would be 1590, and GLTJP says only “late 16th century.” So please read the date of Gamo’s arrival as roughly 1590 to 1592.

GLTJP says Gamo built a full-scale castle with stone walls and a keep and renamed the town Wakamatsu, and that the name Tsuruga may come from his childhood name, Tsuruchiyo, although it notes there are several theories. The Edo Legacy page gives 1593 for the building and says the keep was said to have had seven stories, and that after a major earthquake in 1611 it was replaced by a five-story keep. I found that in only one source, so I treat it as a claim to check.

In 1643 the castle passed to Hoshina Masayuki, who took the Matsudaira name; Nippon.com calls him a son of the shogun Tokugawa Hidetada, and says his descendants ruled until 1868. That family tie explains a lot about what happened next.

One detail I like is the roof. Nippon.com says the keep originally had black walls and black tiles, and that red iron-glazed tiles were adopted in 1648 because the harsh winters made ordinary tiles crack. The Edo Legacy page also says the glaze resists freezing. I found the year 1648 only at Nippon.com.

The 1868 Siege会津戦争

The Boshin War began in January 1868, and GLTJP says the fighting reached Aizu in August. Nippon.com says the ninth lord, Matsudaira Katamori, was loyal to the shogunate, and that imperial forces invaded the domain. About 5,000 samurai and their families took refuge in the castle (Nippon.com). GLTJP says the siege began on 22 August and the castle held out under bombardment for more than a month, earning a reputation as an “impregnable great castle.”

As allies surrendered one by one, Katamori surrendered unconditionally to spare the people of the domain (Nippon.com). The castle was badly damaged. In 1874 the new Meiji government had the buildings demolished, and only the stone walls and the grounds remained (GLTJP, Fun Japan, Nippon.com).

The Byakkotai, the “White Tiger Corps,” are the part of the story most visitors know. GLTJP calls them teenage boys, sons of Aizu clan soldiers. Nippon.com says about 20 young samurai fled to Mount Iimori during the siege, saw the town burning, believed the castle had fallen, and chose to die rather than be captured. Numbers and details vary in other accounts, so I keep to what these two sources say. From the keep you can see Mount Iimori, which is why this view often goes quiet.

The Red-Roofed Keep赤瓦の天守

The keep you see today was rebuilt in reinforced concrete in 1965, and Nippon.com says it was funded by citizen donations. It has five levels and six stories (Nippon.com). The roof, however, is worth a second look. The 1965 rebuild used black tiles, and in 2011 the roof was refurbished with red ones to match the historical appearance (GLTJP, Nippon.com, Edo Legacy, Fun Japan). The Edo Legacy page says this red roof is the only one of its kind among Japanese castle keeps, which is a claim I could not check elsewhere.

This is the keep as it looked before the roof was changed back. Commons titles it “Tenshukaku of Aizuwakamatsu castle,” and the photographer has released it into the public domain. The roof tiles are dark grey, which matches the sources that say the 1965 rebuild used black tiles and that red ones returned in 2011. The red railing around the top-floor observation deck is already there. Compare this with the 2022 photo at the top of the page to see the difference the roof makes.

Commons gives only an upload date of 21 April 2010 for this one, and the colours are heavily processed, so please read the sky as an effect rather than a typical evening. What I can take from it is the setting: bare trees in front, a mossy stone wall, a few banners, and the keep with its older dark roof above. At 720 pixels wide it is a small file, so I show it small.

Inside is the Wakamatsu Castle Keep Local History Museum. The mytabi Fukushima page says the interior was renovated in March 2004 as a “castle museum.” Nippon.com lists exhibits on the castle’s lords and local figures, including the bacteriologist Noguchi Hideyo, replicas of samurai helmets and a stone-walled storage area under the keep where large amounts of salt were kept. The top floor is an observation deck with views of the city and the Aizu region (Nippon.com, Fun Japan). The Edo Legacy page also mentions AR experiences that bring the lost palace to life, which I have not seen myself.

Several small buildings were rebuilt using Edo-period drawings. GLTJP names the Hishiki-yagura (a storage turret) and the Minami-hashirinagaya (weapons storage). Nippon.com names the Kurogane Gate, the Hoshii Turret and the south barracks, built with Edo-period drawings and photographs from the 1870s, and says a digital art installation in the Hoshii Turret was on hold until the end of September 2026. Since the two lists differ, please look at the signs on site.

Commons titles this “Iron Gate of Tsuruga-jo Castle,” with the Chinese label 鶴之城鐵門. It is a gate with a covered passage and small shuttered windows above it, set between a white wall and a sloping stone base. The stone base is made of large, closely fitted blocks. It was taken in October 2007, so the details may have changed since.

Commons calls this simply “Aizuwakamatsu Castle” and does not name the gate, so I describe only what I can see. A covered gate with shuttered windows frames the view, a steep stone stair runs up at the left, and the keep stands out above the tiled roofs behind it. The red railing on the top floor is a useful guide for finding the keep from anywhere in the grounds. The photographer lists both a GNU licence and CC BY-SA 3.0; I use the Creative Commons one.

Taken from above in July 2022, this shows what the top floor gives you. Long tiled roofs run below, and a white two-storey turret stands at the end of one of them. At the left is the open lawn of the main citadel, with visitors on the paths. Beyond the trees the town stretches to the hills. Commons does not name the buildings, so I do not label them. Notice that the new roof tiles look reddish-brown from here.

Rinkaku, Cherry Trees and the View of Iimori麟閣と桜

Nippon.com says the park covers 22.8 hectares, and the Edo Legacy page says it is a designated National Historic Site. In the main citadel stands the Rinkaku tea house. GLTJP links it with Shoan, the son of the tea master Sen no Rikyu. Nippon.com says it was moved to save it from demolition and reassembled in the park, in a thatched building with a Japanese garden. Fun Japan says it is an Important Cultural Property of Fukushima Prefecture, and that tea ceremonies are held daily. Prices differ slightly between sources (see below).

Commons titles this “Rinkaku Japanese Tea Room at Tsuruga-jō,” so it was taken in the tea-house area. It shows a small open shelter with a bench, a path of stepping stones across bright green moss, a stone lantern at the left and a pine at the right. This is the kind of small, quiet garden detail that is easy to miss from the main path. It is a garden view, not the inside of the tea room.

Another picture from the same visit and the same Commons title. A little wooden gate with a moss-covered roof and bamboo fencing stands over a stepping-stone path, and a low building can be seen behind it. Gates like this mark the move from one part of a tea garden to the next, though I cannot say which gate this is.

Commons describes this as the tea room Rinkaku inside Tsuruga Castle Park, taken on 22 August 2011. A small gate with a moss-covered roof, edged with split bamboo, stands at the left, and a bamboo fence with black rope lashings runs along the garden. Behind a rounded shrub, a low building with ochre walls and paper-screen windows is partly hidden by the trees. A small sign stands at the right, but I cannot read it well enough to say what it says. It is a good picture of how much of Rinkaku is in the garden, not only in the tea house itself.

About 1,000 cherry trees surround the castle (GLTJP, Edo Legacy), and the park is on Japan’s Top 100 cherry blossom list (Edo Legacy). GLTJP says the trees are lit up during the blossom season. Fun Japan calls April the best time for a visit.

Commons describes this as the cherry blossom at Tsuruga Castle in Aizuwakamatsu, taken at 6:09 in the morning on 24 April 2017. From above you can see how the white walls and the red-brown roof of the keep sit inside a ring of trees, with the town and the hills behind. Several white tents are set up on the lawn. The date shows that in 2017 the trees were in bloom in late April, though the timing changes each year.

Commons gives the date as 30 April 2010. The keep glows white in the dark, framed on both sides by heavily lit cherry branches. This matches GLTJP’s statement that the trees are illuminated during the blossom season. The roof here is the older dark one, since the red tiles came in 2011.

The same photographer and date as the picture above, from a lower angle. A mossy stone wall fills the lower left, lit warm, while blossoms hang over the top and the keep rises behind with its red railing. A sign at the lower right appears to be an exhibition notice, but I cannot read it well enough to say more.

Commons titles this “Ttsuruga-jō / Aizuwakamatsu Castle” and does not name the bridge, so I only describe what I can see. A red-lacquered bridge with gold-topped posts runs straight into a gap between two very tall stone walls. The walls are dark with moss and built of large blocks, and the pines above them are the only thing breaking the line. It is a good example of how the castle uses stone and a single colour to control the way you approach. This was taken in late September, so the greens are summer greens.

Commons gives the date as 30 April 2010 and the place only as Otemachi in Aizuwakamatsu, so I cannot tell you which bridge this is. Lights along the rail make the red lacquer glow orange, and floodlights turn the cherry trees beyond into pale clouds against the black sky. The large bare tree at the left shows that not every tree was in bloom at once. A rough stone wall rises at the right edge, much like the walls in the photograph above, though I do not claim it is the same place.

This one is from 12 February 2011. Snow lies on the roofs, the pines have rope supports to hold up their branches under the weight, and rows of snow lanterns and small candles glow in the foreground. People are standing along the rope. The sources I used do not describe a winter event at the castle, so I cannot tell you which one this was, but it shows that the castle is also worth a look in deep winter.

Commons labels this 若松城（鶴ヶ城）, with a time of 9:43 in the morning on 8 February 2020. Fresh snow sits on every ledge of the rough stone base, so the stones look like a heap of white boulders. Bare trees stand at the left and right, and a low wooden fence keeps visitors back from the slope. The keep above shows its red-brown tiles dusted white. It is a reminder that the stone base, not the keep, is the oldest-looking part of the view.

Four minutes later, at 9:47, the same photographer looked up from a lower spot. Pines at the left and centre are wrapped in a fan of ropes running up to a central pole, which holds up the branches under the weight of the snow. This kind of rope support is a familiar winter sight in snowy parts of Japan. Behind the trees the keep climbs through its levels, and a long white wall runs off to the left.

This is the same morning, at 9:49. A long white wall with a tiled roof stands on a base of large cut stones at the left, and the keep rises behind the pines at the right. There is a signpost and an information board on the path, and the open ground in the foreground is covered with snow. I cannot name the wall or the building at the left from Commons, so I treat it as general scenery. Snow like this is typical of the region in deep winter, but I cannot say how often it falls on any given day.

Visiting Tsuruga Castleアクセス

  • Admission (adult): keep 410 yen, elementary and junior high students 150 yen (GLTJP, Nippon.com, mytabi, Fun Japan). A combined ticket for the keep and the Rinkaku tea house is 520 yen (GLTJP, Nippon.com). GLTJP says credit cards are not accepted.
  • Rinkaku alone: 210 yen plus 600 yen for matcha and a sweet (Nippon.com), or 200 yen plus 500 yen (Fun Japan). The two sources differ, so please check at the gate.
  • Hours: 8:30 to 17:00, last entry 16:30; open every day of the year (GLTJP, Nippon.com, mytabi, Fun Japan).
  • By bus from Aizuwakamatsu Station: GLTJP says to take the Haikara-san bus to the Tsurugajo Iriguchi stop and walk about 5 minutes. Fun Japan says the Aizu Loop Bus takes about 20 minutes to the Tsurugajo Kitaguchi stop plus a 5-minute walk, and mytabi says about 10 minutes by bus. The Edo Legacy page says to take the Hongo Loop Line for about 7 minutes to Sanno-cho and walk about 10 minutes. Routes and stop names differ by source, so please check the current timetable.
  • On foot: about 30 minutes from the station (Fun Japan).
  • From Tokyo: Tohoku Shinkansen to Koriyama about 1 hour 30 minutes, then the Ban-etsu West Line to Aizuwakamatsu about 1 hour 11 minutes (Edo Legacy).
  • By car: about 300 parking spaces, charged (mytabi, single source).
  • Time needed: no source gives this. My own estimate is about 1 to 1.5 hours for the keep and the park, longer if you take tea.

Making It a Trip滞在

The mytabi page lists Oyakuen, the garden of the Aizu Matsudaira clan, among nearby sights, but does not say how far away it is, so check a map before planning a walk. Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How much does it cost to enter Tsuruga Castle?
The keep is 410 yen for adults and 150 yen for elementary and junior high students (GLTJP, Nippon.com, mytabi, Fun Japan). A combined ticket with the Rinkaku tea house is 520 yen (GLTJP, Nippon.com). Rinkaku alone is listed as 210 yen plus 600 yen for tea and a sweet at Nippon.com, and 200 yen plus 500 yen at Fun Japan, so please confirm at the gate. GLTJP says credit cards are not accepted.
What are the opening hours?
8:30 to 17:00, with last entry at 16:30, open every day of the year (GLTJP, Nippon.com, mytabi, Fun Japan). Please check the official site before a winter visit.
How do I get there from Aizuwakamatsu Station?
By bus it is about 10 to 20 minutes depending on the route, followed by a walk of 5 to 10 minutes. GLTJP names the Haikara-san bus and the Tsurugajo Iriguchi stop; Fun Japan names the Aizu Loop Bus and the Tsurugajo Kitaguchi stop; the Edo Legacy page names the Hongo Loop Line and Sanno-cho. Walking from the station takes about 30 minutes (Fun Japan). Please check the current timetable.
Why is the roof red?
Nippon.com says red iron-glazed tiles were adopted in 1648 because ordinary tiles cracked in the freezing winters, and the Edo Legacy page also says the glaze resists frost. The 1965 reconstruction used black tiles, and the roof was changed back to red in 2011 (GLTJP, Nippon.com, Edo Legacy, Fun Japan).
Is the keep original?
No. The castle was demolished in 1874 after the 1868 siege, leaving only the stone walls. The current keep is a reinforced-concrete reconstruction completed in 1965 (GLTJP, Nippon.com, Fun Japan). Nippon.com says it was funded by citizen donations.
Who were the Byakkotai?
GLTJP describes them as teenage boys, sons of Aizu clan soldiers, who fought in the Boshin War. Nippon.com says about 20 young samurai fled to Mount Iimori during the siege, saw the town burning, believed the castle had fallen and chose to die. You can see Mount Iimori from the keep (GLTJP).
When are the cherry blossoms?
The park has about 1,000 cherry trees (GLTJP, Edo Legacy) and Fun Japan calls April the best time. GLTJP says the trees are lit during the season. I did not find a peak-bloom date in these sources, so please check a current bloom report.

Before You Leave the Parkむすびに

Give Tsuruga Castle at least an hour. Look up at the red roof from the grounds, climb to the top to find Mount Iimori, and take tea at the Rinkaku if you have time. Please treat the Byakkotai story with the quiet it deserves.

⛩️

The keep was rebuilt. The story it holds is the real one.
