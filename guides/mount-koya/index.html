Mount Koya

高野山

The Mountain Where a Saint Never Died

Every morning before dawn, two monks climb the stone path through Okunoin (奥の院) cemetery and cross the Gobyobashi bridge into the innermost sanctuary of Mount Koya. They carry a tray of food — the same meal, prepared the same way, at the same hour it has been brought for nearly 1,200 years. They bring it to Kobo Daishi (弘法大師). The monk who founded Shingon Buddhism in Japan, who died in 835 CE, is not, in the view of his followers, dead. He is in eternal meditation inside his mausoleum, waiting for the appearance of Maitreya, the Buddha of the future. He needs to be fed. The food is left. The monks leave. The fires in the Hall of 10,000 Lanterns in front of his tomb have not gone out since 921 CE.

This is not metaphor or ritual theater. It is a living religious conviction, and it is the reason Mount Koya exists, the reason 117 temples are crowded onto a mountain plateau in the forests of Wakayama Prefecture, the reason roughly a million people a year make the journey — by train, cable car, and bus — to a town of 4,500 people at 820 meters above sea level. They are coming to visit someone who, from their perspective, is still there.

A Trident Thrown Across the Sea空海と高野山の開創

Kukai was born in 774 in Sanuki Province, present-day Kagawa Prefecture in Shikoku. He was a scholar of unusual ability who abandoned a promising career in the imperial bureaucracy to pursue Buddhism, eventually making his way to Tang Dynasty China in 804 as part of an imperial diplomatic mission. In the Chinese capital Chang'an, he studied under the master Huiguo at the Qinglong Temple, became his designated successor in the esoteric Buddhist lineage, and returned to Japan in 806 carrying scriptures, mandalas, and ritual implements that no Japanese monk had previously possessed.

According to tradition, before leaving China Kukai threw a three-pronged ritual implement called a sankosho (三鈷杵) — a vajra, a symbol of indestructible truth in esoteric Buddhism — in the direction of Japan, praying that it would land where he should build his training center. The implement reportedly flew across the sea and landed in a pine tree on a remote mountain in the forests of the Kii Peninsula.

When Kukai, years later, finally climbed that mountain, he found the sankosho hanging from the branches of the pine tree that still stands in front of the Miedo hall at Danjo Garan today. He named the mountain Koya, requested permission from Emperor Saga to establish a monastic complex there, and received it in 816.

Whether or not you understand the sankosho story as literal, the geography it describes is real: Mount Koya is genuinely remote, genuinely elevated, genuinely surrounded by forest on all sides. The eight surrounding peaks are said to resemble the petals of an open lotus flower, cradling the plateau where the temples stand. Kukai spent the rest of his life building what he had envisioned. He died there in 835. The posthumous title Kobo Daishi (弘法大師) — Great Teacher of Spreading the Law — was granted by the emperor in 921 CE.

Okunoin奥の院

The heart of Mount Koya is not the temple complex at its center but the cemetery at its eastern edge. Okunoin (奥の院) is Japan's largest cemetery, with over 200,000 tombstones stretching through a forest of cedars some of which are centuries old. The path through it runs roughly two kilometers from the Ichi-no-hashi (一の橋) bridge to the Gobyobashi (御廟橋) bridge that marks the entrance to the innermost sanctuary.

Walking that path is unlike any other experience at a Japanese religious site. The dead buried here span the entire history of Japan: feudal lords and samurai, monks and merchants, soldiers from the Pacific War, and — arranged among them with no apparent irony — memorial stones placed by modern corporations for their employees, for products that have been discontinued, even for termites eliminated in the course of construction work. There are Jizo (地蔵) bodhisattva statues by the thousands, many wearing small red bibs placed by parents who have lost children. The oldest stones are so weathered their inscriptions are unreadable. The newest are polished granite. All of them orient, eventually, toward the same point: the mausoleum of Kobo Daishi at the far end of the path.

Note: Cameras are prohibited beyond the Gobyobashi bridge. The Torodo (燈籠堂), the Hall of Lanterns that stands before the mausoleum, houses more than 10,000 lanterns burning continuously — some since the 11th century — donated by worshippers across the centuries. The hall is dark and the lanterns make it luminous in a way that photographs cannot capture even where they are permitted.

Danjo Garan and Kongobuji壇上伽藍と金剛峯寺

The other pole of Mount Koya is the Danjo Garan (壇上伽藍), the sacred temple precinct at the center of town that Kukai first built as a place for ascetic training. Among its nineteen structures, two stand out. The Konpon Daito Pagoda (根本大塔) — 48.5 meters tall, a deep vermilion — was originally designed by Kukai as a physical embodiment of Shingon's esoteric world view, a mandala in three dimensions rather than two. The current structure dates from a 1937 reconstruction after fire. The Kondo (金堂), the Golden Hall, is the main hall of the entire mountain, rebuilt repeatedly over the centuries and most recently in 1932.

Kongobuji (金剛峯寺), the head temple of Shingon Buddhism — with 3,600 affiliated temples and ten million followers across Japan — stands nearby. Inside, gilded sliding doors painted with cranes and plum blossoms, scenes from Kukai's journey to China and the founding of Koyasan. The temple complex also contains Japan's largest rock garden: Banryutei (蟠龍庭), a raked gravel composition depicting two dragons emerging from clouds, made from 140 large stones brought from across Wakayama Prefecture.

Staying Overnight宿坊に泊まる

More than fifty of Mount Koya's 117 temples offer shukubo (宿坊) — temple lodging — to visitors. Staying overnight is not merely a logistical convenience; it changes what Mount Koya is. Day visitors see the buildings and the cemetery. Overnight guests wake before dawn for morning prayers, walk the lantern-lit path to Okunoin in darkness, and eat shojin ryori (精進料理) — the Buddhist vegetarian cuisine served at temple lodgings, governed by the "rule of five" that specifies five flavors, five cooking methods, and five colors in every meal.

The five vegetables considered too strongly flavored for monastic cooking — onion, scallion, chive, garlic, and ginger — are absent. What remains is precise, beautiful, and, for most visitors, completely unlike anything they have eaten before.

Reservations for shukubo should be made well in advance, particularly for autumn foliage season (mid to late November) and the major festival periods. Temple stay packages for Mount Koya that include shukubo, shojin ryori, and guided morning prayers can be booked through Viator and typically include transport from Osaka.

For a detailed walkthrough of what to expect from morning prayers to breakfast — including the specific temples recommended for international visitors — see Sacred Japan's complete guide to the Koyasan temple stay experience.

Getting Thereアクセス

Mount Koya is accessible from Osaka via the Nankai Koya Line to Gokurakubashi Station (極楽橋駅), followed by the Koyasan Cable Car (高野山ケーブル) to the summit — a total journey of roughly 90 minutes from Osaka Namba. From Kyoto or Nara, the journey requires a change at Osaka but remains straightforward.

FROM OSAKA

Take the Nankai Koya Line from Osaka Namba to Gokurakubashi Station (approximately 80 minutes), then the cable car to the mountain summit (5 minutes). Local buses connect the cable car station to the main temple districts and Okunoin.

FROM KYOTO / NARA

Travel by JR to Osaka, then transfer to the Nankai Koya Line at Namba. A combination ticket covering the cable car and local buses is available at Osaka Namba Station and covers most transportation needs within the mountain.

If you prefer to travel with a guide who can explain the significance of each site as you walk — the symbolism inside Okunoin, the meaning of the corporate memorial stones, the specific prayers made at the mausoleum — guided tours of Mount Koya depart from Osaka and typically run a full day or overnight.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Kobo Daishi really considered still alive?
Yes, by the followers of Shingon Buddhism. The formal teaching is that Kukai entered a state of eternal meditation (nyujo, 入定) in 835 CE rather than dying in an ordinary sense, and that he continues to intercede for those who pray to him. The twice-daily meal offerings to his mausoleum have been maintained continuously since the 10th century.
Do you need to be Buddhist to visit Mount Koya?
No. Mount Koya welcomes all visitors regardless of religious background, and the shukubo temple lodgings explicitly cater to international travelers. Morning prayers are open to guests to observe or participate in as they wish.
How long should you spend on Mount Koya?
One night and two days is the minimum to experience both the cemetery at dawn and the main temple precinct without rushing. A single long day trip from Osaka is possible but means missing the dawn visit to Okunoin, which is when the mountain is at its most atmospheric.
What is shojin ryori?
The vegetarian cuisine served at Buddhist temples, made without meat, fish, or strongly flavored vegetables. At Mount Koya's shukubo, it is served as a multi-course meal following traditional presentation rules governing flavor, cooking method, and color. The five excluded vegetables — onion, scallion, chive, garlic, and ginger — are considered too stimulating for monastic practice.
Is Mount Koya a UNESCO World Heritage Site?
Yes. It was designated in 2004 as part of the "Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range," along with Yoshino, Omine, and the Kumano pilgrimage routes.
How do you get to Mount Koya from Osaka?
Take the Nankai Koya Line from Osaka Namba to Gokurakubashi Station (about 80 minutes), then the cable car to the mountain (5 minutes). Local buses connect the cable car station to the temples and Okunoin. A combination ticket covering the cable car and buses is available at Osaka Namba Station.
