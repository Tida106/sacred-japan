Gifu Castle: Nobunaga's Mountain Castle, Its Closed Keep and What You Can Still See

岐阜城

Nobunaga's mountain castle above Gifu, with the keep closed until late 2027.

As a licensed guide, I describe Gifu Castle as a place where the mountain matters more than the building. The keep you see from town is a 1956 reconstruction, but the hill under it, Mount Kinka, is where Saito Dosan made his stronghold and where Oda Nobunaga chose the name Gifu in 1567 for the castle and the town below.

One thing to know before you plan anything: the keep and museum are currently closed for seismic reinforcement. GLTJP gives the closure as 19 May 2026 to late October 2027, and followingtheshogun says reopening is planned for November 2027. The ropeway still runs and the ruins are still open, so this guide tells you what you can do now and what to save for later. Where my sources disagree I say so, and I mark claims I found in only one.

Inabayama, Dosan and Nobunaga岐阜城の歴史

The castle was first called Inabayama Castle. GLTJP says a Kamakura shogunate official set up a fortress on the mountain in 1201. go-centraljapan names him as Nikaido Yukimasa, and the Japanese government’s multilingual database (MLIT) says the first fortification is believed to date from 1201 to 1204 and was built by the Nikaido clan. In the 15th century, GLTJP says, Saito Dosan developed the castle. MLIT is more specific: Dosan took the mountain in 1539 and his family held it for three generations.

In 1567 Oda Nobunaga defeated Saito Tatsuoki, took the castle and its town, and renamed the castle Gifu (MLIT, Nippon.com). Nippon.com says he used a seal reading tenka fubu, “unify the realm with military might.” He moved his base to Azuchi Castle in 1576 and left Gifu to his son Nobutada (Nippon.com). Both men died in the Honnoji Incident of 1582.

The end came after Nobunaga. MLIT says that in 1600, under Oda Hidenobu, the castle was attacked by Tokugawa Ieyasu’s forces and destroyed. GLTJP says Ieyasu ordered the castle abandoned in 1601, and Nippon.com says he had it torn down after Sekigahara. The year differs between sources, so I give 1600 to 1601. MLIT adds that some stone walls and wooden structures were reused to build Kano Castle a few kilometers to the south. GLTJP’s FAQ counts 22 lords before the abandonment.

MLIT says the ruins cover more than 2 square kilometers and are a National Historic Site. It also quotes a contemporary account: a Portuguese missionary called the palace and gardens at the foot of the mountain a “paradise on earth.” Nippon.com says excavations of Nobunaga’s residence began in 1984 and found gold-leafed chrysanthemum and peony tiles, with a 2012 analysis confirming the gold leaf.

The Keep: Three Builds in a Century天守

GLTJP and Nippon.com agree on the modern keep’s history. A wooden replica was built in 1910 (GLTJP calls it Japan’s first replica keep, and Nippon.com says it was a tourist attraction). Fire destroyed it in 1943. The present reinforced-concrete keep was completed in 1956, funded by local donations (GLTJP). It has three tiers and four floors, and Nippon.com says the lower three floors hold a museum and the top floor is an observation area. Nippon.com also says the modern keep follows historic records for its dimensions and appearance, and that the stone walls are original, dating from the Warring States period.

Commons labels this only “au château de Gifu” and dates it 15 May 2025. It shows the approach as I describe it: a stone-edged path with wooden railings climbs toward the keep, and tall yellow banners carrying a black crest line the way. I take the crest to be the Oda one, because the same crest is on the goshuin I mention below, but the Commons text does not say so. The fresh maple leaves explain the date, and in November the same trees are why the autumn color is worth the climb. This was taken when the keep was open, which it is not at the moment.

This is the same keep from closer in, taken on the same day. You can see the three roof levels, the gilded decoration on the central gable, the black-painted lower walls and, at the top, the observation deck with a few people looking out. That deck is the 360-degree viewpoint that is currently closed. Some of the gold looks worn in this picture, so treat the colors here as a snapshot of that day.

This is the keep seen from the stairs below on 21 February 2021, in the clear winter light. The black-painted lower floor, the white walls above it and the gold ornaments on the gables stand out against the blue sky, and yellow banners line the steps up to the stone base. A few people are on the stairs and on the observation deck, which gives a sense of scale. The bare branches are why winter is a good season for a clear view of the building, though it is cold on the ridge.

I tell visitors plainly: this is not an original keep, and nobody knows exactly what Nobunaga’s tower looked like. What you come for is the position, high above the Nagara River, and the walls that are original.

Commons describes this as Gifu Castle and the Gifu Castle Museum on top of Mount Kinka (the original description is in Japanese), taken on 18 February 2012. The smaller building to the right is therefore the museum, which GLTJP says sits on the east side of the grounds and was restored in 1975 in the style of a corner turret. The picture is from a distance in winter, when the bare trees show how small the buildings are on top of the mountain. Both buildings are closed at the moment according to my sources (see below).

What Is Closed, What Is Open現在の見学状況

  • Keep: closed since 19 May 2026 for seismic reinforcement; late October 2027 end, reopening planned November 2027 (GLTJP, followingtheshogun).
  • Museum: followingtheshogun says it has been closed since 1 April 2026 on the same schedule. I found that date in one source only.
  • Summit ruins and the area around the keep: free and open, including the presumed first gate ruins, the stone walls and the well remains (followingtheshogun).
  • Gifu Park at the foot of the mountain: free and open, with the ruins of Nobunaga’s residence, the garden ruins, Mitarashi Pond and an excavation information center that, according to followingtheshogun, is open 9:00 to 16:00 and closed on Tuesdays.
  • Ropeway: running, with maintenance suspensions listed for 1 to 28 December 2026 and 7 January to 16 February 2027 (followingtheshogun, one source).
  • Trails and toilets: followingtheshogun says some trail sections near the summit are closed until the end of September 2027 and the summit toilets are out of service until then. One source only.

Everything above can change, so please check the City of Gifu before you travel.

What to See Today見どころ

The ropeway and the view of the plain. The Mt. Kinka Ropeway leaves from Sanroku Station in Gifu Park and reaches the summit in about 4 minutes (GLTJP, Nippon.com). GLTJP’s ropeway page says it runs every 15 minutes, or every 10 minutes at peak times. Even with the keep closed, the ride gives a view over Gifu and the Nagara River.

Commons labels this only “au parc de Gifu” (“at Gifu Park”), and I take the cabin to be on the Mt. Kinka Ropeway, which starts there. The sign on its front appears to read as a 70th-anniversary message, and a small butterfly decoration with the kanji for Kicho sits on the pole above it; both are my reading of the picture and not something Commons states. What the photo does show well is the slope itself: steep, wooded and in fresh spring green, so the four-minute ride is a climb through forest before it is a view.

This was taken from the keep on 15 May 2025, and Commons labels it only as Gifu Castle. A roof ridge with its end tile points the way, and the wide river with a long bridge below is, by the geography, the Nagara, though the Commons text does not say so. Behind it lie the flat city, a sports complex and a line of hazy hills. It is the view you cannot have at present, because the keep is closed, so I include it to show what the reopening in November 2027 will bring back.

The walls and the area around the keep. The stone walls near the summit are the oldest thing here. Walk the grounds slowly and imagine the keep as a lookout and not as the centerpiece. On rainy days, followingtheshogun suggests the lower slopes instead, because the summit steps get slippery.

Commons names this the Tenka Daiichi-no-mon (“First Gate Under Heaven”) and labels it a restoration gate; the sign on the right carries the same name in English. GLTJP says a kabuki-style gate of this name, 4 m high and 5.5 m wide, was put up on Mount Kinka in 1990 and renovated in 2020. I take it to be the same gate, but this photo is from 2008, before that renovation, so it may look different now. Beyond it, stone steps climb between banners that carry the castle’s name. A stone marker at the left appears to read as a commemorative gift from a local club, which is my reading and not something Commons states.

Gifu Park and Nobunaga’s residence. At the foot of the mountain, GLTJP says the park was the site of the residences of Dosan and Nobunaga, with excavation still going on as of 2021. This is where the gold-leaf tiles were found. It is free and flat, which makes it a good alternative on a day with no view.

Commons titles this Gifu Park, the site of the Oda Nobunaga residence, and the tall stone marker on the right reads as exactly that in Japanese. A plain timber gate frame stands at the foot of a stone staircase that climbs the slope. It is a modern marker of the place and not an original gate, and I do not know what stands at the top of the steps, so I do not describe it. The photo was taken on a winter afternoon in 2014, when the trees were bare, and the layout of the park may have changed since.

Commons labels this “Gifu Park, Nobunaga’s garden” and dates it to the same afternoon as the gate, a few minutes earlier. A dark pond holds two small islets, one with what looks like a stone frog and another with a turtle, and an arched bridge and two stone lanterns sit among moss and bare trees. I cannot say how much of this is original to Nobunaga’s time and how much is a later garden; I only know that the park is the place where his residence is thought to have stood. Treat it as a quiet, free corner for a rainy day, as the section above suggests.

Commons titles this “Gifu Castle, three-storey pagoda and statue” and dates it 3 May 2013. In the foreground is a bronze rider on a leaping horse, one arm raised with a bow. Behind him the wooded slope of Mount Kinka rises, with a small red pagoda on the hillside and, on the summit at the top, the tiny white keep. I do not know who the statue depicts, because Commons does not say, so I leave it unnamed. The picture shows how high the keep sits above the town, which is the point of the whole climb.

The Squirrel Village. GLTJP’s ropeway page says a squirrel village just outside the summit station was created in 1965 as Japan’s first, using the wild squirrels on the mountain. I could not confirm whether it is open during the keep closure, so please check.

The Nagara River and cormorant fishing. The river runs below the castle and is known for ukai, fishing with trained cormorants from small boats. GLTJP says Nobunaga invited guests to watch it as hospitality and the Edo shogunate protected it. I did not verify the current season dates or ticket prices, so please check those separately if you want to go.

Commons titles this “Nagara River cormorant fishing” and dates it 7 July 2025, shortly after eight in the evening. The blaze at the bow is the fire basket that lights the water so the fish can be seen; sparks stream back from it. One man poles the boat from the stern and the other, crouched near the fire, appears to be handling the cords that run out toward the birds, which is my reading of the picture. Two cormorants float in the foreground. The photo shows why people book a boat: the whole scene is lit by that one fire.

Commons describes this as a cormorant of the ukai fishing, photographed in Gifu City, with the Latin name Phalacrocorax capillatus, dated 1 May 2010 in the afternoon. It is a daytime portrait and not taken on the river. The hooked tip of the beak is what catches the fish, the eye is a striking teal with a blue rim, and the bright yellow skin at the base of the bill and the white throat and neck feathers make the bird easy to recognise. It gives a face to the fishing described above.

Autumn color. GLTJP says the foliage usually peaks from early to late November, and lists Japanese maple, ginkgo and lacquer trees. It also says the castle is on a “Hida-Mino 33 Selections” list of autumn spots. Given the December ropeway suspension listed above, late November is the time to go if you want both.

Commons describes this as Mount Kinka seen from Mount Takasu in Gifu City, taken on 26 November 2023 at about half past eleven. The tiny white shape on the summit is the keep. The mountain is mostly dark evergreen forest, and the color comes in patches: the orange oak-like leaves in the foreground, rust and yellow on the far slopes and bare grey rock between them. So expect a mixed, not a uniformly red, mountain in late November. This is a view of the whole mountain from a neighbouring peak, not from the castle path, and the exact peak of the color changes from year to year.

A goshuin stamp. GLTJP says a goshuin with the Oda clan crest is sold at the shop by the ropeway station, with a premium version at some events.

Visiting Gifu Castleアクセス

  • From Nagoya: JR Tokaido Main Line rapid to JR Gifu Station, about 20 minutes and about 480 yen (followingtheshogun).
  • To Gifu Park: Gifu Bus from JR Gifu or Meitetsu Gifu Station, about 15 minutes on the N80 or the City Loop Line, then about 3 minutes on foot to Sanroku Station (GLTJP). Stop names differ between sources, so check the sign.
  • Ropeway: about 4 minutes, then about 8 minutes on foot to the castle (GLTJP, go-centraljapan); Nippon.com says 10 minutes. Fare 1,300 yen round trip for adults and 650 yen for children, or 800 and 400 yen one way (followingtheshogun). Nippon.com gives 1,100 yen, which may be older.
  • Hiking: four routes of 1.1 to 2.3 km, 40 to 60 minutes each (GLTJP). Some sections near the summit are closed (see above).
  • Normal keep hours (when it reopens): GLTJP gives 9:30 to 17:30 from 16 March to 16 October and 9:30 to 16:30 from 17 October to 15 March, with 6:30 to 16:30 on 1 January. go-centraljapan adds an 8:30 opening from 12 May to 16 October. Please check the official pages at that time.
  • Normal keep and museum ticket: 200 yen for adults and 100 yen for children, for ages 4 to under 16 (GLTJP).

Making It a Trip滞在

Gifu is an easy day trip from Nagoya, and it pairs well with our Inuyama Castle guide, which is one of Japan’s oldest original keeps and was built by Nobunaga’s uncle (per GLTJP). The keep at Nagoya Castle is also closed at the moment, so Inuyama is the better choice for going inside a castle in this part of Japan.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Can I go inside Gifu Castle right now?
No. GLTJP says the keep is closed for seismic reinforcement from 19 May 2026 to late October 2027, and followingtheshogun gives the same dates and says reopening is planned for November 2027. followingtheshogun adds that the museum has been closed since 1 April 2026. Dates can change, so please check the City of Gifu or the ropeway’s own pages before you go.
Is it still worth visiting while the keep is closed?
It depends on what you want. The stone walls, the well and the area around the keep are free to walk, the ropeway is running, and the ruins of Nobunaga’s residence at the foot of the mountain are open (followingtheshogun). What you cannot do is climb the keep for the 360-degree view or see the museum. If the view and the exhibits are the point, waiting until the reopening planned for November 2027 is reasonable.
How do I get to the top?
Take the Mt. Kinka Ropeway from Gifu Park. The ride takes about 4 minutes (GLTJP, Nippon.com), and the walk from the top station to the castle is about 8 minutes (GLTJP, go-centraljapan), though Nippon.com says 10 and another source says 15. You can also hike one of four routes of 1.1 to 2.3 km, which GLTJP puts at 40 to 60 minutes each. followingtheshogun says that sections of the trails near the summit are closed until the end of September 2027, so check which routes are open.
How much does it cost?
The keep and museum ticket is 200 yen for adults and 100 yen for children (GLTJP, Nippon.com, go-centraljapan), but both are closed. The ropeway is 1,300 yen round trip for adults and 650 yen for children, or 800 and 400 yen one way (followingtheshogun). Nippon.com gives 1,100 yen round trip, which may be an older price. The ruins and Gifu Park are free (followingtheshogun).
Does the ropeway run all year?
followingtheshogun says it runs normally but lists maintenance suspensions on 1 to 28 December 2026 and 7 January to 16 February 2027. I found that in one source only, so please check the ropeway’s own site before planning around those dates.
How do I get to Gifu from Nagoya?
followingtheshogun says the JR Tokaido Main Line rapid takes about 20 minutes and costs about 480 yen. From JR Gifu or Meitetsu Gifu Station, GLTJP says a Gifu Bus (the N80 or the City Loop Line) takes about 15 minutes to Gifu Park, followed by a 3-minute walk to the ropeway’s Sanroku Station. Bus stop names differ between sources, so check the sign on the bus.

Before You Go Upむすびに

Go for the ropeway, the walls and the view of the Nagara River, and treat the closed keep as a reason to return in late 2027. If you need an open keep today, Inuyama is the safer choice, and if you want Nobunaga, the walls above the plain are still the place to stand.

⛩️

The man who named Gifu left it in 1576, but the mountain kept his name.
