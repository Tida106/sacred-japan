Hirosaki Castle: The Keep That Moved and the Cherry Blossom Moat

弘前城

Tohoku's only original keep, currently on the move, and the cherry blossom park around it.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I have to give guests an unusual warning about Hirosaki Castle: the keep is not where it is supposed to be. It was lifted off its stone base in 2015 and slid sideways so the wall beneath it could be rebuilt, and it is only now being pulled back. That makes this an unusual castle visit, and a rewarding one, because the park is also one of Japan's best-known cherry blossom spots.

This guide covers the keep's history, the move and the delays, what is open and what is not, the blossoms, and the practical details. The short version: the keep interior is closed until fiscal 2032, and the city expects the exterior to be visible only until about autumn 2028, so earlier is better.

The Keep That Burned and Was Rebuilt Small天守の歴史

According to GLTJP, the castle was planned in 1603 by Tsugaru Tamenobu, the first lord of the Hirosaki domain, and finished in 1611 under the second lord, Nobuhira. Wikipedia adds that work stalled after Tamenobu's death in 1604 and resumed in 1609. The original keep had five stories.

It did not last. Both sources say lightning struck in 1627 and the keep burned down. GLTJP says it was struck at the shachihoko ornament on the roof. For almost two centuries there was no keep at all.

The one you see now dates from 1810, when the ninth lord, Tsugaru Yasuchika, had a three-story keep built. GLTJP describes it as a remodeled corner turret, the Tatsumi Turret, while Wikipedia says it was built at the southeast corner rather than the original site. Either way, it is smaller than the first keep. It is one of Japan's 12 surviving Edo-period keeps and, according to GLTJP, the only one in the Tohoku region. It is also an Important Cultural Property.

The Keep on the Move天守の曳家

Here is the part most guidebooks have not caught up with. The city of Hirosaki says the stone wall on the east side of the main bailey was bulging, with a risk of collapse. To repair it, the keep, which weighs roughly 400 tons (sources give figures between 360 and 400), was moved about 70 meters to a temporary platform in 2015 using the hikiya method, in which a building is jacked up and slid on rails without being taken apart. The city page gives the distance as about 77.6 meters.

The photo at the top of this page shows the keep as it stands today. It sits on a temporary platform inside the Honmaru, a low wooden fence around its base is hung with purple banners bearing a crest, and a snow-capped peak rises in the distance. The Commons description for this May 2022 photo notes that the keep was moved by the hikiya method because of the large-scale work on its base and stone wall.

For comparison, these two photos were taken before the 2015 move, when the keep still stood on its stone base. The first, from May 2007, shows it at dusk during the cherry blossom season, with its white walls lit from below, its curved gables and a tall stone base beneath it. The Commons file notes that it was taken during the festival.

The second, from May 2010, is a night view with the keep floodlit against a black sky and blossoms in the foreground. Photos like these are why the keep is hard to separate from the cherry trees in most people's minds.

The wall repair took years. The city says the stone re-stacking on the east face started in June 2021 and finished on December 19, 2024, with 2,185 stones reset. After that, the keep had to come home.

The move back began in 2026. According to the Web Toonippo local newspaper, the keep had been raised by 76 centimeters by early July, and the first stage of the move began on July 1, with the plan to move about 78 meters in three stages. The city also ran a public event in August in which volunteers helped pull the keep.

Then the schedule slipped. The original target was November 2026. The city's latest update, which I checked in late October 2026, says the keep is expected to be back on its original base by March 2027, and fixed to it by autumn 2027. Local reports link the delay to damage found in the keep's timber base, though the city page I read does not state the reason. After that come seismic reinforcement and preservation repairs.

🏯 What this means for your visit: The keep interior has been closed since November 24, 2025, and the city says it will stay closed through fiscal 2032. The city also says the exterior will be visible until about autumn 2028, after which a protective enclosure will hide it for several years. These dates are plans and may change, so please check the Hirosaki City site before you go.

What You Can See in the Park弘前公園

Even with the keep in transit, the park is worth the trip. GLTJP says five castle gates and three turrets survive from the Edo period, and Wikipedia lists the Ninomaru Tatsumi, Hitsujisaru and Ushitora turrets among them. GLTJP says the Ushitora Turret dates from 1610. The Otemon Gate is the usual starting point.

This April 2025 photo looks at the Otemon Gate through branches of cherry. Pink blossoms on the left and weeping branches on the right frame the dark timber gate, whose green tile roof carries fish-shaped ornaments at the ridge ends, while visitors walk through the opening. The Commons description lists it as a nationally designated Important Cultural Property.

One detail I like: GLTJP says the Kikko Gate is the only structure at the castle that still carries arrow scars from battle. I have not checked this against a second source, so please treat it as one source's claim.

The keep sits on a temporary base inside the Honmaru, so you can see it from close range. The city also says work can be viewed from an observation deck, and that it plans to share progress by video or site tours. GLTJP also lists a botanical garden of about 8 hectares in the park.

In winter the park is a different place. This February 2020 night photo shows Minami-uchi-mon, the South Inner Gate as I translate it, under falling snow, with its white walls lit up against the dark timber. At the left, a snow lantern holds a painted warrior face, and the red and white signs warn of falling snow and mark a fire hydrant. The Commons file does not name an event, so I cannot tell you whether the lanterns and lighting are a regular winter feature.

Autumn is also worth a look. In this November 2010 night photo, a white three-story turret stands above the moat while maples lit in red and orange are reflected in the still water. Commons describes the file only as Hirosaki Park in autumn, so I am not naming the turret. Lighting schedules change, so please check the park's official information.

Cherry Blossoms and the Petal Moat桜とお濠

Hirosaki Park is known for its cherry blossoms. GLTJP says the park has about 2,600 trees of roughly 50 varieties, centered on Somei Yoshino, and that peak bloom is usually from late April to early May. Another guide gives the festival as running from about April 23 to May 5, with night illumination in the evenings. Dates move every year, so please check the official forecast, which the park posts in early April.

At night, the blossoms are lit from below. This 2021 photo shows a red arched bridge crossing a moat between two banks of lit cherry trees, with a line of visitors on the bridge and the pink canopy reflected in the water.

This April 2026 photo looks down a straight stretch of moat, with cherry trees leaning in from both banks and their reflection doubling the canopy on the black water. It is the kind of view that makes people stay far later than they planned.

The sight to look for is the hanaikada, or petal raft: after the blossoms fall, petals cover the surface of the moat. GLTJP says it has been named one of the world's most spectacular views to see before you die. One guide says it forms two or three days after the peak, which is useful to know if you can only come once.

Here is the petal raft itself. In this April 2026 photo, the water under arching branches is covered with petals, with a bright light at the far end of the moat reflected in the surface.

One more April 2026 view: trees in full bloom along a bank with dark pines behind them, their reflection broken by floating petals.

I tell guests to come early in the morning during the festival, because one guide recommends 9:00 to 10:30 for lighter crowds. For a quieter visit, the same guide says the Honmaru is free to enter in winter, though the keep interior stays closed.

Visiting Hirosaki Castleアクセス

  • Getting there: GLTJP says to take the 100-yen Konan bus from JR Hirosaki Station to the Shiyakusho-mae Koen Iriguchi stop, then walk about 3 minutes. Another guide lists about 15 minutes by bus, or about 30 minutes on foot to Otemon Gate. From Aomori Station, the JR Ou Line takes about 45 minutes according to the same guide.
  • Hours: GLTJP lists 9:00 to 17:00 from April 1 to November 23 for the Honmaru and Kita-no-Kuruwa, and until 21:00 during the cherry blossom festival. This year's paid season ends on November 23.
  • Tickets: GLTJP lists 320 yen for adults and 100 yen for children. Sources differ on whether the botanical garden is included. Please confirm at the gate.
  • Keep interior: closed to visitors since November 24, 2025, with the city planning the closure through fiscal 2032.
  • Time needed: about two hours for the park. In blossom season, plan more.
  • Check first: the keep schedule, hours and prices can change. Please confirm on the Hirosaki City and Hirosaki Park sites before you go.

Making It a Trip滞在

Hirosaki is a small city in Aomori, and from Aomori Station it is about 45 minutes on the JR Ou Line, so it works well as a stop on a trip through northern Tohoku. If you come for the blossoms, book your stay early.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Can I go inside Hirosaki Castle's keep?
Not at the moment. The Hirosaki Park website says the keep interior closed to visitors after November 23, 2025, and the city says the closure runs through fiscal 2032. You can still walk the grounds and see the keep from outside, but the city says the exterior will be visible only until about autumn 2028, after which a protective enclosure is planned. Schedules can change, so please check the city site.
Why was the keep moved?
The city says the stone wall on the east side of the main bailey was bulging and at risk of collapse. In 2015 the keep was moved about 70 meters inward using the traditional hikiya method so the wall could be taken apart and rebuilt. The city lists the keep as being moved back about 78 meters, with the first stage starting on July 1, 2026.
How much does Hirosaki Castle cost?
GLTJP lists 320 yen for adults and 100 yen for children for the Honmaru and Kita-no-Kuruwa areas, which are open from April 1 to November 23, 9:00 to 17:00 (until 21:00 during the cherry blossom festival). Most of the park is free. Sources differ on whether the botanical garden is included, so please confirm at the gate.
When are the cherry blossoms at Hirosaki Castle?
Peak bloom is typically late April to early May, and the Hirosaki Cherry Blossom Festival runs around the same period. Sources give about 2,600 trees of roughly 50 varieties, mostly Somei Yoshino. The petals-on-the-moat view, called hanaikada, forms a few days after the peak. The park posts a bloom forecast in early April.
How do I get to Hirosaki Castle?
GLTJP says to take the 100-yen Konan bus from JR Hirosaki Station to the Shiyakusho-mae Koen Iriguchi stop and walk about three minutes. One guide lists about 15 minutes on the bus, or about 30 minutes on foot to Otemon Gate. From Aomori Station the JR Ou Line takes about 45 minutes.

A Castle Worth Seeing Twiceむすびに

Hirosaki is the castle I recommend to guests who like to see history while it is still being made. The keep is in transit, the blossoms come every spring, and the walls beneath it have just been rebuilt stone by stone.
