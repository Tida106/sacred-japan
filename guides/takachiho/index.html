Takachiho: Where Japanese Mythology Actually Happened

高千穂

The sun goddess hid in a cave and plunged the world into darkness. The other gods threw a party to lure her back out. In a river gorge in Miyazaki, the cave, the gorge, and the nightly ritual dance are all still here.

Every mythology has its geography. Greece has Delphi and Olympus; the Norse have Asgard projected onto Scandinavia's mountains. Japan's oldest myths — the stories recorded in the Kojiki (古事記) (712 CE) and Nihon Shoki (日本書紀) (720 CE) that describe how the gods created the islands, how the sun goddess quarreled with her brother and darkened the world, how the imperial line was established — are mapped onto a narrow river gorge in the mountains of Miyazaki Prefecture in southern Kyushu. The place is called Takachiho. The cave is still there. The river still runs through it. Every night, in a hall on the shrine grounds, priests perform the dance that the gods invented to lure the sun back out.

The Myth天岩戸神話

In the beginning — or rather, after the creation of Japan but before things had fully settled — Susanoo, the god of storms, was on one of his worst behaviors. His mother, Izanami, had died and he was grieving loudly; his grief expressed itself as destruction: crops flattened, irrigation ditches buried, sacred buildings defiled. His sister, Amaterasu, the sun goddess and ruler of the heavenly plain, watched this escalate until she could tolerate it no more. She retreated into a cave — Ama-no-Iwato (天岩戸), the Heavenly Rock Cave — and sealed the entrance behind her. The world went dark.

In the darkness, the eight million gods of the Japanese pantheon gathered at a cave downstream — Amano Yasukawara (天安河原), the Heavenly Peaceful River Source — to hold a crisis council. The god of wisdom, Omoikane, proposed a plan: they would hold a festival so raucous and apparently joyful that Amaterasu's curiosity would be stronger than her anger. They hung jewels and a sacred mirror in the branches of a tree outside the cave. They set roosters crowing. Then Ame-no-Uzume, the goddess of mirth, climbed onto an overturned tub and performed a dance so wild and suggestive that the assembled gods burst into helpless laughter.

Amaterasu, alone in the dark with eight million gods clearly enjoying themselves outside her door, could not stand it. She cracked the entrance open.

The god Ame-no-Tajikarao, who had been waiting precisely for this moment, seized her hand and pulled her out. Light returned to the world. A holy rope — shimenawa (注連縄) — was stretched across the cave entrance to prevent her ever going back in. The sacred mirror she had glimpsed in the tree before the cave became the Yata no Kagami, one of Japan's three imperial treasures, now housed at Ise Grand Shrine.

This is the myth. Three things are worth noting. First, it is recorded in Japan's two oldest texts as the foundational explanation for why the sun rises and sets — a cosmological story that also explains why every Shinto shrine has a mirror, why roosters are kept at shrines, why shimenawa rope marks sacred space, and why kagura ritual dance exists. Second, it places all of this in Takachiho. Third, Takachiho takes this seriously. The cave is still enshrined. The river still runs past it. No one has built a theme park.

Amano Iwato Shrine天岩戸神社

Amano Iwato Shrine stands on the banks of the Iwato River, divided between two halls on opposite sides of the water: the Nishi-Hongu (西本宮) (West Hall) and Higashi-Hongu (東本宮) (East Hall), both enshrining Amaterasu. The cave itself is across the river from the main worship area, set into the cliff face — visible from a designated viewing platform on guided tours conducted by shrine priests, but not directly accessible to ordinary visitors. No mortal has entered the cave since the shimenawa was stretched across it, according to shrine tradition, and the tradition is maintained literally.

The tour gives a clear view of the shallow grotto cut into the rock above the river, with a small torii gate at its entrance. The priest explains the myth, points out the relevant features of the cliff face, and answers questions. It takes about twenty minutes and is included in the shrine visit. The cave is smaller than the myth implies — all caves are — but the river gorge surrounding it, the cedar forest above, and the knowledge of what the site represents create an atmosphere that larger and more elaborate sacred sites in Japan rarely match.

No mortal has entered the cave since the shimenawa was stretched across it. Shrine tradition maintains this literally — there is no side entrance, no special-access tour, no exception made for researchers or clergy. The cave has been off-limits for as long as the myth has been told.

Amano Yasukawara: Where the Gods Held Council

A ten-minute walk downstream from the shrine along the river leads to Amano Yasukawara — the cave where the eight million gods held their council. The trail follows the river through forest before opening into a large, shallow cave set into the cliff bank. Inside, thousands of small stone cairns cover every surface, built by pilgrims and visitors over years. The cave is open, the river audible outside, and the cairns — each representing a prayer or a wish — give it a texture that photographs only partially capture. There is a small shrine at the back. The atmosphere is genuinely strange in a way that is difficult to attribute to any single element.

Takachiho Gorge高千穂峡

The other anchor of a Takachiho visit is completely different in character. Takachiho Gorge was carved by the Gokase River through basalt columns formed by volcanic eruptions from Mount Aso roughly 90,000 years ago. The gorge runs about a kilometer in length, its walls of hexagonal basalt rising 80–100 meters, a narrow strip of sky visible at the top. At its center, the Manai waterfall drops 17 meters into the river below.

Rowboats can be rented from a boathouse at the gorge entrance and paddled along the river beneath the basalt walls — the only way to see the waterfall and gorge from below, and the experience most people come to Takachiho specifically to have. The boats hold up to three people, cost approximately 2,000 yen for 30 minutes, and require a reservation in advance during peak seasons (spring and autumn). A walking path along the rim of the gorge offers views from above.

The gorge has no direct connection to the Amaterasu myth — it is a geological formation, not a sacred site — but the combination of gorge in the morning and shrine in the afternoon makes a full day in Takachiho feel genuinely complete rather than padded.

The Nightly Kagura高千穂神楽

Every night, at 8:00pm, Takachiho Shrine stages a kagura performance — the ritual Shinto dance that, according to the myth, originated with Ame-no-Uzume's performance outside Amaterasu's cave. The hour-long performance presents four excerpts from the 33 dances of Takachiho Yokagura (高千穂夜神楽), the full version of which is performed through the night at various community events throughout the year. The four excerpts dramatize key scenes from the Kojiki: the creation of the islands, the emergence of Amaterasu, the slaying of the eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi by Susanoo, and the descent of the imperial line.

The performances are held inside the kagura hall on the shrine grounds, open to visitors regardless of language. No Japanese is required to follow the narrative — the masks, movement, and occasional humor are the story. Susanoo's role here bookends the myth in an unexpected way: after his rampage forced Amaterasu into hiding, he was exiled from the heavens, slew the serpent Orochi on his way down to earth, and was ultimately enshrined at Izumo Taisha — the same troublemaker god connecting two of Japan's most important sacred sites.

The Takachiho kagura tradition is designated an Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property. Admission to the nightly performance is approximately 1,000 yen.

Getting to Takachihoアクセス

Takachiho is remote by the standards of Japanese tourist destinations, and this is part of its character. There is no direct train — the Takachiho Railway was permanently closed after a typhoon in 2005. Because of this remoteness, many visitors choose guided tours from Fukuoka or Kumamoto that handle the transfers and time the visit to the shrine, gorge, and evening kagura performance within a single trip.

FROM KUMAMOTO

The most commonly used route: approximately 2 hours by highway bus from Kumamoto Station (around 2,000 yen), connecting to the Kyushu Shinkansen from Osaka, Fukuoka, or Tokyo.

FROM NOBEOKA

Approximately 1.5 hours by bus from Nobeoka Station on the JR Nippo Main Line, which connects to Fukuoka/Hakata and Miyazaki.

LOCAL TRANSPORT

Within Takachiho, buses connect the town center to Amano Iwato Shrine (15 minutes, around 200 yen one way; infrequent — check the schedule in advance). Taxis are available for approximately 2,700 yen one way. Rental cars and bicycles are available in the town center for visitors who want more flexibility.

Admission
Amano Iwato Shrine and Amano Yasukawara are free. Takachiho Gorge is free to walk; rowboats cost around 2,000 yen per 30 minutes. The nightly kagura performance is around 1,000 yen.
Kagura hours
Nightly at 8:00pm, roughly one hour, at the kagura hall on the Takachiho Shrine grounds.
Best way to visit
An overnight stay makes far better use of the trip than a rushed day visit — see the FAQ below for a suggested sequence across two days.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is Takachiho famous for?
Takachiho is the setting of Japan's most important Shinto creation myths, recorded in the Kojiki (712 CE) and Nihon Shoki (720 CE). The cave where the sun goddess Amaterasu hid — Ama-no-Iwato — is enshrined here, as is Amano Yasukawara, where the eight million gods gathered to plan her rescue. The town also has a dramatic river gorge and a nightly kagura ritual dance performance.
Can you enter the cave at Amano Iwato Shrine?
No. The cave is the shrine's sacred object (goshintai, 御神体) and no ordinary visitors are permitted inside. It is visible from a designated viewing platform on guided tours conducted by shrine priests. The shimenawa sacred rope stretched across the entrance is maintained as a physical symbol of the cave's permanent sealing.
What is Amano Yasukawara?
Amano Yasukawara is the cave beside the Iwato River where, according to Japanese mythology, the eight million gods gathered to plan how to lure Amaterasu out of hiding. It is a ten-minute walk downstream from Amano Iwato Shrine. Inside the cave, visitors leave thousands of small stone cairns as prayers and offerings.
What is the kagura performance at Takachiho Shrine?
A nightly one-hour performance of excerpts from Takachiho Yokagura — the ritual Shinto dance tradition that traces its origin to the dance Ame-no-Uzume performed to lure Amaterasu out of the cave. Four scenes from the Kojiki are dramatized using masks and traditional costumes. Admission is around 1,000 yen; no Japanese language knowledge required. Performances begin at 8:00pm.
How do I get to Takachiho?
There is no direct train service. The most common route is by highway bus from Kumamoto (approximately 2 hours, around 2,000 yen), connecting to the Kyushu Shinkansen from Fukuoka, Osaka, or Tokyo. Buses also run from Nobeoka on the JR Nippo Main Line (approximately 1.5 hours).
Can Takachiho be done as a day trip?
Technically yes from Kumamoto, but it is rushed. An overnight stay — allowing for the gorge in the afternoon, the kagura performance in the evening, and Amano Iwato Shrine in the morning — makes significantly better use of the journey. The town has a handful of traditional ryokan and guesthouses.
