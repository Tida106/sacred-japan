Arita Ware: How to Choose Authentic Japanese Porcelain

有田焼の選び方

Japan's first porcelain came from a small town in Saga, and its blue-and-white and colorful enamel designs are among the most copied. Here is how I tell the real thing, care for it, and visit the kilns.

As a licensed guide, I often meet travelers who stand in front of a shop shelf of blue-and-white plates, unsure what they are looking at. One asks, “Is this Arita or Imari?” Another asks, “Why is one bowl 30 dollars and another 300?”

This guide is the short version of what I tell them. It covers what Arita ware is, the main styles, how to check what you are buying, and how to enjoy the town if you go.

What Arita Ware Is有田焼とは

Arita ware is porcelain from Arita in Saga Prefecture, on the island of Kyushu. It is usually dated to around the turn of the 17th century, when the Korean potter Yi Sam-pyeong (known in Japan as Kanagae Sanbee) found porcelain stone at Izumiyama. The page I checked on the official craft site says this was probably the first porcelain made in Japan. Imari-Arita ware was designated a traditional craft in 1977.

Arita ware was shipped from Imari Port, which is why it is also called Imari ware. In the late 17th century the Dutch East India Company exported it to Europe, where it became known as “Imari”.

Making it takes many steps, and the official craft site says skilled craftsmen usually handle each stage separately. The clay is made from ground porcelain stone, the piece is shaped and fired, decorated, glazed, and fired again at about 1300°C. Overglaze enamels are then painted on and fired once more at a lower temperature.

The Main Styles主な様式

You do not need to memorize every style, but these four names come up most often in shops and museums.

  • Ko-Imari: early export-style Imari, which appealed strongly to Europeans.
  • Kakiemon: overglaze enamel on a milky-white base, leaving areas of blank space. Sakaida Kakiemon is credited with developing overglaze enamel decoration in the 1640s.
  • Kinrande: deep blue underglaze decoration heavily accented with red and gold.
  • Nabeshima: made under strict domain control as tribute and gift items.

The simple blue-and-white pieces most visitors buy use cobalt pigment painted under the glaze. It is called gosu or sometsuke in Japan.

How to Spot the Real Thing本物の見分け方

There is no single stamp that settles it, so I check several things together.

  • The kiln or maker. Ask who made it. A real piece comes with a kiln or maker name, not just a brand-like word.
  • The origin. A listing that only says “Japanese style” or gives no origin is not enough. Look for Arita or Saga.
  • The decoration. Ask whether it is hand-painted or printed. Hand-painted lines show small natural variations.
  • The feel. Check the weight, the thickness of the rim and the stability of the foot. Small variations in glaze and shape are part of handmade work and are not flaws.
  • The price. Prices range widely, from souvenir pieces to artist works. I will not quote a figure, but a very low price for a large, richly painted piece deserves a second look.

🔍 The guide's tip: Start with a plate about 15 cm across. It is the most versatile size, easy to pack, and a good way to learn what you like before you buy a larger set.

Care and Packingお手入れと梱包

  • Dishwasher and microwave: check each item, because it varies.
  • Gold, silver and delicate overglaze: wash by hand.
  • Before first use: ask the shop whether the piece needs medome, a treatment that reduces water absorption.
  • Packing: ceramics are fragile, so ask about secure packing and overseas shipping before you pay.

Visit Arita有田を訪ねる

Arita is about 40 minutes from Saga Station on the JR Sasebo Line, and a little under 90 minutes from Hakata Station by express train. These are the places I would put on the list:

  • Kyushu Ceramic Museum: a good first stop for the origins and history of the local ceramics.
  • Arita Porcelain Park: a garden and palace in the style of 18th-century German Baroque architecture, a nod to Arita's influence on European ceramics.
  • Tozan Shrine: dedicated to the deity of Arita ware, with many features that would usually be stone made of porcelain instead. It is on a hill with views over the town.
  • Arita streetscape: designated an Important Preservation District for Groups of Traditional Buildings.
  • Arita Ceramics Home Plaza: open year-round, with over 20 stores selling ceramics.
  • Okawachiyama Village (Imari): a secluded mountain village with kilns, workshops and shops.

The biggest event is the Arita Ceramics Fair, held every year in Golden Week, generally April 29 to May 5. It has hundreds of vendors and affordable pieces, and it is very crowded. Arrive at opening time, and check the official page for the year's details.

Where to Buy買える場所

In Arita, the Ceramics Home Plaza, kiln shops and the Golden Week fair are the best places. In Japan's department stores you will also find reliable makers. From abroad, specialist Japan-goods shops that name the kiln are my first choice. If you shop on Amazon, use the checks above. A plate, a rice bowl or a small sake cup is a good first piece. For a cup to go with it, see Sake and Shrines.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Arita ware the same as Imari ware?
Yes, they are closely linked. Arita ware was shipped from Imari Port, so it came to be called Imari ware, especially in the Dutch trade to Europe. Today Arita is the town where much of the porcelain is made.
How can I tell if Arita porcelain is genuine?
Ask for the kiln or maker name and where it was made, and look at how it is decorated. Handmade pieces show small variations in glaze and shape, which are not flaws. Treat listings that only say Japanese style or give no origin with caution.
Can I put Arita ware in the dishwasher or microwave?
Check each item, because suitability varies. Pieces with gold or silver accents or delicate overglaze painting are best washed by hand. If you are unsure, ask the shop before you buy.
When is the Arita Ceramics Fair?
It is held in Arita every year during Golden Week, generally April 29 to May 5. It is one of Japan's largest ceramic fairs, with hundreds of vendors, and it gets very crowded, so arriving at opening time helps.
How do I get to Arita from Fukuoka?
Express trains from Hakata Station take a little under 90 minutes. From Saga Station, the JR Sasebo Line takes roughly 40 minutes.

A Plate for Daily Useむすびに

Good porcelain is made to be used, not kept in a cabinet. Check the kiln, feel the weight, and wash the delicate pieces by hand. If you are planning a Kyushu trip, add Arita to the list. For more crafts, see our Japan crafts guide, or the glass and iron guides for Edo Kiriko and Nambu Tekki.
