Katsuragawa Sokusho Myoo-in: A Tendai Training Sanctuary in Shiga

葛川息障明王院（滋賀県大津市）

Katsuragawa Sokusho Myoo-in (葛川息障明王院) lies in the mountains of Otsu, in Shiga Prefecture, beside the Katsura River. It is a Tendai Buddhist temple, but not a garden to stroll through: it was built as a training hall and, according to the City of Otsu, it remains a place of practice today. A visit here is a different kind of autumn outing, quieter and more austere than the famous foliage temples of Kyoto, and it is best approached as a visit to a working sanctuary.

Soo and the Mountain Tradition相応和尚

The temple was built in the Jogan era (859 to 877) by the monk Soo as a training hall for Tendai practice. Tendai is the school that Saicho brought to Mount Hiei, and the mountain was the birthplace of the practice known as kaihōgyō, the thousand-day circuit of the mountain. Scholars who have studied the practice point to Katsuragawa as having played an important part in how it developed, and the temple is described as continuing as a training ground for the Tendai practice today. You can read the story of the headquarters mountain in our Enryaku-ji guide.

A Temple Rebuilt in 1715

The main hall that stands today was rebuilt in 1715 and was designated an Important Cultural Property in 1993. It is the building that gives the valley its sense of age.

A Place of Practice, Not a Garden修行の道場

This is not a manicured foliage garden. The setting is a river gorge in forested mountains, and the autumn leaves here colour the valley rather than frame a pavilion. The experience is defined by quiet, cold air, and the knowledge that the halls around you are used for training. If you want a sense of the atmosphere, think of it as the opposite of a crowded Kyoto temple.

Autumn Colour

Leaves in the mountains of Shiga typically colour earlier and drop sooner than in the cities, so check the current foliage reports before you plan the trip. The air is cold in late autumn, and the valley can be damp and misty.

Getting There and Preparingアクセスと準備

The valley is remote. Most visitors rely on a local bus with a limited timetable, so check it carefully, including the last bus back, because missing it can leave you stranded. Dress in heavy layers and bring sturdy shoes. Before you set out, check the temple's current notices: because the halls are used for practice, access to some areas may be limited.

Etiquette in a Training Sanctuary

  • Keep silent or speak very softly; this is a place for reflection, not conversation.
  • Respect the monks' training. Do not enter areas that are closed, and follow every sign.
  • Photograph with restraint, and never in front of monks or people at prayer.

The general temple etiquette is covered in our Shrine vs Temple guide. For other foliage sanctuaries guarded by inconvenience, see The Myth of “Hidden Gems”.

Sources参考

Founding, dates, and cultural-property status come from the City of Otsu's English tourism guide: Otsu City, sightseeing guide (PDF). The role of the temple in the development of kaihogyo is discussed in scholarship on Tendai mountain asceticism. Confirm current access and rules with the temple or the city before you go.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Who founded Katsuragawa Sokusho Myoo-in?
According to the City of Otsu, the hall was built in the Jogan era (859 to 877) by the monk Soo (Souou Kasho) as a training hall for Tendai practice.
Is the main hall a designated cultural property?
Yes. The main hall was rebuilt in 1715 and designated an Important Cultural Property in 1993, according to Otsu City's English guide.
Is it a sightseeing temple?
No. It remains a place of Tendai training linked to the mountain practice known as kaihogyo, so quiet and respect for the temple's rules matter more than photographs.
