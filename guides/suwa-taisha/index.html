Suwa Taisha: The Shrine With Nothing at Its Center

諏訪大社　四社めぐり

Introduction

As a licensed guide, I'm fascinated by Shinto shrines built around an absence. Suwa Taisha, spread across four sites on opposite shores of Lake Suwa in Nagano, is the most extreme case I know: three of its four main shrines have no honden at all — no building housing the god, because for most of its 1,300-plus-year history nobody felt the need to put one up. The deity lived in the mountain itself, in a sacred rock, or inside a living high priest. You didn't build a house for a god who was already standing in front of you.

Four Shrines, One God, No Main Hall

Suwa Taisha isn't a single shrine but a complex of four — Kamisha Honmiya and Kamisha Maemiya on the lake's south shore, Shimosha Harumiya and Shimosha Akimiya on the north — plus roughly sixty smaller auxiliary shrines scattered through the surrounding hills. Instead of a central sanctuary, worship historically focused on whatever stood in for the god's physical presence at each site: Mount Moriya behind the Kamisha Honmiya, a sacred rock (iwakura), or — most unusually — a living child chosen as Ōhōri, a human high priest treated as the deity's temporary vessel. At the Shimosha sites, the stand-in was a tree instead: a cedar at Harumiya, a yew at Akimiya.

The God Who Lost a Wrestling Match

The myth behind the main deity, Takeminakata, is a story of a sore loser who became a local hero. According to the Kojiki, Takeminakata challenged the thunder god Takemikazuchi to a test of strength over control of the land — and lost badly, his arm reportedly crushed like a reed. He fled to Suwa and vowed never to leave, which is where later tradition picks up the story: at the Kamisha, a local god named Moriya supposedly refused to yield and had to be defeated before Takeminakata could take the site, an episode some historians think was reshaped centuries later to echo a famous clan conflict rather than reflect anything from the earliest period. Despite the inauspicious start, Takeminakata's defeat didn't stick. He became a hunting god — deer were sacred to him, and the shrine issued ritual permits that let hunters eat venison without spiritual penalty, an unusually pragmatic accommodation for a Shinto deity — and later a full-blown war god, adopted as a patron by samurai across the country.

Onbashira: Riding Trees Down a Mountain

Every six years, in the Year of the Monkey or the Year of the Tiger, Suwa Taisha holds the Onbashira Festival — and it is exactly as dangerous as it sounds. Teams fell sixteen enormous fir trees in the surrounding mountains, then drag and ride them down steep slopes to the shrine grounds, where they're raised as sacred pillars at the four corners of each site. People have died doing this. It isn't a reenactment or a sanitized tourist spectacle; it's the same ritual that's been staged roughly every six years for centuries, and locals plan their calendars around it generations in advance. If your trip happens to land on an Onbashira year, rearranging your itinerary to see it is worth the effort.

The Pillars Up Close

Each of the four sites raises four pillars, numbered one to four, and a small sign beside each says which it is. These are the second and third pillars as they stand between festivals: bare, pale trunks held upright by a bundle of logs at the base and fenced off with paper streamers. The festival cycle is six years by the calendar, but Japanese counting includes both end years, which is why you will also see it called a seven-year festival.

The third pillar at Kamisha Maemiya stands on open ground against the hills, ringed by a low wooden frame hung with streamers. Look at the base, where a ring of upright logs holds the trunk in place.

The fourth pillar at Maemiya stands on a slope of fallen leaves with bare trees around it, inside a small fence hung with paper streamers. The sign on the trunk reads Maemiya Yonnoonbashira, the fourth pillar of Maemiya.

The second pillar at Kamisha Honmiya, with the roof of the long covered corridor just visible at the left. The sign on the post reads Honmiya Ninoonbashira, the second pillar of Honmiya.

At Shimosha Akimiya the second pillar rises beside a small hall, its sign reading Akimiya Ninoonbashira. Each trunk was once a living fir that had to be brought down a mountain.

The second pillar at Shimosha Harumiya, fenced with streamers, with a shrine building and lanterns on the right. The Harumiya and Akimiya pillars are the ones you can see on the same trip along the north shore.

This one is not at the four Suwa Taisha sites. The sign reads Suwa Jinja Ichinoonbashira, the first pillar of a local Suwa shrine, a reminder that branch shrines of Suwa across Japan sometimes raise their own pillars. I do not know which town this one is in.

Walking the Four Shrines

The four sites feel quite different on the ground. These photos were taken in different seasons and years, and some show repair work, so the scenery you meet may not match exactly.

Kamisha Honmiya: Corridors and Carvings

Kamisha Honmiya is the largest of the four, with its buildings strung along a wooded slope: a gate, a long covered corridor, worship halls and a scatter of small shrines in the trees.

The walk begins at this large stone torii, with the shrine’s name stone at the left and a flight of steps beyond. It is a plain, solid gate, and a good place to bow before you go in.

A second, much more imposing torii, its pillars the green-grey of weathered bronze and a heavy shimenawa slung across the upper beam. Sunlight flares through the cedars at the left, and a roofed gate waits at the far end of the paved approach. The wooden board at the right is a notice of rules for visitors; I can read only the heading, so check it on the day.

The dragon-shaped water basin, a bronze dragon curled over a mossy rock and pouring water from its mouth, with bamboo ladles laid ready. A bamboo fence hung with paper strips runs behind it, and visitors are walking toward the worship halls in the background. The bare ground and light suggest an early-spring visit.

Next to the dragon basin is a second one, fed from the mouth of a carved stone lion. The signpost reads Myojin-yu and, as I read it, says the water is traditionally linked to Suwa Myojin and to the source of Suwa’s hot springs. Rows of sake barrels stand in the background at right.

This is the gate at the head of the Nunobashi, the long covered corridor. A carved dragon coils across the beam, a pair of stone lanterns flanks the entrance and the white curtains carry a leaf crest that I read as the Suwa emblem. The corridor runs away from you toward the halls.

Seen from the gravel below, the Nunobashi is a long, low-roofed corridor raised on a stone-walled bank, with an information board in front and tall cedar trunks standing like pillars in the foreground. Its dark wooden frame lets you look through to the greenery behind.

Stepping inside, the Nunobashi becomes a long wooden tunnel: plank floor, rounded brackets carved with a small crest, white paper streamers and metal lanterns hanging along both sides, and a bright opening far ahead with a couple of walkers. Mossy ground and old cedar trunks show through the open sides.

The Shikyakumon, the four-legged gate, seen straight on. It has a curved shingle roof, a row of white-tipped rafter ends under the eaves, and latticed doors through which you can see the halls beyond. Thatch-roofed buildings with fresh pale timber stand on either side.

The same gate from the side, with a branch of fresh leaves crossing in front of it. Here you can see the paved courtyard on the right and, at the far end, what looks like a large drum under the eaves. The warm, new-looking wood beside the older timber shows how often these buildings are repaired.

The open gravel court in front of the worship hall, with a rack of ema wishing plaques at the right. Scaffolding behind the roof shows that repairs were under way when this was taken, so expect the view to vary with the work schedule.

The Haisho is the open worship hall where visitors stand to pray. White curtains bearing the shrine’s leaf crest hang across the front, and two visitors stand inside. A sign at the right, as I read it, asks that only people receiving a formal prayer use this entrance and others go to the offering place.

Under the eaves hangs a large plaque with a horse carved in relief, still showing flecks of gold. The characters at the top read hono, “dedication”. A dedication in this shape is the origin of the small ema you can buy and write on.

Look up under the eaves and the beams come alive: a dragon with a blue-tinted eye, a bird with spread wings and swirling clouds, carved in deep relief. I cannot tell from the photo which building this is, only that it is among the Honmiya buildings.

The Haiden from the front. A curved central gable carries gold fittings and fine carving, with long, low-roofed wings stretching to either side and a stone lantern at each edge. The deep green forest behind makes the gold stand out.

From an angle you can see how deep the building is: carved beams and brackets under the curved gable, gold-edged stairs and rails, and a tall tree growing up beside it. A wooden stand holding a folded white offering sits in the courtyard at the right.

The sign on this hall reads Chokuganden, which I understand as a hall for prayers offered on behalf of the emperor. It stands on a platform of stone steps in deep green woods, quiet compared with the main halls.

Calm wooden buildings in a quiet corner of the grounds: a raised hall on the left, a long low corridor to its right and tall cedars all around. The Commons title calls them the Chokushiden and the Gokenro, which I take to be a hall for imperial envoys and a related corridor, but I cannot tell the exact roles from the photo.

A tiny shrine to Okuninushi, the god of Izumo, beside moss-covered lanterns. The sign beside it notes that Takeminakata is recorded as Okuninushi's second child. So the loser of the wrestling match has his father's shrine here, and you can read more of that family story in the Izumo Taisha guide.

The Shinmesha stands in a clearing beside a great old tree, with a large moss-covered stone lantern at the right. The name suggests a stable for the shrine’s sacred horse, and a clear paved path leads up to it.

A large rock wrapped in moss and small ferns, roped off with a shimenawa hung with paper streamers and lit by a patch of sun. A stone lantern with a mossy cap stands behind it. I read it as an object of worship in its own right, though the photo does not explain its role.

Kamisha Maemiya: The Quieter Older Site

Kamisha Maemiya is known as one of the oldest sites in the complex. It is smaller and calmer than Honmiya, and it sits a short way off.

The Maemiya torii with its shimenawa, flanked by guardian lion-dogs whose bases are marked with the characters for “offering”. Stone steps climb behind it, and the roof of the Jikkenro peeks out at the left.

The Jikkenro is a long, open hall under a big shingle roof, with latticed sides and paper streamers hanging along the eaves. It is easy to walk past if you are heading for the main steps, so take a minute to look at the roof line.

The worship area at the top of the stone steps, with a thick shimenawa across the front, a white curtain bearing the shrine crest and a stone lantern at each side. Cedars lean in overhead, and the whole place feels tucked into the hillside.

The shrine building at the top of the steps, a shimenawa across its front and cedars all around. The sign in front reads Keidai kin’en, a polite request not to smoke in the grounds.

Shimosha Akimiya: Torii, Bridge and Small Shrines

Shimosha Akimiya is the one with the great shimenawa on the Kaguraden at the top of this page, and it also has smaller things worth stopping for.

The Akimiya torii stands beyond a short arched stone bridge, with lanterns on both sides and the approach stairs rising behind. The stone and bronze look much the same in every season, so it is the greenery that tells you when it was taken.

The Heihaiden at Akimiya from the front: a two-tier central gate-like hall with a curved roof and heavy carving, and long open galleries running to both sides. Three visitors stand at the steps to pray, and the white sign at the right reads Kitosha iriguchi, the entrance for those receiving a prayer, as I read it.

From the side the layering is clearer: a sweeping curved roof over the central hall, gold-tipped rafters and fittings, dense carving below, and galleries stepping away on each side. A wooden stand at the right holds ema and paper cranes. This is the same hall as the photo above, taken on a cooler, overcast day.

A row of small auxiliary shrines in the grounds. As I read the sign on the left, it says Kashima-sha, which would make it a shrine to Takemikazuchi, the thunder god who won the wrestling match against Takeminakata. The racks of metal objects hung beside the buildings are something I cannot identify from the photo.

Two roofed auxiliary shrines beside a paved path, with wooden name boards that, as I read them, include Kashima-sha, Kamo Kamishimo-sha and Yasaka-sha. A metal box in front is marked for fire-fighting water.

A group of three miniature shrines on a stone platform, with a larger one in the middle. The boards beside them read Kotaijingu-sha (for the deities of Ise) and Inari-sha, as I read them, so Ise and Inari are both represented here among the smaller shrines.

Shimosha Harumiya: Carved Eaves and a Round Stone Buddha

Shimosha Harumiya is the more rural of the two north-shore sites. The buildings are modest, but the carving is fine.

The approach at Harumiya is a stone path with a band of cobbles down the middle, running straight toward the Kaguraden under tall cedars. The board at the right announces the Onbashira festival, as I read it.

The Kaguraden up close, its great straw rope slung across the front and an ema rack with plaques and paper cranes at the right. The cobbled path leads straight to the steps.

What appears to be the Heihaiden of Harumiya from the front: a layered central roof with carved decoration and lower-roofed buildings reaching out to each side. There are no grand steps or crowds, which suits the place.

Two visitors stand to pray in front of the Heihaiden. An ema rack and strings of colourful paper cranes hang at the right, so even this quiet site has a steady stream of worshippers.

Looking up under the eaves shows the gilded fittings and the carving around the beams, the part most visitors do not look at. It rewards a few minutes of craning your neck.

The Manji Stone Buddha has a rounded, almost bell-shaped body and a distinctive, rather blunt face. A date for Manji 3 (1660) is said to be carved on it, and stone basins sit in front of it. It is one of the more unexpected things on a walk through the Suwa shrines.

Getting There 訪問の準備

The four sites sit on opposite sides of Lake Suwa in Nagano Prefecture, roughly two and a half hours from Tokyo by limited express train to Kami-Suwa or Shimo-Suwa Station, with local buses and short taxi rides connecting the shrines from there. Seeing all four in one day is possible but tight; most visitors pick the Kamisha Honmiya, the largest and most architecturally complete of the four, and the Shimosha Akimiya, both reachable within a couple of hours of each other. Shimo-Suwa, the lakeside onsen town near the Shimosha sites, is a pleasant place to break the trip with a hot-spring foot bath between shrines — and the same lakeside town that inspired the fictional Itomori of Your Name.

Frequently Asked Questions よくあるご質問

Why don't most of Suwa Taisha's shrines have a main building?
For most of the shrine's history, the deity wasn't thought to reside in a building at all — Mount Moriya, a sacred rock, a living human high priest, or a sacred tree stood in for the god's presence instead. Only the Kamisha Honmiya has a conventional haiden-style structure; the other three sites still worship without a honden.
When is the Onbashira Festival?
Every six years, in the Year of the Monkey and the Year of the Tiger according to the Chinese zodiac — most recently 2022, with the next falling in 2028. It involves hauling sixteen massive fir logs down steep mountain slopes by hand, a ritual with a real history of injuries and fatalities.
I only have time for one of the four shrines — which should I visit?
Kamisha Honmiya, on the lake's south shore, is the largest and most architecturally developed of the four, with the clearest sense of the complex's scale. If you're also curious about the “no building” tradition, pair it with Shimosha Akimiya on the north shore, which still worships around a sacred yew tree rather than a conventional sanctuary.
Is Suwa Taisha connected to the anime Your Name?
Yes — Makoto Shinkai's Your Name draws heavily on Suwa Taisha and the wider Lake Suwa area for its fictional shrine and the town of Itomori, and fans of the film regularly make the trip here as part of an anime pilgrimage.

Three of its four shrines have nothing at the center where a god is supposed to live — which is, in its own way, the most honest answer Shinto has ever given to the question of where a god actually is.
