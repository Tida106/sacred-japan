Kitano Tenmangu: Kyoto\'s Tenjin Shrine, Plum Blossoms, Maple Garden and the 25th Market

北野天満宮

The head shrine of Tenjin in Kyoto: the gate, the oxen, the plum garden, the maples and the market on the 25th.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I send students here first, then everyone else. Kitano Tenmangu in northwest Kyoto is the original shrine of Tenjin, the god of learning, and it keeps the habits of a place that has been asked for good marks, and for good health, for about a thousand years.

It also works as a visit in any season. There are plum blossoms in February and March, maple leaves into early December, and a street market on the 25th of every month. This guide covers who is enshrined, what to look for at the gate and the main hall, what the oxen are about and the practical side: fees that differ between sources, and the bus from Kyoto Station.

How a Man Became a God菅原道真と天神信仰

Discover Kyoto gives the outline: Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), a scholar and statesman, was demoted to a low post in Dazaifu in Kyushu in 901 and died there in 903 without returning to Kyoto. After disasters struck the capital, his spirit was blamed. The shrine was founded in the Kitano area in 947, and he was later deified as Tenman Tenjin in 987 (Discover Kyoto). MLIT calls it the first shrine in Japan to enshrine a real person as a deity, and the shrine says the same.

The shrine says there are as many as 12,000 shrines dedicated to Michizane in Japan, with Kitano Tenmangu as the origin and the main shrine. For the other end of the story, see our Dazaifu Tenmangu guide, and for the Tokyo shrine famous for exam season, the Yushima Tenmangu guide.

The Torii, the Gate and the Oxen鳥居・三光門・牛

From the bus stop you come to a large stone torii with a plaque I read as 天満宮, a stone komainu on a pedestal on each side, and a blue notice board at the right. Two black taxis frame the shot at the edges. The photo is a stretched panorama, and the Commons description does not say which torii it is.

The central gate, the Sankomon, is the one to look at. GLTJP says it was built in the Momoyama style and is an Important Cultural Property. The Commons file calls it the Chumon, which I take to be the same gate. Its name is the “Gate of Three Lights” (Discover Kyoto), and GLTJP lists it among the Seven Mysteries of Tenjin-sama: the “star-missing” gate, which has carvings of only the sun and the moon. Discover Kyoto adds that it carries carved animals and figures from Chinese mythology. In the photo, a stone komainu stands at each side and a curtain with a crest hangs across the doorway; I could not read the crest.

Komainu like this one flank the gate. The lantern is painted with pine branches, and the small round mark on the gable above looks to me like a five-petal plum crest, though the Commons description says only that it is a komainu at the shrine.

The oxen are the signature of every Tenmangu. MLIT says the link comes from Michizane having been born in the year of the ox. Discover Kyoto says the oxen are his messengers and are always shown lying down, as the legendary ox did, and that the largest statue kneels near the central gate beneath a white plum tree. GLTJP says to rub the head if you are praying for academic success; Discover Kyoto lists other body parts for other wishes. Our sacred animals guide covers the ox among the other shrine animals.

GLTJP also lists, among the Seven Mysteries, a Main Hall set off-axis with a smaller shrine straight ahead, the only standing ox, carved into the transom of the worship hall, and a pine where Tenjin is said to descend after the first snow.

The National Treasure Main Hall国宝の御本殿

GLTJP says the present Main Hall was built in 1607 by Toyotomi Hideyori, that it is a National Treasure and that it is a rare surviving example of the Momoyama style. Discover Kyoto instead gives 1607 for the Sankomon and says Hideyori also built the main building, without a date; the prefecture says Hideyori set up the present precinct. I would treat 1607 as the approximate date of the Hideyori work and check the shrine’s own account if the year matters.

The architecture is the interesting part. GLTJP describes it as yattsumune-zukuri, also called gongen-zukuri, and says it became the model for the gongen-zukuri style that spread in the Edo period. Our shrine architecture guide explains the layout, with a worship hall and a main hall joined under a single roof.

This photograph is strongly processed, so the sky and the gravel are more intense than they look on a normal day. I read the building as the main worship hall front, with its red-painted eaves, hanging lanterns and a tall pine at right, but the Commons description only names the shrine.

Up close the eaves are carved and gilded, with golden lanterns hanging along the front. Discover Kyoto says visitors ring the bell, toss in a coin and pray here, and that the carvings are attributed to the legendary artisan Hidari Jingoro, which I would read as tradition, not a documented attribution. In the photo a few visitors read the notice boards at the side.

This winter view shows the front of the hall with its curved gable, rows of hanging lanterns, stacked barrels at the left and a pine at the right, and a crowd gathering before it. Bare branches at the far left are probably a plum, but I cannot confirm that from the file. It is a stretched panorama, so the proportions are exaggerated sideways.

Plum Blossoms and the Tea Gathering梅苑と北野大茶湯

Michizane loved plum trees, and japan-guide says one, the tobiume or flying plum, is said to have followed him from Kyoto to his exile in Kyushu. That is why a plum tree stands in front of the worship hall at Tenmangu shrines. The garden has plums in large numbers: GLTJP and Discover Kyoto say about 1,500 trees of roughly 50 varieties, while japan-guide says nearly 2,000. GLTJP puts the best viewing at early February to late March.

Admission is where sources split. Discover Kyoto and GaijinPot give 700 yen for adults; japan-guide gives 3,000 yen without saying what it covers. Treat both as old or incomplete and check the shrine’s website in the weeks before you go.

On 25 February the shrine holds the Baika-sai, a tea ceremony in the plum grove where geiko and maiko of Kamishichiken serve matcha and sweets (shrine, japan-guide). Discover Kyoto traces it to the 1587 tea gathering that Toyotomi Hideyoshi hosted on these grounds with Sen no Rikyu, a claim I found only there. Our tea ceremony guide has more on Hideyoshi and Rikyu.

The Maple Garden on Hideyoshi’s Earthwork史跡御土居のもみじ苑

In autumn the second garden opens. The Odoi is an earthwork that Toyotomi Hideyoshi built around Kyoto and is a designated historic site (Kyoto Travel, in Japanese); the Kitano section is said to be closest to the original form. About 350 maples colour beside the Kamiya River, and the vermilion Uguisu Bridge is the usual photo spot (Kyoto Travel, Weathernews). The photo above shows a stream channel with a bamboo railing under red and yellow maples, which fits that setting to my eye, though the Commons description names only the shrine. It is strongly processed, so the colours are more intense than a normal day.

For 2026, Kyoto Travel gives 31 October to 6 December, with evening lighting from 13 November. NTA lists hours of 9:00 to 16:00, with 9:00 to 20:00 and last entry 19:40 during the lighting (the Walkerplus 2025 page gives the same last entry), and 1,500 yen for adults and 700 for children with tea and sweets (NTA, Weathernews); 2025 was 1,200 and 600 (Walkerplus). Colour is best from mid-November to early December (GLTJP).

Tenjin-san: The Market on the 25th天神さん（毎月25日の縁日）

On the 25th of every month there is a street market on the grounds and around them. The shrine says it has its roots in the anniversary of Michizane’s birth and death, and that it runs from 6 a.m. until sunset. Japan-guide says stalls sell plants, antiques, tools, toys and crafts, plus festival food such as yakisoba, karaage and takoyaki. Discover Kyoto says the January and December markets are the largest; the December one is called Shimai Tenjin, the last Tenjin-san of the year.

It is the busiest day for the shrine and the worst for cars: Weathernews says parking is not available on the 25th. The shrine’s museum also opens on the 25th, at 9:00 to 16:00 with adult admission of 1,000 yen on the shrine’s page, although Discover Kyoto still lists 300 yen. Treat the shrine’s figure as current.

Visiting Kitano Tenmangu参拝案内

  • Address: Bakurocho, Kamigyo Ward, Kyoto 602-8386. Phone 075-461-0005 (shrine).
  • By bus: City Bus 50 from Kyoto Station to Kitano Tenmangu-mae: 230 yen, about 30 minutes (japan-guide) or about 35 (GLTJP). Discover Kyoto also lists Bus 101, and Route 203 from Demachiyanagi.
  • By tram: Randen Kitano Line to Kitano-Hakubaicho Station, 5 minutes on foot (Weathernews) or 10 (Discover Kyoto).
  • Hours: the shrine’s page has shown 7:00 to 17:00 and 7:00 to 20:00 at different times, with prayer from 9:00 to 16:00. Hours vary by season, so check before you go. Entry is free.
  • Nearby: Japan Travel (JAL) places it near Kinkaku-ji; see our Kinkaku-ji guide.
  • In autumn: our Kyoto illuminations guide and autumn sanctuaries guide cover the evening lighting and other maple spots.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Who is enshrined at Kitano Tenmangu?
Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), a scholar, poet and statesman of the Heian period who was exiled to Dazaifu in 901 and died there (Discover Kyoto, MLIT). After disasters in the capital were blamed on his spirit, the court founded this shrine in 947, and the shrine itself says it was the first in Japan to enshrine a real person as a deity. He is worshipped as Tenjin, the god of learning.
Why are there ox statues everywhere?
MLIT says the link comes from Michizane having been born in the year of the ox. Discover Kyoto says oxen are Tenjin’s messengers and are always shown lying down. GLTJP says to rub the ox’s head if you are praying for academic success, and Discover Kyoto lists other body parts for other wishes.
When are the plum blossoms and the maple leaves?
GLTJP gives early February to late March for the plum garden (its FAQ says mid-March), and japan-guide says the trees bloom from February to March. For maples, GLTJP gives mid-November to early December. In 2026 the maple garden is listed as open from 31 October to 6 December, with evening lighting from 13 November (Kyoto Travel).
How much does it cost?
The shrine grounds are free (GLTJP, japan-guide). The gardens charge separately, and sources disagree on the plum garden: 700 yen (Discover Kyoto, GaijinPot) or 3,000 yen (japan-guide, which does not say why). The 2026 maple garden fee is 1,500 yen for adults and 700 yen for children (Weathernews, NTA), up from 1,200 yen in 2025 (Walkerplus). Check the shrine’s site for the current price.
What is the market on the 25th?
Tenjin-san, a street market held on the 25th of each month on the grounds and in the streets around them. The shrine says it has its roots in the anniversary of Michizane’s birth and death, and runs from 6 a.m. until sunset. Discover Kyoto says the markets in January and December are the largest. Weathernews says parking is not available on the 25th.
How do I get to Kitano Tenmangu?
Take City Bus 50 from Kyoto Station to Kitano Tenmangu-mae: 230 yen and about 30 minutes (japan-guide), or about 35 (GLTJP). Discover Kyoto also lists Bus 101, and Route 203 from Demachiyanagi. The Randen Kitano Line’s Kitano-Hakubaicho Station is a 5 minute walk (Weathernews) or 10 (Discover Kyoto).

Before You Goむすびに

Go with a bow at the gate and a pat on the ox, then pick a season: plum in February, maples in late November, or the 25th for the market. Whichever you choose, read the current fees on the shrine’s site first.
