Naritasan Shinshoji: The Temple Born From a Curse

謌千伐螻ｱ譁ｰ蜍晏ｯｺ

Founded not as an act of devotion but as an act of war 窶・a 10th-century exorcism designed to destroy a rebel who had declared himself emperor, with consequences still quietly observed in Tokyo a thousand years later.

Most visitors arrive at Naritasan Shinshoji the easy way: a twenty-minute train ride from Narita Airport, luggage still in hand, eel restaurants lining the approach street. Few realize they are walking into the result of a 10th-century exorcism 窶・a temple built specifically to spiritually destroy one of the most feared rebels in Japanese history. That history is still alive today, in a quiet, half-serious taboo that many people in Tokyo still observe.

A Temple Built to Defeat a Warlord蜿堺ｹｱ繧帝式繧√◆逾医ｊ

In 939, the Kanto region was in open revolt. The warrior Taira no Masakado had declared himself "Shinno" 窶・the New Emperor 窶・and seized control of several provinces, the first time in Japanese history that a regional warlord had directly challenged the imperial court's authority. Emperor Suzaku responded on two fronts: he sent an army, and he sent prayers.

The prayers were entrusted to a high priest named Kancho Daisojo, a monk of imperial blood who would later become the first Daisojo (Grand Archbishop) of the Shingon sect. Kancho carried with him a statue of Fudo Myoo, the fierce, sword-wielding "Immovable Wisdom King," said to have been carved and consecrated by Kobo Daishi (Kukai) himself at Jingoji temple on Kyoto's Mount Takao roughly 130 years earlier. Kancho sailed from Naniwa, landed on the Boso Peninsula, and made his way to a place called Kozu-ga-hara 窶・modern-day Narita 窶・where he began a 21-day fire ritual (goma) dedicated to subduing the rebellion.

According to temple history, on the very day the ritual reached completion, Masakado was struck in the forehead by a stray arrow during a battle against the forces of Fujiwara no Hidesato and Taira no Sadamori, and died on the spot. The court took this as proof of Fudo Myoo's power. When Kancho prepared to carry the statue back to the capital, it reportedly would not move 窶・as immovable as the deity's own name suggested 窶・and an oracle declared that it wished to remain and protect the people of the region. Emperor Suzaku granted the temple its name: Shinshoji, "the temple of new victory."

An Old Enemy Who Never Quite Left蟆・摩縺ｮ諤ｨ髴・/span>

Masakado's death did not end his story. According to legend, his severed head was displayed in Kyoto, only to fly back toward the east, eventually landing near present-day Otemachi in Tokyo, where his grave mound still stands today as one of the city's most carefully protected, and quietly feared, landmarks. Decades later, after a plague swept Edo, frightened residents enshrined Masakado's spirit at a shrine to pacify him: Kanda Myojin, now one of Tokyo's most important shrines.

This is where Naritasan's history collides, awkwardly, with the present. Because Shinshoji's entire founding was a ritual aimed at destroying Masakado, a folk belief has persisted for generations: worshippers loyal to Kanda Myojin should not visit Naritasan Shinshoji, and vice versa 窶・and visiting both on the same trip is said to invite bad luck, since you would be paying respects to a deity and the temple that vanquished him in the same breath.

It is not official doctrine from either institution 窶・but the taboo is well known enough in the Kanto region that some Tokyoites still plan their New Year's shrine-hopping around it, a thousand years later.

The Kabuki Connection That Made Narita Famous豁瑚・莨弱→謌千伐螻ｱ

For centuries after its founding, Naritasan remained a respected but regional temple. Its transformation into a nationally known pilgrimage site came courtesy of an actor. In the late 17th century, the kabuki star Ichikawa Danjuro I, childless and devoted to Fudo Myoo, prayed at Naritasan for a son. When his prayer was answered, he repaid the favor by writing and performing a play depicting the deity himself, drawing enormous crowds in Edo and turning Naritasan's name into a household word among commoners who would never travel to Chiba themselves.

The Danjuro acting dynasty 窶・still active in kabuki today 窶・has carried the stage name "Naritaya" ever since, a living advertisement now well over three centuries old. Few marketing campaigns in history have lasted quite so long.

Inside the Temple Grounds蠅・・繧呈ｭｩ縺・/span>

Naritasan Shinshoji sits on roughly 330,000 square meters of land, and the buildings span nearly its entire thousand-year history. The Daihondo (Great Main Hall), rebuilt in 1968, hosts the daily goma fire ritual 窶・performed multiple times a day, every day, in an unbroken tradition said to stretch back to Kancho's original 21-day rite. Nearby stand several Edo-period structures now designated Important Cultural Properties: the three-story pagoda (1712), Komyodo Hall (1701), where visitors pray for good marriages, and Shakado Hall (1858).

The newer Great Peace Pagoda, built in 1984, houses a hall of Fudo Myoo imagery and a sutra-copying space open to visitors. Behind the temple complex, the landscaped Naritasan Park offers a quieter contrast to the incense and crowds of the main grounds.

Two annual events draw especially large turnouts. The Setsubun-e bean-throwing ceremony each February 3rd regularly features celebrity guests and sumo wrestlers tossing roasted beans to ward off misfortune. The Gionkai festival in early July, with roughly 300 years of history, opens the doors of the Okunoin behind Komyodo Hall and sends decorated floats through the surrounding streets.

Visiting Today: Practical Guide螳溯ｷｵ繧ｬ繧､繝・/span>

Getting there
The temple is a 10-minute walk from either Keisei Narita Station or JR Narita Station, both about a 20-minute train ride from Narita International Airport 窶・making it one of the easiest major pilgrimage sites in Japan to fit around a flight. No car needed.
Hours and cost
There is no admission fee to enter the main grounds. The temple is open from early morning until evening daily. Some special exhibitions inside the Great Peace Pagoda may charge a separate fee.
Best time to visit
Weekday mornings are quietest outside of festival dates. The three days around New Year's bring some of the largest hatsumode crowds in the country 窶・Naritasan consistently ranks among Japan's top three New Year's destinations. February 3rd draws large numbers for the Setsubun-e ceremony, and early July brings the Gionkai festival procession.
How long to allow
Most visitors spend 1窶・ hours exploring the main hall, pagoda, and surrounding buildings. Add 30 minutes for the Omotesando approach street, and more if joining a goma ritual or guided tour.
Connecting your visit
The Omotesando approach street leading to the temple gates is lined with traditional shops and restaurants famous for grilled eel (unagi), a local specialty worth the detour even before reaching the gates. Free guided tours in English are available from volunteer guides at a reception desk near the main hall, generally without reservation for individual visitors. Frequently Asked Questions繧医￥縺ゅｋ縺碑ｳｪ蝠・/span>

Is Naritasan Shinshoji free to visit?
Yes, there is no admission fee for the temple grounds. Some special exhibitions inside the Great Peace Pagoda may charge a separate fee.
What deity is enshrined at Naritasan Shinshoji?
The principal deity is Fudo Myoo, the Immovable Wisdom King, in a statue traditionally attributed to the priest-sculptor Kukai (Kobo Daishi), founder of the Shingon school of Buddhism.
Why shouldn't you visit Naritasan and Kanda Myojin on the same trip?
A long-standing Kanto folk belief holds that the two should not be visited together, since Naritasan was founded specifically to spiritually subdue Taira no Masakado, who is now enshrined as a deity at Kanda Myojin. It is not official temple doctrine, but it remains a well-known superstition in the Tokyo area.
How far is Naritasan Shinshoji from Narita Airport?
About 20 minutes by train to Narita Station, followed by a 10-minute walk to the temple grounds 窶・making it a practical stop before a flight or shortly after arrival.
What is the best time to visit Naritasan Shinshoji?
Weekday mornings are quietest outside of festival dates. The three days around New Year's bring some of the largest hatsumode crowds in the country, and February 3rd draws large numbers for the Setsubun-e ceremony.
How long should I plan to spend there?
Most visitors spend 1窶・ hours exploring the main hall, pagoda, and surrounding buildings. Add 30 minutes for the Omotesando approach street, and more if joining a goma ritual or guided tour.

A Temple That History Never Finished With繧縺吶・縺ｫ

Naritasan Shinshoji rewards visitors who look past the eel restaurants and the airport convenience. The temple was founded not in peace but in urgency 窶・a priest ordered to pray a rebel out of existence, whose statue then refused to leave. A thousand years later, the goma fire burns every single day, the kabuki dynasty still carries the name of this place, and Tokyoites still quietly avoid combining certain shrine and temple visits in a single day. Very few buildings in Japan carry quite this density of unresolved history.

Narita is easy to fit around a flight, and the Omotesando approach is one of Japan's best-preserved temple approach streets. But the grounds behind those gates hold something more than scenery: a story of power, fear, fire, and a strange kind of lasting consequence.
