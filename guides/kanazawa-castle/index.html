Kanazawa Castle: Lead-Tile Turrets, a 1788 Gate and the Park Next to Kenrokuen

金沢城

The Maeda family's seat in Ishikawa: no keep since 1602, but a 1788 gate, turrets rebuilt in wood and lead tile, and a free park beside Kenrokuen.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I tell visitors that Kanazawa Castle is a castle being put back together. The keep burned in 1602 and was never rebuilt, almost everything else burned later, and then the grounds became a barracks and a university campus. Since the park opened to the public, the city has rebuilt turrets, a storehouse and gates in wood, and one real gate from 1788 stands among them.

This guide covers the castle's history, what is original and what is rebuilt, the garden, the practical details and how to pair it with Kenrokuen across the road. Where my sources disagree, I say so.

From Temple Stronghold to the Maeda Seat前田家の居城へ

The hill was a power base before it was a castle. Kanazawa Station's guide says a fortified Buddhist temple, Kanazawa Mido (Oyama Gobo), was built there in 1546 and became the stronghold of an independent “Peasants' Kingdom.” In 1580 Sakuma Morimasa, serving Oda Nobunaga, captured the temple and began developing the castle.

In 1583, after the Battle of Shizugatake, Maeda Toshiie entered the castle, and the Maeda family ruled the Kaga domain from here until the early Meiji period (GLTJP, Kanazawa Station). Japan Experience says Toshiie began construction and that his son Toshinaga completed it. Visit Kanazawa says the Maeda governed Kaga for more than 280 years. Every June the Hyakumangoku Festival parade marks Toshiie's entry into the castle (Kanazawa Station).

Fires, Barracks and a University火災と軍用地

The castle burned several times. Kanazawa Station's guide gives 1602 for the loss of the keep, 1759 for the Great Kanazawa Fire that nearly destroyed the castle, and 1881 for fires that destroyed more buildings. GLTJP, in user reviews, also lists fires in 1631 and 1808, which I have not confirmed elsewhere.

After the Maeda, the grounds became a military barracks and the moats were filled in, according to Kanazawa Station. Visit Kanazawa says the site later served as a Kanazawa University campus, and Japan Experience says the university moved out in 1995. Kanazawa Station says the local government took control of the site in 1996 and that many buildings have been rebuilt and part of the moat restored since. Visit Kanazawa says the site was designated a National Historic Site in 2008.

The Ishikawa Gate石川門

The Ishikawa Gate is the original. Kanazawa Station and Japan Experience date it to 1788, and Japan Experience says it has ishiotoshi holes for dropping stones on attackers. Visit Kanazawa says it was rebuilt in 1788 and designates it, with the Sanjikken Nagaya, as an important cultural property. It gives 1858 for the Sanjikken Nagaya, while Japan Experience says the gate and the Sanjikken Nagaya are the only buildings surviving from the original construction, which does not match 1858 as a rebuild date, so I would not give a single date for the Nagaya.

Commons titles this simply “Ishikawa Gate, Kanazawa Castle.” It was taken in October 2001, around the time the rebuilding described below was finishing, and it came to Commons from Wikivoyage. A bridge with a green lamp post leads toward a white turret on a stone wall at the left and a gabled gate roof at the back. The pink flowers along the railing look like cosmos, though that is my reading. This is a small 1280 by 960 photo that is more than twenty years old, so please expect the planting and fittings to have changed.

Commons labels this “First Gate Kanazawa Castle,” taken at 9:16 in the morning. A black timber gate stands between white plaster walls set with a grid of dark square tiles, and a visitor walks away through the opening in the sun. The cherry tree at the right is in full bloom. I read this as the outer gate on the way in, but I am going by the Commons label.

Commons labels this “Ishikawa Gate of Kanazawa Castle,” and it was taken about a minute after the first-gate photo above. The gate is black timber with round metal studs on its heavy pillars, under a white upper floor with slit windows and a tiled roof. Through the opening you can see a small building that appears to be an information office, with a few visitors beside it. The stone blocks at both sides are cut and fitted tightly, which is a good contrast with the rougher walls elsewhere in the park.

Commons titles this “Cherry Blossom at Kanazawa Castle,” taken at 9:15. A white turret and a patterned wall rise behind a mass of cherry trees in full bloom, and a stone lantern post stands in the right foreground. The turret looks like the Ishikawa Gate area to me, but Commons names only the castle. Bloom timing changes from year to year, so please treat 9 April as one year's example.

Commons gives this the same title, taken at 9:15 as well. A wide stone bridge with pale green railings leads toward a white turret and a gate roof, and the cherry trees arch over the path beyond. The bridge looks like the one in the 2001 photo above, though Commons does not name it. Visitors are stopping to take pictures, which gives a sense of how popular the spot is in bloom.

Taken at 9:09, the earliest of the three. A white turret and a gate roof rise above a long stone wall and a white wall with a grid of dark square tiles, framed by cherry trees. Small signboards stand on the lawn in front. Please read this one as an illustration of how the turret and the wall work together.

The Rebuilt Turrets and Storehouse復元建築

Between 1999 and 2001 the city rebuilt the Hishi Yagura, the Gojikken Nagaya storehouse and the Hashizume-mon Tsuzuki Yagura (Kanazawa Station, GLTJP, Japan Experience). GLTJP and Visit Kanazawa describe large wooden buildings with white plaster walls and flat tiles, and lead roof tiles. Japan Experience says the Gojikken Nagaya is about 90 meters long, and that its name refers to fifty ken, with one ken roughly 1.8 meters. It adds that Kyoto carpenters trained local craftsmen, and that the work followed traditional methods based on original plans.

Commons labels this “Higashi Yagura of Kanazawa Castle,” taken at 9:30. A long white building with a layered turret at the left end stands beyond a wide, open plaza, with pines and cherry blossoms along the horizon. A sign marked 入口 stands near the building's entrance. I am following the Commons label for the name, and I have not matched it to the building names in my sources, so please read this as an illustration of the rebuilt turret-and-storehouse group.

Commons labels this one Higashi Yagura as well, taken three minutes after the wide view. Up close you can see the layered grey roofs, the slit windows and the grid of dark square tiles on the lower wall. A single visitor walks along the base, which gives a sense of scale for a building of this length.

Commons gives the Japanese title 金沢城・菱櫓, meaning the Hishi Yagura, the turret the city rebuilt in 1999 to 2001. The photo shows a projecting lattice window under a curved gable, with a flower crest on the ridge tile and on the round eave tiles. The five-petal mark looks like the plum-blossom crest associated with the Maeda family, but Commons does not say so. The wall below is the same grid of dark square tiles on white plaster you see across the complex.

Kanazawa Station says the Kahoku-mon Gate, the Maeda-era main entrance, was rebuilt in 2010 with a wooden frame and a lead-tile roof. GLTJP says the Nezumi-mon gate and bridge were completely rebuilt in 2020, which is a single-source detail I have not confirmed elsewhere. The interior of the Gojikken Nagaya is open to visitors, and GLTJP says the three buildings are wheelchair accessible.

Commons titles this 金沢城河北門・一の門, the first gate of the Kahoku-mon, taken at 4:23 in the afternoon. A small tiled gate stands between white plaster walls with square openings, and beyond it you can see across to a lawn. At the right is a tall white building on a base of many-sided, tightly fitted stones. Patches of snow are banked at the edges, which is a reminder that the park is open in winter too. The Kahoku-mon is the gate Kanazawa Station says was rebuilt in 2010, as described above.

Commons titles this 金沢城河北門・二の門, the second gate, taken a minute before the first-gate photo. A long white building with a tiled roof sits on a sloped base of fitted stones, with a wooden staircase at its right. The green signboard in the foreground reads “Kahokumon Ninomon Gate,” free admission, opening hours 9:00 to 16:30. That is what the sign said in 2015, so please confirm the current hours.

Commons describes this as the Hashizume-mon, the main gate of the Ninomaru area of Kanazawa Castle, taken on 19 October 2018. The doors and pillars are made of vertical wooden bars strapped with black iron, with round studs and decorative metal fittings, and the underside of the roof shows the timber frame in warm orange. Through the opening, a paved path climbs between lawns and pines. It is a good place to see how much of the rebuilt castle is craft detail.

Commons titles this only “夜の金沢城,” night Kanazawa Castle, taken at 6:55 in the evening on 5 November 2022. A two-storey gate with a grid of dark square tiles glows from inside through its lattice windows, and a wooden bridge with glass side panels and low lights leads up to it. The gate and bridge look like the Nezumi-mon and its bridge, which GLTJP says were rebuilt in 2020, but that is my reading and Commons does not say so. A few visitors are walking through the gate. Evening lighting varies by date, so please check the schedule above.

Commons titles this Nezumi-mon and its bridge, and the photographer has released it under CC0. It was taken from the bridge itself, so the railings run diagonally toward the gate, with small lights set into the deck and glowing along the rails. The gate sits at the end with warm light in its windows and the white edge of each roof picked out. It is slightly soft, as hand-held night shots tend to be, but it shows what you actually see when you walk across after dark. The date is from August 2020, so the lighting may have changed since.

The same evening, from a different spot. The stone wall at the bottom is washed with pink and orange light, and the white walls and green-tiled roofs above it glow in a cooler tone. The dark patterned lower walls under the windows show up well. Commons does not name the buildings, so I treat this as the turrets of the main group of buildings; please do not take that as a firm label.

This is the same gate as the front view above, seen from the side. The lit bridge runs along the left edge, with warm lights under its deck, and the gate stands behind with its windows glowing. Seen from here, the thickness of the stone base is easier to judge. As before, the name is my reading rather than Commons’s.

The moon sits high in a black sky at the upper left, and a lit turret fills the right side, with its dark square-patterned lower wall and layered roofs. The small tiled roof at the lower left is probably part of another structure, but Commons does not say which. Photos like this are only possible if you stay until dark, which is a good reason to plan the visit around the lighting.

Commons labels this “Interior of the Higashi Yagura,” taken at 9:43. It is a long timber hall with exposed beams, a wide plank floor, and rows of small windows that throw patches of light across the boards. Small display stands and a screen stand along the walls, and a visitor is in the distance. I cannot tell from Commons which building this is, so please take it as the kind of space the rebuilt interiors offer.

Gyokusen'inmaru Garden and the Walls玉泉院丸庭園と石垣

Visit Kanazawa says the third Maeda lord began building Gyokusen'inmaru Garden in 1634, that it was destroyed at the end of the feudal period, and that it was reconstructed in 2015. It says there is a light show after sunset on Saturdays and on designated dates. GLTJP says the park is lit in the evening from sunset to 9 p.m., which is not the same as a Saturday-only show, so please check the schedule.

Commons titles this Gyokusen Inmaru Garden, taken on 9 September 2016, and the author has released it into the public domain. Looking down from the slope, you see ponds with arched bridges, islands planted with pines, stepping paths, and a thatched-roof building at the back. Tall city buildings show above the trees, which is a reminder that the garden sits in the middle of Kanazawa. The garden was rebuilt in 2015 according to Visit Kanazawa, so this was taken about a year after.

Commons describes this as Gyokusen’inmaru Garden in Kanazawa Castle, taken on 19 October 2018 at 10:28 in the morning. A single rock sits in the middle of the pond, pines lean over the water, and a stone arched bridge crosses at the right. Rocks and grasses in the foreground frame the view, and two small spotlights among them are used for the evening lighting. A group of visitors is entering the building on the right, which gives some sense of the scale. Behind it you can see how close the modern city is, and a few trees in the distance are already turning. The same photographer has a wider panorama taken two minutes earlier that I do not use here.

The same garden at dusk, a few weeks after the 2015 reopening mentioned above. The sky is still deep blue while the stone wall on the hill is washed in purple, with a touch of red at its right end. A small arched bridge glows orange in the middle, and green uplights pick out the pines. The two night photos by this photographer carry slightly different time stamps, and I cannot say which is which, but the date is the same. The colour scheme is a special lighting effect and will not match every evening.

Same evening, in a calmer mood. The wall on the hill is lit in amber instead, and the pines in front are lit from below in yellow-green, with their reflections in the pond. The bridges are small stone and wooden ones along the path. Compare it with the purple version above to see how much the lighting changes the feel of the same stone.

Visit Kanazawa says the stone walls differ across the site and the oldest are over 400 years old. GLTJP says the stones were placed in particular orientations, reportedly to avoid unlucky directions, which I have not confirmed elsewhere. I would walk the walls slowly, since they are the part of the castle that has never been rebuilt in timber.

Commons titles this “Stone wall of Kanazawa Castle,” taken on 29 September 2006, and the photographer has dedicated it to the public domain. It shows two tiers of wall with different stonework: the lower tier uses rounded, closely fitted stones, and the upper tier looks rougher and more angular. This is the kind of difference Visit Kanazawa means when it says the walls vary across the site. The photo is from 2006, so the planting in front may have changed.

Visiting Kanazawa Castle Parkアクセス

  • Park admission: free (GLTJP, Visit Kanazawa, Kanazawa Station).
  • Turrets and storehouse: 320 yen for adults and 100 yen for ages 6 to 17, free for 65 and over with ID (Visit Kanazawa, Kanazawa Station). Japan Experience gives 300 yen. Please confirm officially.
  • Park hours: 7:00 to 18:00 from March 1 to October 15, and 8:00 to 17:00 from October 16 to the end of February; open every day (GLTJP, Visit Kanazawa).
  • Turret hours: 9:00 to 16:30, last entry 16:00 (Visit Kanazawa, Kanazawa Station).
  • Bus: Kanazawa Loop Bus from the station's East Gate, roughly 18 minutes on the right-hand route and 220 yen (Kanazawa Station); Japan Experience gives 200 yen. About 5 minutes on foot from the bus stop (Visit Kanazawa).
  • Walk: 20 to 25 minutes southeast of the station (Japan Experience).
  • Parking: none (GLTJP, Visit Kanazawa). Lockers are available (GLTJP).
  • Address: 1-1 Marunouchi, Kanazawa, Ishikawa (GLTJP, Visit Kanazawa).

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is there a keep at Kanazawa Castle?
No. Kanazawa Station's castle guide says the castle tower burned down in 1602 and was never replaced. GLTJP says the same in a user review, which adds lightning as the cause, so I would treat the cause as unconfirmed. What you see now is the Ishikawa Gate, rebuilt turrets and storehouses, and the stone walls.
Is Kanazawa Castle Park free?
Yes. GLTJP, Visit Kanazawa and Kanazawa Station's guide all say the park is free to enter. The rebuilt Hishi Yagura, Gojikken Nagaya and Hashizume-mon Tsuzuki Yagura charge 320 yen for adults and 100 yen for children aged 6 to 17 (Visit Kanazawa, Kanazawa Station), while Japan Experience gives 300 yen. People 65 or over are free with ID, according to Visit Kanazawa. Please confirm officially.
What are the opening hours?
The park is open every day: 7:00 to 18:00 from March 1 to October 15, and 8:00 to 17:00 from October 16 to the end of February (GLTJP, Visit Kanazawa). The turrets and storehouse are open 9:00 to 16:30, with last entry at 16:00 (Visit Kanazawa, Kanazawa Station). Hours vary by facility, so please check the official site.
How do I get there from Kanazawa Station?
Kanazawa Station's guide says to take the Kanazawa Loop Bus from the East Gate bus stop 7; the right-hand route takes roughly 18 minutes and costs 220 yen, while Japan Experience gives 200 yen. Visit Kanazawa says it is about 5 minutes on foot from the Kenrokuen Garden and Kanazawa Castle Park stop. Japan Experience says it is a 20 to 25 minute walk southeast of the station. There is no parking at the park (GLTJP, Visit Kanazawa).
Can I visit Kenrokuen on the same day?
Yes. Kanazawa Station's guide says Kenrokuen is directly across the road from the castle, and Japan Experience says it is across the Ishikawa Bridge. GLTJP lists it about 540 meters away, and a reviewer there suggests half a day to a full day for both. Visit Kanazawa does not describe the garden as part of the castle park, so I would treat them as two separate sites.

A Castle Being Put Back Togetherむすびに

Kanazawa Castle is worth a visit if you like to see how a castle is rebuilt. Go through the Ishikawa Gate, step inside the Gojikken Nagaya, walk the walls, and then cross the road to Kenrokuen.

⛩️

The keep is gone. The gate stands, and the rest is being rebuilt.
