Hiraizumi: The Golden City That Made Marco Polo Dream of Japan

平泉

In the 12th century, Hiraizumi was Japan's second city — a Buddhist utopia built from gold mine wealth, home to more than 40 temples. One hall survives, still gold, still containing the mummified lords who built it.

When Marco Polo described Japan in the late 13th century — a country he never visited, reporting what merchants along the Silk Road told him — he wrote of a king whose palace was roofed with pure gold, whose floors were paved with gold two fingers thick, whose rooms and corridors glittered with the metal beyond any possibility of calculation. Historians have never definitively identified the building he was describing. The leading candidate is not in Kyoto. It is in a small town in Iwate Prefecture called Hiraizumi, and it is a hall measuring five and a half meters on each side — smaller than many living rooms — encased entirely in gold leaf inside and out, completed in 1124, and still standing today with roughly 90 percent of its original materials intact.

Inside the hall, beneath three altars of gilded Amida Buddha statues, are the mummified remains of the lords who built it.

The Northern Fujiwara and Their Impossible City奥州藤原氏

Hiraizumi's story begins with a man named Fujiwara no Kiyohira, the founder of what historians call the Northern Fujiwara clan — a family that, through gold mining, horse trading, and trade with the Ainu and continental Asia, accumulated enough wealth and military power to rule the entire Tohoku region of northern Honshu as an effectively independent state for nearly a century.

Kiyohira had grown up in war. The Former Nine Years' War (1051–1062) and the Latter Three Years' War (1083–1087) had consumed his family and his region; he had lost his mother, his stepmother, and his wife to the conflicts. When he finally consolidated power over Tohoku around 1100, he moved his base to Hiraizumi and made a decision that shaped everything that followed: he would build a Buddhist Pure Land on earth, a city of temples and gardens that would serve as the repose of all souls lost in the wars — enemy and ally alike. Not a monument to his own glory. A memorial for everyone.

The temple at the center of this vision, Chuson-ji, had existed in modest form since around 850, said to have been founded by Ennin, the third head abbot of the Tendai sect headquartered at Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei. Kiyohira transformed it from a mountain temple into an imperial-scale monastic city: he built Daichojuin, a 15-meter-high temple housing a 9-meter golden statue of Amida Nyorai. When Japan's first shogun, Minamoto no Yoritomo, later saw it, he was so impressed he modeled his own temple in Kamakura after it.

At its peak, the Chuson-ji complex contained more than 40 halls and pagodas and over 300 monks' residences. Hiraizumi became the second largest city in Japan after Kyoto. Contemporary accounts describe it as a place of extraordinary beauty, its temples and gardens laid out according to Pure Land Buddhist principles, representing paradise made visible.

Of all of this, one building survives from the original 12th-century construction: the Konjikido.

The Golden Hall金色堂

The Konjikido was built in 1124 by Fujiwara no Kiyohira. Almost everything on the interior and exterior, including the Buddhist ritual utensils, was entirely covered with gold — a style known as kai-konjiki (皆金色), or all-gold. The decoration goes beyond the gold: mother-of-pearl inlays, woodwork, metalwork, lacquerwork, and paintings bring together many aspects of late Heian period arts and crafts.

The hall measures roughly five and a half meters on each side and eight meters tall — intimate by the standards of Japanese Buddhist architecture, almost shockingly small given what it contains. The interior contains three altars, one for each of the first three Fujiwara lords, each with a seated Amida Nyorai surrounded by standing Kannon and Seishi Bosatsu, six Jizo Bosatsu, and two Niten statues. Beneath each altar are the mummified remains of the lords themselves: Kiyohira, Motohira, and Hidehira, along with the head of the fourth lord, Yasuhira, whose body was not preserved — his head was returned to Hiraizumi after being displayed publicly by Yoritomo following the clan's defeat.

Japan does not have a widespread tradition of deliberate mummification. The Konjikido's mummies are among the oldest preserved human remains in the country, and their survival in Hiraizumi's cold, humid climate — conditions not particularly favorable to preservation — has long puzzled researchers. Studies conducted in the 20th century confirmed the identities through examinations of the remains; more recent academic work has attempted to understand the preservation methods used, without reaching full consensus.

One theory holds that the gold palace Marco Polo heard about while in China was the Konjikido — that the "land of gold" dreamed about by explorers including Christopher Columbus might have been this small hall in Iwate Prefecture.

Photography inside is prohibited.

The Fall: Yoshitsune and Yoritomo義経と頼朝

The Northern Fujiwara's century of independent rule ended in 1189, and its end is inseparable from one of Japanese history's most famous fugitives. Minamoto no Yoshitsune — the brilliant younger brother of Yoritomo, who had led the Minamoto forces to victory in the Genpei War — was a wanted man. After defeating the Taira on behalf of his brother, he fell from Yoritomo's favor through a combination of political miscalculation and his brother's jealousy; Yoritomo issued orders for his arrest and death. Yoshitsune had spent time in Hiraizumi as a young man under the protection of Fujiwara no Hidehira, the third Northern Fujiwara lord, and returned there as a fugitive.

Hidehira protected him. But Hidehira died in 1187, and his son Yasuhira, under sustained military and diplomatic pressure from Yoritomo, made the fateful decision to attack Yoshitsune's residence. Yoshitsune was killed at Koromogawa in 1189 — the exact circumstances are disputed, but the most widely accepted account has him killing his wife and children and then committing ritual suicide rather than be taken captive. He was 30 years old.

Yoritomo then used the killing of Yoshitsune as a pretext — or perhaps a genuine cause — to march north with a large army and destroy the Northern Fujiwara entirely. Yasuhira was killed by his own retainers; his head was sent to Yoritomo. Hiraizumi's temples were burned or abandoned. The city of gold effectively ceased to exist within a generation of Yoshitsune's death.

The connection to Tsurugaoka Hachimangu, the great shrine Yoritomo built in Kamakura, is direct: the same man who destroyed Hiraizumi was the patron of that shrine, and the wars that created both the Kamakura shogunate and the end of Hiraizumi are the same wars.

Matsuo Basho's Haiku芭蕉の句

Five centuries after Hiraizumi's fall, in 1689, the haiku poet Matsuo Basho traveled to the ruins of the city as part of the journey recorded in his masterpiece Oku no Hosomichi (Narrow Road to the Deep North). Standing in the grass where the city had been, he wrote what became one of the most famous haiku in the Japanese language:

夏草や 兵どもが 夢の跡
Natsu kusa ya / Tsuwamono-domo ga / Yume no ato
"Summer grasses — / all that remains / of soldiers' dreams."

The haiku is about Hiraizumi specifically, about the total disappearance of something that was once, by any measure, extraordinary. Basho's visit helped fix Hiraizumi in the Japanese literary imagination as a place of mono no aware (物の哀れ) — the bittersweet awareness of impermanence — and the haiku is still recited at the site today.

Motsu-ji and the Pure Land Garden毛越寺

A ten-minute walk from Chuson-ji brings visitors to Motsu-ji, a temple that was built by Kiyohira's son Motohira and expanded under Hidehira. At its peak, contemporary sources described Motsu-ji as surpassing even Chuson-ji in scale and splendor. Today, all of the original buildings are gone; what remains is the Pure Land Garden — a large pond, carefully arranged stones, and seasonal plantings designed to represent the paradise described in Buddhist sutras. The garden is the only surviving example of its kind from the Heian period in a form close to its original state, and it is designated a Special Historic Site and Special Place of Scenic Beauty. It is quieter and more contemplative than Chuson-ji, and the contrast between the garden's surviving beauty and the absence of its buildings makes the impermanence theme Basho articulated feel genuinely present rather than literary.

Visiting Today参拝案内

Hiraizumi is accessible from Tokyo via the Tohoku Shinkansen to Ichinoseki Station (approximately 2 hours 20 minutes), followed by a 10-minute local train ride to Hiraizumi Station. From the station, a loop bus (¥150 per ride or ¥400 day pass) connects Chuson-ji and Motsu-ji; bicycles are also available to rent. For visitors who prefer not to plan the transfers themselves, guided day trip tours from Sendai or Tokyo cover Chuson-ji, Motsu-ji, and the surrounding UNESCO sites in a single itinerary.

Chuson-ji
Open daily 8:30–17:00 (16:30 November through February). Admission to the Konjikido and treasure hall is ¥1,000 for adults.
Motsu-ji
Open daily 8:30–17:00. Admission is ¥700.
Fujiwara Festival
Held annually in May, with historical processions in period costume and Noh performances on the temple grounds.
Getting there
Tohoku Shinkansen from Tokyo Station to Ichinoseki Station (about 2 hours 20 minutes), then the JR Tohoku Main Line for 10 minutes to Hiraizumi Station. A loop bus connects the station to Chuson-ji and Motsu-ji.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What are the mummies at Hiraizumi?
The Konjikido (Golden Hall) at Chuson-ji Temple contains the mummified remains of three Northern Fujiwara lords — Kiyohira, Motohira, and Hidehira — beneath three golden altars, along with the severed head of the fourth lord, Yasuhira. They are among the oldest preserved human remains in Japan.
Is Hiraizumi connected to Marco Polo's description of Japan?
Possibly. Marco Polo described a Japanese palace roofed with pure gold in his Travels (c. 1300), a description he gathered from merchants in China. Historians have not definitively identified the building, but the Konjikido — entirely covered in gold leaf inside and out — is a leading candidate for what those accounts were describing.
What happened to Hiraizumi?
At its 12th-century peak, Hiraizumi was Japan's second largest city, with over 40 temples. It was effectively destroyed by Minamoto no Yoritomo in 1189, following the death of Minamoto no Yoshitsune, who had taken refuge there. Only the Konjikido survived from the original complex.
Why did Matsuo Basho visit Hiraizumi?
Basho traveled to Hiraizumi in 1689 as part of the journey recorded in Oku no Hosomichi (Narrow Road to the Deep North). Standing in the ruins of the city, he composed the haiku "Natsu kusa ya / Tsuwamono-domo ga / Yume no ato" — "Summer grasses — all that remains of soldiers' dreams" — which became one of the most famous poems in Japanese literature.
How do I get to Hiraizumi from Tokyo?
Take the Tohoku Shinkansen from Tokyo Station to Ichinoseki Station (approximately 2 hours 20 minutes), then transfer to the JR Tohoku Main Line for a 10-minute ride to Hiraizumi Station.
Is Hiraizumi a UNESCO World Heritage Site?
Yes, since 2011, under the designation "Hiraizumi — Temples, Gardens and Archaeological Sites Representing the Buddhist Pure Land."
