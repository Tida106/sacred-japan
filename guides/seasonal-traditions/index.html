The Shrine and Temple Year: Seasonal Rituals and the Foods That Go With Them

四季の行事と食

From a ladle of sweet tea over a baby Buddha in April to a bowl of noodles at midnight in December, the sacred calendar of Japan is also a calendar of things to eat.

As a licensed guide, I tell visitors that the easiest way to understand Japan's shrines and temples is to follow the year. A shrine is not only a place where you pray when you have a problem. It is a place that marks time: the turn of the year, the end of the cold, the half-way point, the full moon of autumn. And almost every one of those moments has a food attached to it, something small and symbolic that people eat to make the ritual part of their body as well as their mind.

This guide walks through that year in four seasons. For each one I have picked the rites I think are the most rewarding to see, and the foods that go with them. They are not the grand festivals, which have their own guides. They are the quieter customs that happen in ordinary neighbourhoods, and that you can often join as a visitor.

Spring: A Ladle of Sweet Tea for a Baby Buddha春の行事

The great spring ritual of Japan's shrines is, of course, the cherry blossom, and I have given that its own guide: Cherry Blossoms at Sacred Sites. But if you want a temple ritual that almost nobody outside Japan knows about, go on April 8.

Hana Matsuri: The Buddha's Birthday

Hana Matsuri, the flower festival, celebrates the birth of the Buddha, and it is held on April 8 at most Japanese Buddhist temples, with the exception of those of the Nichiren school. In front of the main hall, the temple sets up a small pavilion covered in flowers (hana-mido). Inside it stands a small statue of the newborn Buddha, one hand pointing up and one pointing down, in a shallow basin.

Visitors take a ladle and pour sweet tea over the statue's head, which recreates the infant's first bath. The tea is called amacha. It is made from the leaves of a particular hydrangea, and it tastes distinctly sweet. Worshippers can drink it or take a small bottle home. It is one of the gentlest rituals you can take part in.

🍵 The guide's tip: Hana Matsuri is held at ordinary neighbourhood temples as well as famous ones, so you rarely need to travel for it. If you are in Japan on April 8, ask your hotel for the nearest Buddhist temple and go in the morning.

Summer: Walking Through a Ring of Grass夏の行事

Summer's loud festivals (Gion, Tenjin, and the others) are in our summer festivals guide. The quietest and most beautiful summer rite happens at the very end of June, and it is a purification.

Nagoshi no Harae: Cleansing the First Half of the Year

On the last day of the sixth month, June 30, Shinto shrines across Japan hold the Nagoshi no Harae, a purification rite that removes the impurities gathered over the past six months. Some shrines begin several days before. The centre of the rite is a large ring of woven grass, the chinowa, set up in front of the shrine's main hall.

The walk is a short choreography. You bow before the ring, pass through it, circle back to the front from the left, pass through again, this time moving to the right, and pass through a final time while you reflect on the past year. In Kyoto, several shrines do it with some ceremony:

  • Kitano Tenmangu: June 25 to 30, with a ceremony from 16:00 on June 30 and the largest ring, about five metres tall.
  • Kifune Shrine: June 25 to 30, with a ceremony from 15:00 on June 30.
  • Nonomiya Shrine: June 30 only, with a ceremony from 15:00.
  • Kamigamo Shrine: June 30, with ring activities from 10:00, and paper dolls floated down the river at 20:00.

Minazuki: The Triangular Sweet of June 30

The food of the day is minazuki, a Kyoto sweet that is eaten on June 30 as a charm against bad luck. It is a triangular cake of uiro, steamed glutinous rice flour and sugar, topped with sweet azuki beans, and it is sold in Kyoto confectioners throughout June. It is commonly believed to have grown from the combination of the purification rite with an old imperial court ice ceremony, the Himuro no Sechie.

🍡 The guide's tip: If you are in Kyoto in the last week of June, buy a piece of minazuki at a traditional confectioner in the morning, and walk through the chinowa at Kitano Tenmangu in the afternoon. It is a perfect small day.

Autumn: Moon Dumplings and Seven-Five-Three秋の行事

Autumn is the season when shrine communities give thanks, and it is the richest season for visitors: the leaves, the great matsuri, and two gentle family customs. For the leaves, see our guide to the sanctuaries of autumn, and for the festivals, our guide to Japan's autumn and winter festivals, which opens with Karatsu Kunchi.

Tsukimi: Watching the Harvest Moon

Tsukimi is moon-viewing, held on the fifteenth night of the eighth month of the old calendar, which falls somewhere between September and October on the modern one. The custom came from China during the Heian period, when aristocrats gathered to watch the moon and compose poems. By the Edo period it had spread to ordinary townspeople, and it had become linked to giving thanks for the autumn harvest.

The table for it is simple. Round white dumplings, tsukimi dango, stand for the full moon. They are displayed with stalks of pampas grass (susuki), which stand for ears of rice and express hopes for a good harvest, and which are also believed to ward off evil. Autumn produce, such as sweet potatoes and taro, is placed beside them, often displayed by a window or in the alcove of a room.

Shichi-Go-San: Seven, Five, Three

On or near November 15, you will see small children in kimono and hakama, accompanied by proud parents, at almost every shrine in Japan. This is Shichi-Go-San, "seven-five-three": girls aged three and seven, and boys aged five. It combines three Heian-period customs that marked a child's growth: letting the hair grow after the toddler haircut at three, boys beginning to wear hakama at five, and girls tying on their first obi at seven.

Families dress up, visit a shrine, and ask for the child's health and well-being, often with a priest's blessing. The sweet that goes with it is chitose-ame, a long, thin candy whose name means "thousand-year candy". It is stretched during production, and its length stands for a long life. Many families now visit on a nearby weekend rather than on the day itself.

📷 The guide's tip: Visitors are welcome to watch Shichi-Go-San, but please ask before you photograph a family, and never block the path of children who are walking in their formal clothes. A smile and a short bow go a long way.

Winter: Midnight Bells, First Prayers and Flying Beans冬の行事

Winter has the densest ritual calendar of the year, and the one I most recommend to visitors. It begins on the last night of December and runs until the first days of spring.

Joya no Kane and Toshikoshi Soba: The Last Night of the Year

On New Year's Eve, temples begin to ring their great bells before midnight, striking them 108 times as the year turns. At some temples, including Chion-in in Kyoto, visitors are allowed to take a turn at the bell. The food of the night is toshikoshi soba, "year-crossing" buckwheat noodles, traditionally eaten while listening to the bells. The long noodles stand for a wish for a long life. You can also eat them at lunch or dinner on December 31.

Hatsumode: The First Visit of the Year

Hatsumode is the first shrine or temple visit of the year. Most people go between late on New Year's Eve and January 3, and the grounds are very crowded. People make wishes for the year, draw a fortune slip (omikuji), write wishes on wooden plaques (ema), buy new charms and return the old ones to be burned. If a fortune is bad, the custom is to tie it to a tree in the grounds and leave the bad luck behind. For the story of how the custom became national, see our guide to Kawasaki Daishi.

The great hatsumode shrines draw millions: Meiji Shrine in Tokyo recorded about 3.2 million visitors in the first three days of January 2010. In Kyoto, the classic choices are Fushimi Inari Taisha and Yasaka Shrine, and in Osaka, Osaka Tenmangu. The meal of the morning is at home: osechi, the New Year dishes, and zoni, the soup with rice cake, often with a sip of spiced sake first.

⛩️ The guide's tip: If you want the atmosphere without the worst crowds, go on January 2 or 3 in the morning, or choose a smaller neighbourhood shrine. For manners at the shrine itself, read our shrine etiquette guide first.

Setsubun: Throwing Beans at Demons

Setsubun falls on the day before Risshun, the first day of spring in the old calendar, so it is usually February 3 (it was February 2 in 2021 and 2025). The ritual is mamemaki, bean scattering. Roasted soybeans are thrown out of the front door, or at someone in a demon mask, while everyone shouts "Oni wa soto! Fuku wa uchi!", "Demons out! Fortune in!", and then the door is closed. Afterwards you eat one bean for each year of your age, plus one more for luck.

The food that has taken over the day is ehomaki, an uncut sushi roll. You eat it in silence while facing the lucky direction of the year. It is said to have originated in Osaka, and it spread nationwide largely through convenience-store and supermarket marketing. Some households also hang sardine heads on holly sprigs at the entrance to keep evil spirits away.

For visitors, the temples and shrines are the place to be. Senso-ji in Asakusa draws nearly 100,000 people on the day, and in Kyoto, apprentice geisha perform a dance and then throw packets of roasted beans to the crowd, a custom you can see at Yasaka Shrine in Gion.

The Year at a Glance年中行事の早見表

Dates change by temple and shrine, and several customs follow the old calendar, so use this list for planning and then confirm with the venue.

  • April 8, Hana Matsuri: sweet hydrangea tea poured over a baby Buddha at Buddhist temples.
  • June 30, Nagoshi no Harae: walking through the grass ring, with minazuki sweets.
  • September to October, Tsukimi: moon-viewing with dango and susuki grass.
  • November 15, Shichi-Go-San: children's blessings, with chitose-ame candy.
  • December 31, Joya no Kane: 108 temple bells, with toshikoshi soba.
  • January 1 to 3, Hatsumode: first prayers of the year, with osechi and zoni at home.
  • February 3, Setsubun: bean-throwing, with roasted soybeans and ehomaki.

How to Visit Respectfully参拝のマナー

  • Watch first, then join. At the chinowa, Hana Matsuri and bean-throwing, follow what the people in front of you do.
  • Check dates. Some temples hold their rites on a different day, and the moon-viewing and Setsubun dates move from year to year.
  • Expect crowds at New Year. Allow extra time, carry little, and keep to the queue lines.
  • Ask before photographing people. This matters especially for children at Shichi-Go-San.
  • Eat the seasonal food where it is sold. Confectioners, temple stalls and supermarkets all carry these items in season, and our regional food guide shows what to try in each area.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is Hatsumode and when do people do it?
Hatsumode is the first shrine or temple visit of the year. It is usually done on the first, second or third day of January, and many people also go in the hours after midnight on New Year's Eve. Visitors make wishes for the year, draw a fortune slip (omikuji), write wishes on wooden plaques (ema), buy new charms and return the old ones to be burned. Grounds are very crowded, so expect queues.
What do people eat at Setsubun?
On Setsubun, usually February 3, people throw roasted soybeans to drive out demons, then eat one bean for each year of their age, plus one more for luck. Many households also eat ehomaki, an uncut sushi roll eaten in silence while facing the lucky direction of the year. The roll is said to have originated in Osaka.
What is the Nagoshi no Harae, and what is the triangular sweet people eat on June 30?
Nagoshi no Harae is a Shinto purification rite held at the end of June to cleanse the impurities of the past six months. Shrines set up a large ring of grass (chinowa) for visitors to walk through. In Kyoto, people traditionally eat minazuki on June 30, a triangular steamed cake of glutinous rice flour and sugar topped with sweet azuki beans, as a charm against bad luck.
What is Shichi-Go-San?
Shichi-Go-San means seven-five-three. It is celebrated on or near November 15 for girls aged three and seven and boys aged five. Families dress up, visit a shrine to pray for the child's health, and give the child chitose-ame, a long thin candy whose name means thousand-year candy, a symbol of long life.
Can visitors take part in these rituals?
Yes, in most cases. Hatsumode, Setsubun bean-throwing, the chinowa ring and New Year's Eve bell ringing at some temples are open to the public. Dates can shift by shrine or temple, and some events are very crowded, so check the venue's official page before you go.

After the Last Bellむすびに

I think of these rites as the way Japan keeps its attention on time. Nothing in them is grand, and nothing needs a ticket. A ladle of sweet tea, a walk through a ring of grass, a bowl of noodles with the bells ringing outside: each one is small enough to remember and old enough to mean something. If you can arrange your trip around even one of them, I promise it will be the part you tell people about.
