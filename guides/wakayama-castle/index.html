Wakayama Castle: The Kishu Tokugawa Seat, a Linked Keep and the Slanted Bridge

和歌山城

The Kishu Tokugawa seat, a rebuilt linked keep and a slanted bridge corridor, a short bus ride from the station.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I describe Wakayama Castle to visitors as the castle of a family that nearly ruled Japan. Ieyasu’s tenth son was sent here in 1619 to found the Kishu Tokugawa, one of the three branches of the shogun’s own clan, and the white keep on its small hill still looks the part. It is a postwar replica, but it comes with a stone-wall puzzle, a roofed bridge that slopes down a hillside and a garden built by that first lord.

This guide covers the castle’s history, what you can see today, and the practical details. Where my sources disagree I say so, and I mark claims I found in only one place.

From Hidenaga to the Kishu Tokugawa紀州徳川家の城

The castle began with Hideyoshi. Fun Japan and Japan Experience say Toyotomi Hideyoshi had the castle built in 1585, with his younger brother Hidenaga in charge, and japan-guide says Hideyoshi gave Kii Province to Hidenaga, who built an early version on this site. GLTJP credits the first build to Todo Takatora in its introduction and to Hidenaga in its history section, so I would just say it was begun in 1585 under Hideyoshi’s orders.

This is the keep as you meet it when you climb: white plaster walls under stacked roofs, each with a small gable, and dull green ornaments on the ridges. The outline is that of a castle built to be seen from the town. The photo is from 26 November 2005, and the photographer has placed it in the public domain.

GLTJP says that after Sekigahara in 1600 Asano Yoshinaga became lord, and that work on the linked keep and the Honmaru progressed in his time. In 1619 Tokugawa Yorinobu, Ieyasu’s tenth son, entered the castle and founded the Kishu Tokugawa family, which held it for about 250 years (GLTJP). Japan-guide describes the Kishu branch as one of three cadet branches of the Tokugawa clan, the others being based in Nagoya and in present-day Ibaraki.

Japan Experience says the castle sits on a small hill called Torafusu, about 49 metres high, and that its curved corners follow the contours of the hill. I found that height in one source only.

Commons says this view was taken from Wakayama City Hall. It shows the point of the hill well: the keep and its long white walls sit on a small rise, with the moat and the roofed bridge in front and mountains on the horizon. The hill is modest, but it dominates the flat ground around it. Some autumn colour is starting in the trees.

Lightning, Abandonment and 1945焼失と再建

Fun Japan says lightning destroyed the keep in 1846 and that it was rebuilt in 1850; I found that in one source only. Japan-guide says the castle was abandoned after the Meiji Restoration and many buildings were demolished, and that the grounds opened to the public in 1901 (one source only). In the Second World War, a large bombing raid destroyed eleven important buildings including the keep (japan-guide); GLTJP dates this to the 1945 Wakayama air raid.

The present keep is a reconstruction. GLTJP says it was rebuilt in 1958 with the same exterior; japan-guide gives 1957. Japan Experience adds that the Otemon Gate was rebuilt in 1989 and that only the Okaguchi Gate and the stone walls survive from the original castle (one source only).

Here the keep is set in summer foliage, photographed on 24 July 2021. A smaller tower stands to its right. The thick trees hide the stone base, so the keep seems to float above the green. It is a good reminder that this is a postwar replica on an old hill, in a park.

Commons titles this only as Wakayama Castle, so I am reading what I see: the front of the keep complex, with a smaller tower to the left joined to the main keep, a curved gable over the doorway and a gravel path lined with clipped shrubs. It shows the linked layout that GLTJP describes, where the main keep and a smaller tower are connected. Photographed on 13 June 2009.

What You Can See Today見どころ

The keep is a white three-storey building connected to a smaller tower by a connecting turret (tamon yagura), according to GLTJP, which calls it one of Japan’s three great linked-tower keeps. Fun Japan names Himeji and Matsumoto as the other two, whereas other lists I know name Himeji and Matsuyama, so I would not rely on any one list. Inside, GLTJP says there are arms, armour and Kishu Tokugawa documents, and japan-guide says the top floor has an observation deck over the city.

Commons describes this as a low-angle view of the keep from 23 February 2020. The camera looks almost straight up the white wall, past a drum-shaped bulge at one corner, to the stacked gables and the green ornaments. In February the trees are bare, so nothing hides the structure.

Commons lists this under 曲輪 (kuruwa, an enclosure within the castle), photographed on 29 November 2010. A long white wall with slit windows runs along a stone base toward the keep, and a notice board stands at the lower right. The red leaves at the right edge match the late-November colour that GLTJP suggests. I did not confirm exactly where this stands.

Photographed on 23 February 2020, the same day as the low-angle view above. Bare branches cross the frame, and the keep rises over its stone base with a dark boulder at the right. In winter the white walls and the blue sky are at their sharpest, and the stone base shows its mix of green and brown stones.

Same photographer and day. I read this as a gate with a turret above it, though I have not matched it to a named gate, so I do not name it. The dark roof tiles, the green ornaments on the ridges and the stones in orange, green and grey are all clear. The stone work is the part I would walk close to.

This is the view from the upper part of the keep, taken on 23 February 2020, with the green copper ridge ornament in the foreground. Houses, trees and low hills spread out to the horizon, and at the right edge I can make out a small red torii and steps among the trees. This is the view the observation floor gives you.

Commons says the photographer stood at the site of the Honmaru Palace (本丸御殿跡) on 23 November 2018. The palace is gone, and what remains is open ground with a view up to the keep and its neighbouring tower. The leaves nearby are starting to turn.

Commons names this the Inui Turret (乾櫓), photographed on 23 November 2008 from a high point. You see the layered grey roofs, the white plaster beneath and a bright patch of autumn leaves in the foreground, with the modern city behind. The old roof tiles and the town together give a sense of how close the castle sits to the streets.

Commons labels this the turret over the Ninomon Gate (二の門櫓門), photographed on the same day. It is an upright picture of stepped tile roofs with small gables, looking down on the town. It is a useful picture for the detail in the roof tiles, with their rounded ends, which are hard to see from the ground.

The slanted bridge corridor is my favourite part. GLTJP says it is a roofed corridor linking Nishinomaru with the Ninomaru Ooku, that in the Edo period only a limited number of people such as the lord could use it, and that it slopes at about 11 degrees with non-slip steps. It was restored in 2006 from Edo-period drawings and you can cross it freely.

Commons titles this only as Wakayama Castle. It shows the sloping roofed bridge crossing the moat, with the keep high above. The bridge slopes down from left to right, so it does not run level. The yellow of the trees suggests early December. The bridge was restored in 2006, and this photo was taken two years later.

The stone walls carry a puzzle. GLTJP says about 170 types and more than 2,100 mason’s marks have been identified, on tops, bottoms and sides, with circles, triangles, family crests and names, and that their meaning is unclear. If you look closely as you climb, you will start to spot them.

This is a wide, paved path beside a high rough stone wall, photographed on 13 June 2009. Commons gives only the castle name, so I do not know which part of the grounds it is. It is a good example of the walls that line the approach, built from uneven stones in rough courses.

A rough stone path climbs beside mossy walls, with two visitors at the top. A small label on a tree reads ヒノキ (hinoki, cypress). The stones are of different sizes and shapes, which is the look of the walls I mention above. Commons does not say where in the grounds this is.

Photographed on 23 February 2020. A calm moat runs between a high stone wall with a sharp corner and a lower wall along the bank, and the pines are reflected in the water. Bands of a straw colour are wound around some of the trunks, but I do not know their purpose. The walls here are good for seeing how the stones are laid in rough courses.

Commons names this the Okaguchi Gate (岡口門), photographed on 16 August 2025. This is the gate that Japan Experience says survives from the original castle, and I found that in one source only. The white upper storey with slatted windows sits on a heavy dark wooden gate, and a large rough stone wall stands beside it. The information board at the right gives the castle map.

Commons names this the Oimawari Gate (追廻門), photographed on 5 October 2007. It is a red-painted gate set between stone walls. I have not confirmed its history, so I treat it as a gate you may pass, not as an original. This is a small picture (640 by 480 pixels), and I have kept it at its original size.

Nishinomaru Garden is a national Place of Scenic Beauty and, according to GLTJP, was created in the early Edo period by Yorinobu for tea ceremonies and Noh. It is a strolling pond garden with a two-tiered pond and a spring-fed waterfall, and it is known for autumn colour, which is why GLTJP also calls it Momijidani. Entry is free (GLTJP, japan-guide).

I read this as the pond in the Nishinomaru Garden, since Commons labels several of this photographer's files that day with the garden's name. A small pavilion stands over still water, and the maples on the left have turned deep red, doubled in the reflection. The date is 4 December 2008, which is within the late-November to early-December window GLTJP suggests.

Commons calls this a distant view of the autumn-leaf garden (紅葉渓庭園 遠景), from 23 November 2018. The same pavilion appears, but now the maples are red, orange and pink, with pines in between and a few visitors on the far path. The colour is more advanced than in the earlier photo, even though it was taken earlier in the month, so it is worth knowing that timing varies by year.

Commons labels this the garden's Momijidani (紅葉渓庭園), 23 November 2008. A small arched wooden bridge crosses above a pond full of rocks, and a few walkers cross it. The leaves are only starting to change, which gives a calmer, greener view than the other pictures. A small signpost stands in the pond.

This is the entrance gate to the garden, as I read the stone marker beside it: 名勝 和歌山城西の丸庭園 (紅葉渓庭園), a Place of Scenic Beauty. The moss-covered thatched roof frames a path of stepping stones that leads to bright maples. The notice boards at the left are in Japanese, and Commons gives the date as 4 December 2008.

Beside the garden, GLTJP lists the Koshoan tea house (470 yen per serving, 9:00 to 16:30) and a free small zoo on the grounds. The zoo’s closing days (Tuesdays in June to September and December to February) are from GLTJP alone, so please check before you plan around it.

On the south side of the park, GLTJP and Japan Experience both mention the Museum of Modern Art, and GLTJP adds the Wakayama Prefectural Museum, which it says opened in 1994 and was designed by Kisho Kurokawa.

Visiting Wakayama Castleアクセス

  • Keep: 410 yen for adults and 200 yen for elementary and junior high students (GLTJP, japan-guide, Fun Japan); Japan Experience lists an older 350 yen, and japan-guide mentions a higher figure from April 2025 that I could not confirm. Please check the day’s price.
  • Hours: 9:00 to 17:30, last entry 17:00; closed 29 to 31 December (GLTJP, japan-guide). Japan Experience lists 9:00 to 16:30, which looks out of date.
  • Nishinomaru Garden: free, 9:00 to 17:00 (GLTJP); japan-guide gives last entry 16:45.
  • From JR Wakayama Station: bus route 0 or 25 to Wakayama-jo-mae in about 5 to 6 minutes (GLTJP), 230 yen (japan-guide). Walking takes 20 to 30 minutes depending on the source.
  • From Nankai Wakayamashi Station: bus in 5 minutes for 220 yen (japan-guide), or a walk of 10 minutes (Fun Japan, Japan Experience) to 15 to 20 minutes (japan-guide).
  • Taxi: about 10 minutes and about 1,000 yen from either station (japan-guide).
  • Time needed: my own estimate is an hour for the keep and the corridor bridge, and another hour for the garden and a museum.

Making It a Trip滞在

Wakayama is an easy day trip from Osaka. Our Osaka Castle guide covers Hideyoshi’s own castle, and our Himeji Castle guide covers the original linked-keep castle that Wakayama is often compared with.

Cherry blossoms and colour: GLTJP says about 600 trees, mainly Somei Yoshino, bloom on the grounds, with a festival from late March to early April and evening illumination from sunset to 23:00. It recommends late November to early December for the autumn leaves.

Commons says this is the skyline of Wakayama viewed from the castle, uploaded to Flickr on 8 April 2007 (the date is the upload date and not necessarily the photo date). Cherry trees in full bloom fill the court between the tiled roofs of the buildings, and a tower of the castle rises behind them, with the city and the river in the haze. It gives a good idea of how the blossom sits within the castle, not just around it.

Commons has this as a night view from 4 April 2017. The keep and its tower glow white on the dark hill, and below them a row of red lanterns and pale blossoms in the trees suggest cherry season. The lawn in front is a good place to stand for this view. I have not confirmed the exact illumination hours for any year; GLTJP gives sunset to 23:00 in season, which is worth checking before you go.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is the keep at Wakayama Castle original?
No. The keep was destroyed in the 1945 air raid and the present one is a ferroconcrete replica. GLTJP gives the rebuild as 1958 with the same exterior; japan-guide says 1957. Fun Japan adds that an earlier keep was destroyed by lightning in 1846 and rebuilt in 1850 (one source only). Only the stone walls and a few gates survive from the original castle.
How much does it cost to visit?
The keep is 410 yen for adults and 200 yen for elementary and junior high students (GLTJP, japan-guide, Fun Japan), open 9:00 to 17:30 with last entry at 17:00 and closed 29 to 31 December. An older listing on Japan Experience says 350 yen, and japan-guide also mentions a much higher figure from April 2025 that I could not confirm, so please check the day’s price. The grounds and Nishinomaru Garden are free (GLTJP, japan-guide).
What is the slanted bridge corridor?
It is a roofed corridor bridge that links the Nishinomaru area with the Ninomaru Ooku (GLTJP). GLTJP says that in the Edo period only a limited number of people, such as the lord, could use it, that it slopes at about 11 degrees and has steps to stop people slipping, and that it was restored in 2006 from Edo-period drawings. You can walk across it for free.
When are the cherry blossoms and autumn leaves?
GLTJP says about 600 trees, mainly Somei Yoshino, bloom on the grounds, with a festival from late March to early April and evening illumination from sunset to 23:00. The same page says the autumn colours in Nishinomaru Garden are best from late November to early December. Both dates move with the weather.
How do I get there?
From JR Wakayama Station, GLTJP says to take Wakayama Bus route 0 or 25 to the Wakayama-jo-mae stop, about 5 to 6 minutes; japan-guide gives 5 minutes and 230 yen. From Nankai Wakayamashi Station, japan-guide says a bus takes 5 minutes (220 yen) and Fun Japan and Japan Experience say about a 10-minute walk. Walking from JR Wakayama Station is 20 to 30 minutes depending on the source.
How long should I allow?
That is my own estimate: about an hour for the keep and the corridor bridge, and another hour if you add Nishinomaru Garden and the museums beside the park. GLTJP and Japan Experience both mention the Museum of Modern Art, and GLTJP also mentions the Wakayama Prefectural Museum.

Before You Cross the Bridgeむすびに

Go for the corridor bridge, the marked stones and the garden, and treat the keep as a handsome postwar copy of a Tokugawa original. Walk the slope the way the lord did, then come back down through the autumn trees.
