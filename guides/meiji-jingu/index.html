Meiji Jingu: The 150-Year Artificial Forest

明治神宮

Japan’s iconic sacred sites are often presented to the world as symbols of untouched nature or pure, organic faith. However, academic analysis reveals that these spaces were engineered through raw state power and cutting-edge science. Tokyo's Meiji Jingu is a highly calculated, artificial masterwork born from political imperative.

The Myth of "Untouched Nature"

In the heart of Tokyo lies the dense, silent forest of Meiji Jingu, which many visitors believe to be an ancient, primeval woodland. In truth, this 700,000-square-meter sanctuary is a complete artificial construction—a highly calculated "climax forest" engineered from scratch by early 20th-century scientists.

To create a self-sustaining ecosystem that would eventually require zero human intervention, a team of forestry scientists led by Honda Seiroku engineered a brilliant, four-stage ecological simulation spanning 150 years. This scientific vision, however, sparked a fierce clash with political authority. Prime Minister Okuma Shigenobu demanded a majestic avenue of Japanese Cedar (Sugi) and publicly mocked the scientists' plan to plant broadleaf trees.

The scientists fought back and won using hard ecological data, proving that Yoyogi’s dry Kanto loam soil and the sulfur and soot from nearby train lines would choke the pollution-sensitive cedars.

Imperial Megalogistics

Executing this scientific blueprint required an empire-wide logistics network:

  • Over 100,000 trees of 350 different species were gathered from across the Japanese Empire.
  • A total of 110,000 youth association volunteers from across Japan were mobilized for the massive manual labor of planting the forest.
  • The towering Otorii (Great Torii) gate was constructed using a 1,200-year-old Taiwan Cypress (Taiwan Hinoki) harvested from Mount Ali and donated by the Governor-General of Taiwan.

The Man-Made Eternity
When you wander through Meiji Jingu, you are not looking at a primeval forest. You are standing inside a hyper-calculated, modern scientific simulation designed to fabricate an eternal myth for a rising empire.

Who Is Enshrined Here祀られているのは誰か

Meiji Jingu is dedicated to the spirits of Emperor Meiji, who reigned from 1867 to 1912 and oversaw Japan's transformation from an isolated feudal state into an industrial power, and his consort, Empress Shoken. When Emperor Meiji died in 1912, the grief was national and the question of how to honor him was immediate. Tokyo had no imperial shrine of comparable stature, and a movement of citizens, politicians, and former retainers pushed for one to be built in the capital he had shaped, rather than beside his tomb in Kyoto.

The chosen site was an area of Yoyogi that had once been part of a feudal lord's estate and later an imperial experimental agricultural ground connected with the imperial couple. Construction drew on donations and volunteer labor from across the country and the empire, and the shrine was formally consecrated on November 1, 1920 — a date still marked today as the anniversary of its founding.

Burned in 1945, Rebuilt in 1958戦火と再建

The forest that scientists spent decades engineering survived the war largely intact, but the shrine buildings did not. Allied air raids over Tokyo in May 1945 burned the main hall and its surrounding structures to the ground, leaving the carefully grown woodland standing around a ruin.

Rebuilding a national shrine in a defeated, occupied country took more than a decade. Donations arrived from ordinary citizens across Japan and from Japanese communities abroad, and the reconstructed shrine buildings — built, like the originals, in the unadorned nagare-style associated with Shinto architecture — were completed and re-dedicated in October 1958. What visitors walk through today is this postwar reconstruction, standing inside a forest that is itself more than a century old.

Reinforced Concrete in a Wooden Shrine
Not every building at Meiji Jingu is wood. The Treasure Hall (Homotsu-den), completed in 1921 to hold items associated with Emperor Meiji and Empress Shoken, was one of the first reinforced-concrete structures built in Japan and was designated an Important Cultural Property in 2011 for exactly that reason — it is treated as a landmark of early modern Japanese engineering as much as a shrine building.

Reading the Approach: Camphors, Sake, and the Otorii参道の見どころ

The walk from the station to the main hall is not empty space between attractions; nearly everything along it was placed deliberately and carries its own story.

The Meoto Kusu, the "Married Camphors"

Just past the second torii, two camphor trees lean toward each other, their canopies touching overhead and a shimenawa rope strung between their trunks. Donated in 1920 and now roughly 17 meters tall, they are known as the Meoto Kusu, or "married camphors," and are popularly associated with marital harmony and good relationships — a frequent stop for couples and for anyone praying for a good match.

The Sake and Wine Barrels

Along the main approach, two very different offerings face each other. On one side, stacked barrels of sake (kazaridaru) are donated each year by sake breweries from across Japan as an offering to the enshrined spirits. On the other, barrels of French wine are displayed, donated by Burgundy's wine growers' association — a direct, deliberate echo of Emperor Meiji's own embrace of Western culture and diplomacy during his reign.

The Great Torii

The Otorii standing at the entrance to the inner precinct is built from Taiwan cypress said to be more than 1,500 years old, harvested from the slopes of Mount Ali and donated by the Governor-General of Taiwan during the shrine's original construction. At roughly 12 meters tall with a top beam spanning about 17 meters, it is widely described as the largest wooden myojin-style torii gate in Japan, and it has twice been rebuilt in kind after wood decay, most recently in the 1970s, always using the same cypress species and proportions.

The Inner Garden and Kiyomasa's Well御苑と清正井

Tucked inside the forest, reached by its own gated path, the Inner Garden (Gyoen) predates the shrine itself — it was a private garden on the Yoyogi estate, and Emperor Meiji had an iris garden planted there for Empress Shoken, who was fond of the flowers. It charges a small separate admission (around ¥500) on top of the shrine's free entry, and it rewards the detour: roughly 1,500 irises bloom in June, azaleas color the paths in late April and May, and maples turn in November.

At the garden's far end sits Kiyomasa's Well, a small, clear spring traditionally said to have been dug by the 16th-century warlord and engineer Kato Kiyomasa, who is thought to have maintained a residence on the site generations before the shrine existed. In recent decades the well became famous as a "power spot," with visitors lining up to see it after it was featured on Japanese television as a source of good fortune — a modern legend layered onto a garden built for an empress a century earlier.

Visiting Meiji Jingu明治神宮を訪ねる

Meiji Jingu sits beside Harajuku and Yoyogi stations in central Tokyo, so one of the most atmospheric walks in the city begins a few steps from one of its busiest crossings. Admission to the shrine grounds is free, and the grounds are open from sunrise to sunset, with closing times shifting through the year.

Getting There

Harajuku Station on the JR Yamanote Line is the closest to the main gate and the Great Torii. Meiji-jingumae Station (Tokyo Metro Chiyoda and Fukutoshin lines) and Yoyogi Station also open onto the grounds. From the Great Torii, allow ten to fifteen minutes to walk the wide gravel approach to the main hall at an easy pace.

Beyond the Main Hall

The Meiji Jingu Museum, opened in 2019, displays items related to the imperial couple in a modern building designed by architect Kengo Kuma. The Treasure Hall further into the forest, and the Kagura-den hall built in 1991 for ceremonies and performances, round out the grounds for visitors with extra time. In October, the shrine hosts a Doll Festival exhibition of imperial dolls and artifacts for a separate fee, and a Spring Festival in late April into May brings traditional performing arts to the grounds.

Weddings and Festivals

Traditional Shinto wedding processions, with the bride in white and a priest leading, cross the gravel path on many weekends. Step aside and watch quietly rather than photographing at close range. The first days of January, when Meiji Jingu draws the largest hatsumode crowds of any shrine in Japan — several million visitors over the New Year period — are best avoided if you want to enjoy the forest in peace. Early morning is the quietest time on any other day.

How to Pray

At the main hall, the usual shrine sequence applies: bow, toss a coin, bow twice, clap twice, pray, and bow once. The full routine, including the purification basin, is explained in our Shrine Etiquette guide.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Meiji Jingu free to visit?
Yes, the forest and the main shrine buildings are free to enter. The Inner Garden (Gyoen) charges a small separate fee, as does the Treasure Museum and the Meiji Jingu Museum.
Is the forest at Meiji Jingu natural?
No. It was planted from scratch starting in 1915, using roughly 100,000 trees of around 350 species donated from across Japan and its former empire, and engineered by forestry scientists to become a self-sustaining forest within about 150 years with no further human planting.
Was Meiji Jingu destroyed in World War II?
Yes. Allied air raids in May 1945 burned the main shrine buildings to the ground, though the forest itself survived largely intact. The shrine was rebuilt through nationwide and international donations and was re-dedicated in 1958.
Who is enshrined at Meiji Jingu?
Emperor Meiji, who reigned from 1867 to 1912 and oversaw Japan's rapid modernization, and his consort Empress Shoken. The shrine was built on land once used by the imperial family and associated with the couple, after their deaths.
What is Kiyomasa's Well?
A spring-fed well inside the Inner Garden traditionally said to have been dug by the 16th-century warlord Kato Kiyomasa. It has become known as a "power spot" in modern Japan, and visitors often line up to view it, especially after it gained attention through popular media.
What is the best time to visit Meiji Jingu?
Early morning on any weekday is quietest. The Inner Garden's irises peak in June and its azaleas in late April to May, with maples turning in November. The first days of January bring the largest hatsumode crowds of any shrine in Japan, so visit later in the month for a calmer walk.

A Forest Still Growing Into Its Own Mythむすびに

Meiji Jingu asks visitors to hold two true things at once: the forest is completely artificial, planted tree by tree by scientists who fought a prime minister to get the species right, and it is also, after a century, a real and self-sustaining ecosystem that gives Tokyo one of its quietest places. The shrine itself burned down and was rebuilt, the camphors were planted to grow into each other, and the well in the garden became famous for reasons that have nothing to do with why it was dug.

None of this makes it less sacred — if anything, it is a useful reminder that in Japan, as elsewhere, tradition is often something people build and then grow into, rather than something simply inherited whole.
