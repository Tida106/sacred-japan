Kawasaki Daishi: The Temple That Invented Hatsumode

川崎大師 平間寺

Every January, tens of millions of people make a hatsumode — a first shrine or temple visit of the New Year. The modern form of that custom traces back almost entirely to one temple in Kawasaki and the railway company that needed passengers.

Every January, tens of millions of people across Japan make a "hatsumode" — a first shrine or temple visit of the New Year — treating it as an ancient, unbroken tradition. It isn't. The modern custom, where anyone visits any shrine or temple regardless of family sect or local parish, is barely older than the light bulb, and it traces back almost entirely to one temple in Kawasaki and the railway company that needed passengers.

A Fisherman's Unlucky Year漁師の厄年と創建

The temple's own founding story begins centuries earlier, with a fisherman named Hirama Kanenori. According to temple history, Kanenori was living through his 42nd year — traditionally one of the most inauspicious ages for a man in Japanese yakudoshi (unlucky-year) belief — and had been wrongly accused of a crime. A dream told him to retrieve something from the sea. He did, and pulled up a small wooden statue, which he began to venerate at home. Soon afterward, his name was cleared and he returned safely to his hometown.

Word of the statue's power spread, and in 1128, an itinerant priest named Sonken — who happened to be lodging at Kanenori's house and heard the story firsthand — helped him build a temple to house it properly. They identified the statue as an image of Kukai, posthumously known as Kobo Daishi, the founder of Japan's Shingon school of Buddhism. The temple took its formal name, Heikenji, from Kanenori's surname: Hirama becomes "Hei," and -kenji simply means temple.

It's a story with an unmistakable echo: Tokyo's Senso-ji, the city's oldest and most famous temple, has its own founding legend about fishermen pulling a sacred statue from a river. Whether one inspired the other or both reflect a wider Kanto-region tradition of "objects found in water are objects sent by the gods," the parallel is hard to miss for anyone visiting both temples.

How a Shogun Made It Famous将軍の参拝

Kawasaki Daishi's specific founding story — a man freed from false accusation in his unluckiest year, after devoting himself to this exact statue — made the temple Japan's natural address for yakuyoke prayers: rituals to ward off the bad luck associated with yakudoshi years (traditionally 25, 42, and 60 for men; 19, 33, and 60 for women, plus the years immediately before and after). The temple's reputation grew steadily through the medieval period, but its decisive break into national fame came in the Edo period, when the eleventh Tokugawa shogun, Ienari, made a pilgrimage here specifically for yakuyoke prayers.

A shogunal visit was effectively a celebrity endorsement with the weight of the state behind it, and Edo's townspeople began visiting Kawasaki Daishi in droves soon after. Today the temple is still known nationwide simply as "Yakuyoke no Odaishi-sama" — the Great Teacher who wards off misfortune.

The Railway Campaign That Invented a National Custom鉄道と初詣の誕生

Before the late 19th century, Japanese religious visits followed a different rhythm entirely. People worshipped on ennichi — specific festival days tied to a particular deity or temple, observed monthly or annually, usually at whichever shrine or temple their family or neighborhood was historically affiliated with. Kawasaki Daishi's own ennichi falls on the 21st of each month, with the January 21st observance known as Hatsu-Daishi, the year's "first Daishi day." There was no broad custom of visiting any shrine or temple, regardless of affiliation, simply because the calendar had turned over to a new year.

That changed in 1899, when what is now the Keikyu Daishi Line opened — Japan's first railway built explicitly to carry pilgrims to a single temple. The line's owners had every incentive to fill those train cars, and over the following years their advertising, amplified by newspaper coverage, encouraged Tokyo and Yokohama residents to ride out to Kawasaki Daishi specifically at New Year's, regardless of their family's traditional shrine affiliation.

What had started as one railway's marketing strategy for a single temple became, within a generation, a defining custom of the Japanese New Year nationwide.

The campaigns are widely credited with popularizing the word "hatsumode" itself, gradually displacing the older term gantan mairi (New Year's Day visit) and reshaping it into something closer to its modern form: an open, sect-agnostic pilgrimage anyone could make to any shrine or temple they liked, often by train. Today, Kawasaki Daishi consistently ranks alongside Naritasan Shinshoji as one of Japan's second or third most-visited hatsumode destinations, drawing millions over the three-day New Year holiday — still arriving, as in 1899, by train.

Rebuilt From the Ashes戦禍からの再建

Kawasaki Daishi's physical buildings tell a more recent story. An American air raid on Kawasaki in April 1945 destroyed nearly the entire temple complex — sparing only a small Shinto shrine within the grounds, which still stands today as the sole structure to have survived the war intact. Everything else visitors see now was rebuilt in the decades that followed: the Dai-Hondo (Main Hall), reconstructed in steel and concrete in 1958; the Dai-Sanmon main gate and the Prayer Hall for Safe Driving, both completed in 1977; and the temple's most distinctive landmark, an octagonal five-story pagoda raised in 1984 to mark the 1,150th anniversary of Kobo Daishi's death.

A Sutra Hall, completed as recently as 2004, finally rounded out the temple's seven traditional structures and is said to mark the formal completion of its postwar reconstruction — nearly sixty years after the bombing.

Nakamise-dori and the Approach仲見世と参道

The walk from Kawasaki Daishi Station to the temple gates is as much a part of the experience as the temple itself. Nakamise-dori, the approach street, is famous for the rhythmic, theatrical chopping sound of tontoko-ame, a hard taffy candy cut to order by vendors performing with knives in time to a beat — a sound so associated with the street that it has been designated an Intangible Cultural Property of Kanagawa Prefecture. Alongside the candy shops, stalls sell Daruma dolls and kuzumochi, a local jellied-starch confection with a slightly fermented tang that has been made in Kawasaki for generations.

In July, the same streets host a wind-chime (furin) market drawing chimes from across the country. On December 21st — the temple's final ennichi of the year — old charms and amulets are gathered and ceremonially burned in a bonfire of gratitude. Visiting Today: Practical Guide実践ガイド

Getting there
Take the Keikyu Daishi Line to Kawasaki Daishi Station, then walk about five minutes to the temple. The Daishi Line connects to Keikyu Kawasaki Station, which is roughly 15–20 minutes from central Tokyo (via Keikyu Main Line) or Yokohama. The very line that invented hatsumode is still the easiest way to arrive.
Hours and cost
There is no admission fee to enter the temple grounds. The grounds are open throughout the day; the main hall has standard daytime hours. During New Year's, the temple operates around the clock for the first several days of January.
Best time to visit
Weekday mornings are quietest. The three days around New Year's bring crowds in the millions — Kawasaki Daishi is one of Japan's top hatsumode destinations. The 21st of each month (ennichi) draws regular visitors, and January 21st (Hatsu-Daishi) is particularly lively. July's wind-chime market and December 21st's amulet-burning are atmospheric seasonal events.
What to look for
The octagonal five-story pagoda is the temple's most distinctive architectural feature and unusual in Japan — most pagodas are square. The small Shinto shrine on the grounds, the only structure that survived the 1945 air raid, stands in quiet contrast to everything rebuilt around it. Stop for tontoko-ame on Nakamise-dori and listen for the chopping rhythm before you buy.
Connecting your visit
Kawasaki Daishi pairs naturally with a wider Kanto pilgrimage. Naritasan Shinshoji is its perennial rival in the hatsumode rankings; both are Shingon temples with deep Kanto roots and equally dramatic crowds in early January.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Is Kawasaki Daishi free to visit?
Yes, there is no admission fee to enter the temple grounds.
What is Kawasaki Daishi famous for?
It is one of Japan's leading temples for yakuyoke, prayers to ward off the misfortune associated with yakudoshi (unlucky) years, and it is widely credited with popularizing Japan's modern hatsumode (New Year's visit) custom through a 19th-century railway marketing campaign.
Is Kawasaki Daishi the same as Heikenji?
"Kawasaki Daishi" is the popular name; the temple's formal name is Heikenji, taken from the surname of its founder, the fisherman Hirama Kanenori.
How do you get to Kawasaki Daishi?
Take the Keikyu Daishi Line to Kawasaki Daishi Station, then walk about five minutes. The line connects to Keikyu Kawasaki Station, roughly 15–20 minutes from central Tokyo or Yokohama.
Are the temple buildings original?
No. Almost the entire complex was destroyed in an air raid in April 1945. Only a small Shinto shrine on the grounds survived; everything else, including the main hall and the octagonal pagoda, was rebuilt between the 1950s and 2004.
What are the yakudoshi (unlucky) ages observed here?
Traditionally 25, 42, and 60 for men, and 19, 33, and 60 for women, along with the years immediately preceding and following each. The year before is called maeyaku, the year itself honyaku, and the year after atoyaku — all three are considered inauspicious.

A Temple That Gave Japan a Traditionむすびに

What makes Kawasaki Daishi unusual among Japan's great temples is that its most lasting contribution isn't a building, a treasure, or a deity — it's a habit. The hatsumode custom that tens of millions of Japanese people observe every January, treating it as immemorial tradition, was largely invented here, by a railway company running advertisements in the 1890s. The temple itself is nearly nine centuries older than that invention, founded on the story of a fisherman in his unluckiest year who pulled something sacred from the sea — a story that echoes, quietly and perhaps intentionally, through the founding legends of temples across the Kanto plain.

The approach street still smells of incense and candy. The crowds still arrive by train. And every January, the cycle begins again.
