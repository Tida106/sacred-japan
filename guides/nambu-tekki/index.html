Nambu Tekki: How to Choose a Real Japanese Cast Iron Kettle

南部鉄器の選び方

The pebbled black kettle on every Japan wish list comes from Iwate, and so do many look-alikes. Here is how I tell the real thing, care for it, and visit the makers.

As a licensed guide, I get one question from guests more than any other about crafts: “Where can I buy a real iron kettle?” They have seen the black, pebbled tetsubin in a tea shop window or on a friend's shelf, and they want one. The honest answer is that a real one is easy to find, and a fake one is easier.

This guide is the short version of what I tell them. It covers what Nambu Tekki is, the difference between a kettle and a teapot, how to check what you are buying, and how to keep it from rusting.

What Nambu Tekki Is南部鉄器とは

Nambu Tekki, or Nambu ironware, is a traditional iron craft from Iwate Prefecture in northern Japan. It is handmade in about ten steps, using molds so that one design can be cast again and again. It was designated a Japanese national traditional craft in 1975.

There are two historical types, Mizusawa and Morioka, and both are now usually called Nambu Ironware. Mizusawa ironware is thought to date to the 12th century, and Morioka ironware developed in the 17th century. The bumpy surface is called arare, meaning hailstone, and each artisan makes their own patterns.

If you are interested in how iron casting reached this region, it is linked to Hiraizumi; see our Hiraizumi guide.

Kettle or Teapot?鉄瓶と急須

This is where most buyers go wrong. A tetsubin is a kettle for boiling water, and it can go on direct heat. A teapot is for steeping tea, and most have a smooth enamel lining inside. They look alike, but they are used differently.

  • Kettle (tetsubin): no coating inside, so it needs seasoning before first use. Boil water in it and pour it out until the water runs clear.
  • Teapot: usually enamel-lined, so a rinse is enough, and it should not be heated directly. Heating an enamel-lined pot on a flame or induction hob may crack or lift the lining.

Some people believe iron from an uncoated kettle seeps into the water and softens the taste. Treat that as a popular belief rather than a proven fact, and buy a kettle because you love it.

How to Spot the Real Thing本物の見分け方

There is no single stamp that settles it, so I check several things together.

  • The mark and the leaflet. Genuine pieces usually carry a Nambu Tekki mark and come with care instructions.
  • The maker. Iwachu and Oigen are well-known makers. Look for a named workshop, not just a brand-like word.
  • Where it was made. A Japan travel article warns about kettles made outside Japan and sold as Japanese Nambu Tekki on big shopping and auction sites, which may use poor recycled metal.
  • The inside. A kettle meant for boiling water is not enamel-coated. If you see a smooth enamel interior, you are looking at a teapot.
  • The price. The same article says simple Iwachu or Oigen kettles cost under about 10,000 yen in Japan, craftsman pieces roughly 30,000 to 100,000 yen, and a famous studio such as Suzuki Morihisa starts around 100,000 yen. A “handmade Japanese” kettle at a tiny fraction of that deserves a second look.

🔍 The guide's tip: On any marketplace, read the full description for “Made in Japan”, the Iwate origin and the maker's name. If it only says “Japanese style”, move on.

Care Without Rustお手入れ

  • First use: rinse with water only, then boil clean water and discard it two or three times. No detergent or abrasive pad.
  • Everyday use: fill to about 70 to 80 percent, heat gently, and use a mitt on the hot handle and lid.
  • After use: pour out the water, warm the empty kettle on very low heat for a short time to dry it, then let it cool and wipe the outside.
  • Never: dishwasher, soaking, heating it empty, or cooling it with cold water.
  • Light rust: a little is usually fine unless the water turns reddish. One maker's guide suggests boiling sencha in it to help.

Induction cookers need a kettle made for them. The kettle must be magnetic and sit flat on the hob, so check the product listing.

Visit Morioka盛岡で見る

To see where it comes from, go to Morioka in Iwate. According to our crafts page, the Iwate Industry Museum in Morioka offers casting workshops by appointment, so please check the official page before you go. Watching a kettle come out of the mold is the best way to understand why the price is what it is.

Where to Buy買える場所

In Japan, department stores, craft shops and the makers' own shops are the safest places. From abroad, specialist tea and Japan-goods shops that name the maker are my first choice. If you shop on Amazon, use the checks above. These search pages are a starting point: Nambu tetsubin kettles →, Nambu cast iron teapots →.

Choose a size you will actually use. Cast iron is heavy, so a kettle that is too big becomes a chore to lift.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is the difference between a tetsubin and a teapot?
A tetsubin is an iron kettle for boiling water and can go on direct heat, while a teapot is for steeping tea and usually has an enamel lining. Kettles are not coated inside, so check the product page and use each one as intended.
How can I tell if Nambu Tekki is genuine?
Look for the Nambu Tekki mark, a named maker such as Iwachu or Oigen, and clear information that it was made in Iwate. Be careful with kettles made outside Japan and sold under a Japanese name, since quality can vary. Prices that look too low are a warning sign.
Can I use a Nambu iron kettle on an induction cooker?
Only if the product is made for induction heating. The kettle must be magnetic and sit flat on the hob. Enamel-lined teapots are for steeping tea, and heating them directly may damage the enamel.
How do I stop my cast iron kettle from rusting?
Keep it dry. After use, pour out the water and warm the empty kettle briefly on very low heat to dry it, then let it cool. Avoid detergent, dishwashers and leaving water inside.
Where can I see Nambu Tekki being made?
Morioka in Iwate is the place to go. According to our crafts page, the Iwate Industry Museum in Morioka offers casting workshops by appointment, so please check the official page before you go.

A Kettle for Lifeむすびに

A good Nambu kettle is something you use every day and keep for years, and it gets better with a little care. Check the maker, check the inside, and dry it after each use. If you are planning a Tohoku trip, add Morioka to the list. For more crafts, see our Japan crafts guide.
