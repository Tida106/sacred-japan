One Day in Nara: A Walking Route Through Japan's First Capital

古都 奈良 · 日帰り旅

Nara was Japan's first permanent capital, its deer have been considered divine for over 1,300 years, and the temples and shrines here were not decorative — they were the spiritual infrastructure of the state itself. A day is enough to walk the main circuit.

Most visitors come to Nara for the deer and leave having seen the Great Buddha as well, which is the right instinct. But the city rewards knowing a little more about why those two things are in the same place. Nara was Japan's first permanent capital, the deer have been treated as divine for over 1,300 years, and the temples and shrines here are not decorative — they were the spiritual infrastructure of the state itself. A day is enough to walk the main circuit, but it helps to know what you're walking through.

Why Nara? Japan's First Capital日本最初の恒久的な首都

In 710, Empress Genmei moved the imperial capital to Heijo-kyo, present-day Nara, establishing Japan's first permanent seat of government. Before this, the capital had moved with each new emperor — a practice tied to beliefs about ritual impurity. Settling the capital in one place was partly a bureaucratic decision: you cannot build a permanent Chinese-style government if the city keeps moving. It was also a statement. Japan now had a fixed center of power, modeled consciously on Tang Dynasty China.

The capital moved again to Kyoto in 794, less than a century later. But in that short window, Nara's rulers built temples and shrines on a scale that was meant to impress both their own people and the continent, and much of what they built still stands.

The Sacred Deer神の使い — 奈良の鹿

The sika deer of Nara are not wild in any ordinary sense. They have lived alongside humans here for over 1,300 years, and for most of that time, harming one was a capital offense — the penalty for killing a deer in Nara was, for centuries, death.

The connection to the sacred begins with Kasuga Taisha, the shrine at the eastern edge of the park. According to shrine tradition, when the deity Takemikazuchi-no-Mikoto was invited from Kashima Shrine to protect the new capital, he traveled to Nara riding a white deer. From that moment, the deer were understood to be the physical messengers of the gods — animals that carried divine presence through the world. They are still classified as a National Natural Monument, and there are roughly 1,300 of them living freely in Nara Park today.

For most of Japanese history, killing a Nara deer was punishable by death. Today they are a National Natural Monument — the same animals, the same park, a slightly different legal framework.

A few practical notes on the deer: 鹿せんべい (shika senbei, deer crackers, around ¥200 per bundle) are sold throughout the park and are the only food visitors should give them. The deer near Todai-ji Temple, where crowds concentrate, tend to be more assertive — they will nip at bags and clothing if they smell food. For a calmer encounter, buy crackers early and feed deer in the quieter northern sections of the park before reaching the temple.

The Route: A Five-Hour Walking Circuit五時間の歩き旅

This walking circuit takes roughly five to six hours at an unhurried pace, starting and ending at Kintetsu Nara Station. The Kintetsu Line from Osaka Namba takes about 40 minutes; from Kyoto, about 45 minutes on the Kintetsu Kyoto Line to Yamato-Saidaiji, then transfer. Arrive early — by 8am if possible — before the tour groups arrive around 10am.

Kofuku-ji (15–20 minutes from the station)

The walk from the station passes Kofuku-ji before anything else. This temple, founded in 669 and rebuilt in Nara in 710, was effectively the Fujiwara clan's private temple — and because the Fujiwara dominated Japanese politics for centuries, Kofuku-ji was one of the most powerful Buddhist institutions in the country. Its five-story pagoda, originally built in 730, is a Nara landmark visible from much of the park; the current structure dates from 1426. The temple grounds are free to enter.

Nara Park and the Approach to Todai-ji (30–40 minutes of walking)

The path from Kofuku-ji leads into the open parkland, past Sarusawa Pond, and gradually north toward Todai-ji. This section is where most visitors have their deer encounters. The Nandai-mon gate, the Great South Gate of Todai-ji, appears at the end of the approach — at roughly 25 meters tall, it is one of the largest wooden gates in Japan, housing two guardian figures (仁王, Nio) carved in the 13th century.

Todai-ji Temple

The Daibutsuden (Great Buddha Hall) behind the gate is itself the largest wooden structure in the world, though it was rebuilt in 1709 at two-thirds the size of the original 8th-century building. Inside sits Vairocana Buddha — Daibutsu — cast in bronze in 752, nearly 15 meters tall, at the order of Emperor Shomu as a gesture of gratitude after a series of disasters including plague and rebellion. The casting required enormous quantities of bronze, mercury gilding, and labor; according to temple records, the project took years and involved workers from across the country and from the continent. Admission is ¥1,000 for adults.

One detail most visitors miss: a large wooden pillar inside the hall has a hole cut through its base, roughly the size of the Daibutsu's nostril. Tradition holds that passing through it brings good fortune; it draws a steady queue of children (and adults) throughout the day.

Kasuga Taisha Shrine (20–25 minutes' walk east from Todai-ji)

The path from Todai-ji to Kasuga Taisha runs through a forested section of the park where deer thin out and the atmosphere quiets considerably. The shrine itself was founded in 768 to protect the new capital, and its most distinctive feature is immediately apparent: more than 2,000 stone lanterns line the approach paths, with over 1,000 additional bronze lanterns hanging in the covered corridors. All of them are lit twice a year — during the Mantoro festivals in February and August — when the entire precinct glows in a light that has been described consistently, across centuries of writing, as transformative.

The outer grounds are free. A modest fee (¥500) admits visitors to the inner sanctuary corridor, where the hanging lanterns are concentrated and where the oldest buildings of the shrine complex are visible. It is worth paying. Kasuga Taisha is also one of the sites with strong 御朱印 (goshuin) culture; the dedicated stamp office near the inner sanctuary offers a distinctive design.

Return to the Station via Naramachi

The return route south passes through Naramachi, a well-preserved merchant district from the Edo period, with 町家 (machiya) townhouses converted into cafes, craft shops, and small restaurants. This is the right time for lunch — the area is calmer than the tourist zone near Todai-ji and the food is better. Nakatani-do, a mochi shop near the Higashimuki shopping arcade, is famous for its fresh よもぎ餅 (yomogi mochi), sometimes pounded to order in the street.

Practical Notes実践ガイド

Getting there
Kintetsu Line from Osaka Namba (~40 min, ¥680) or Kyoto (~45 min via transfer, ¥760) is faster and more direct than JR for most visitors. JR from Osaka or Kyoto is also possible but involves more walking from Nara Station. Kintetsu Nara Station is closer to the park entrance.
Admission fees
Nara Park is free. Kofuku-ji grounds are free (museum separate). Todai-ji Great Buddha Hall ¥1,000. Kasuga Taisha outer grounds free, inner sanctuary ¥500.
Best time to visit
Early morning on weekdays, before 10am when tour groups arrive. Late March to early April brings cherry blossoms throughout the park; mid-November brings autumn colour. Summer is hot and very crowded — arriving at opening is especially important in July and August.
Deer crackers
鹿せんべい (shika senbei) are sold for around ¥200 per bundle throughout the park. They are the only food visitors should offer the deer. Deer near Todai-ji are assertive; deer in the quieter northern sections of the park are calmer.
Guided tours
Guided day trips from Kyoto or Osaka that cover Todai-ji and Kasuga Taisha are widely available and useful for visitors who want context on the fly — a knowledgeable guide makes a significant difference at both sites.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How long does a Nara day trip take?
The main circuit — Kofuku-ji, Todai-ji, and Kasuga Taisha — takes five to six hours at a relaxed pace. A half-day (Todai-ji and the deer park only) is possible if you arrive early and skip Kasuga Taisha.
Is Nara worth a day trip from Kyoto or Osaka?
Yes. The train is under an hour, the main sights are within easy walking distance of each other, and the combination of a UNESCO-listed Great Buddha, a major Shinto shrine, and freely roaming sacred deer is unlike anywhere else in Japan.
Why are there deer in Nara?
The deer have been considered sacred messengers of the Shinto gods since Kasuga Taisha was founded in 768, based on a tradition that the deity enshrined there arrived from Kashima riding a white deer. For most of Japanese history, killing a Nara deer was a capital offense; they are now designated a National Natural Monument.
Do the deer really bow?
Some do, yes — particularly deer that have learned the behavior around feeding areas. It is more accurate to say they nod when they want a cracker, having learned that bowing to humans produces food.
Can you do Nara in half a day?
Yes, if you focus on Todai-ji and the deer park and skip Kasuga Taisha. Allow at least three hours minimum, more if you want to spend time at the shrine or in Naramachi.
Is Nara only for tourists?
Nara City is a working city with a population of about 360,000. The deer park and temple district are tourist-heavy, but Naramachi and the areas west of the park are quieter and give a more genuine sense of how the city actually functions.
🦌

Nara's deer were counted as divine for over a thousand years before anyone thought to call them a tourist attraction. The Great Buddha was cast in a prayer large enough for an entire nation. The lanterns of Kasuga Taisha have been burning, on and off, for over twelve centuries. A day here is longer than it looks from the train.
