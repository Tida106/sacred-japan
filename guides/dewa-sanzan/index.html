Dewa Sanzan: The Three Mountains Where You Die and Come Back

出羽三山

Japan's Dewa Sanzan pilgrimage takes you through the present, the past, and the future — in that order. The last mountain forbids photography, video, and speaking about what you see there.

Most pilgrimages have a destination. The Dewa Sanzan has a sequence. Mount Haguro represents the world of the present. Mount Gassan represents the past, the afterlife, where souls go to atone. Mount Yudono embodies the future — the place of rebirth. Walking all three, in order, is understood as a complete cycle of death and return: you begin in your current life, pass through the realm of your ancestors, and emerge somewhere on the other side of Yudono as a new person.

The pilgrimage has been made for over 1,400 years. Mount Yudono, the most sacred of the three, still enforces one of the strictest rules left in Japanese religious practice: no photographing, no filming, and no speaking about what you see on its grounds.

HAGURO

The Present. Accessible year-round. 2,446 stone steps through ancient cedars, the Five-Story Pagoda, and the Sanjin Gosaiden housing all three deities together.

GASSAN

The Past. The highest of the three at 1,984 meters. Open only from early July to mid-September. Alpine flora, snowfields well into summer, and an exposed summit shrine.

YUDONO

The Future. No temple, no shrine — a hot spring rock walked barefoot. Photography, video, and description of what's seen there are forbidden.

A Prince, a Flight, and Three Mountains開山伝説

The Dewa Sanzan were first opened as a religious center in 593 by Prince Hachiko, the first-born son of Emperor Sushun. Hachiko fled to Dewa Province after the Soga clan assassinated his father, and devoted the rest of his life to religious pursuits — enduring harsh ascetic training and a period of penance that led him to the worship of Haguro Gongen, the deity of Mount Haguro. He then extended his worship to Gassan and Yudono, and the three deities came to be enshrined together at the temple on Haguro's summit.

Shugendo (修験道) — "the Way of the Harsh Training" — grew out of this practice: an esoteric tradition blending Shinto mountain worship, Buddhism, and Taoism, built on the belief that enlightenment could be earned through direct physical austerity in the mountains rather than through scripture alone. Its practitioners are Yamabushi — "those who retreat in the mountains" — mountain ascetics recognizable by white robes, small black caps, and conch-shell horns whose sound announced their approach before they came into view. Their training included standing under freezing waterfalls, sleep deprivation, and extended fasting deep in the forest.

Shugendo is Japan's other great mountain pilgrimage tradition, alongside the routes of the Kumano Kodo far to the south — both UNESCO-recognized landscapes built on the same premise: that walking through a mountain can change what you are on the other side of it.

The Dewa Sanzan drew some of the most significant figures in Japanese religious history: En no Gyoja, the founder of Shugendo asceticism, and Kukai, founder of the Shingon school of Buddhism. The haiku poet Matsuo Basho made the pilgrimage in 1689, recording it in his travel journal Oku no Hosomichi (Narrow Road to the Deep North). For Basho, the three mountains ranked among the most spiritually significant places in the country.

Mount Haguro: The Present羽黒山

Mount Haguro is the smallest of the three mountains but the only one open year-round — heavy snowfall closes Gassan and Yudono through the winter. It serves as the gateway to the other two, and is the most visited of the three, both by Japanese pilgrims and international travelers.

The ascent begins at the Zuijinmon gate and follows a stone stairway of 2,446 steps, built over 13 years during the Edo period, climbing roughly 1,700 meters through a forest of ancient cedars designated as Special Natural Monuments. The trail is rated three stars in the Michelin Green Guide Japan. Partway up, the Five-Story Pagoda — a National Treasure dating to the 14th century — stands in a clearing among the trees.

At the summit, the Sanjin Gosaiden — a single hall with a thatched roof 2.1 meters thick, its interior finished entirely in lacquer — enshrines the deities of all three mountains together, letting pilgrims worship the complete Dewa Sanzan in one place during the months when Gassan and Yudono are snowbound. In front of it, the Mirror Pond (Kagami-ike) has yielded hundreds of bronze mirrors from its depths, believed to have been offered by pilgrims across the centuries.

Mount Gassan: The Past月山

Gassan represents the past — the afterlife where the souls of ancestors go to atone, and where pilgrims come to be, briefly, one with those who came before them.

At 1,984 meters, Gassan is the highest of the three mountains, its summit under deep snow for most of the year. The main climbing route opens only from early July to mid-September. During that short window, the alpine zone reveals rare flowering plants and marshland vegetation before giving way to bare rock near the summit, where the Gassan Shrine stands exposed to wind from every direction. The climb from the 8th station road terminus takes about three hours.

Where Haguro is cedar-forest quiet, Gassan is unsheltered — snowfields well into summer, then alpine scrub, then rock. Of the three mountains, it is the one that most obviously asks something physical of the pilgrim.

Mount Yudono: The Future湯殿山

Yudono is what the other two mountains have been preparing you for. Unlike Haguro and Gassan, there is no large temple or shrine here. The object of worship is a massive reddish-brown rock that spouts hot spring water. Visitors remove their shoes and walk barefoot across its warm surface — an experience said to wash away the past and welcome a new self, spiritual rebirth made physical.

The prohibition on photography, video, and description at Yudono is not bureaucratic. It reflects a belief that some sacred experiences must be encountered directly, without mediation — that describing them in advance would diminish or distort them.

Visitors who have made the walk to Yudono consistently honor the restriction, which covers the goshintai — the sacred rock itself — and the specific rituals performed at the shrine. The approach to the shrine precinct, through forest and alongside a river, can be freely described; what happens once you arrive cannot.

The Sokushinbutsu: Monks Who Mummified Themselves即身仏

Dewa Sanzan carries one more layer that almost no other sacred site in Japan possesses. Among the most extreme practices of Shugendo was sokushinbutsu — a monk's attempt at voluntary self-mummification, undertaken as the supreme act of devotion, with the aim of becoming a living Buddha.

The process unfolded over years. A monk gradually reduced his diet to eliminate body fat, then consumed lacquer tree sap to render the body toxic to insects, before finally entering a small stone tomb with a breathing tube and a bell — ringing it daily to signal he was still alive. When the bell stopped, the tube was removed and the tomb sealed. After a set period it was reopened: if the body had been preserved, the monk was venerated as a Buddha. If not, he was resealed and honored as an ordinary monk.

Several sokushinbutsu from the Dewa Sanzan region remain on display today, most notably at Dainichibo and Churenji temples near Mount Haguro. They sit well outside any popular tourist circuit, and are unlike anything else you will encounter in Japan.

The Yamabushi Experience Today山伏体験

Visitors to the Dewa Sanzan can take part in guided Yamabushi training programs run by the mountains' shrines — waterfall prayers at Yudono, prayer sessions with Yamabushi monks on Haguro, and a guided ascent of Gassan.

The gateway community for these programs is Toge, a small village at the base of Mount Haguro in Tsuruoka City, where shukubo pilgrim lodges have operated continuously since the Edo period — much as they have at Mount Koya, Japan's other great shukubo tradition. Today Toge still hosts lodges run by practicing Yamabushi, and maintains connections with ko pilgrimage groups — a rare, still-functioning piece of pre-modern religious life. Staying overnight — eating the mountain-vegetable shojin ryori cuisine, joining morning prayers — is the way to experience the mountains as pilgrims have for over a thousand years, rather than as a day visitor.

Visiting Today参拝案内

The Dewa Sanzan is accessible from Tokyo via the Joetsu Shinkansen to Niigata, then the Uetsu Main Line to Tsuruoka — about 3.5 hours total. From Osaka, the journey runs via Shinkansen to Nagoya or Tokyo with a transfer, roughly 5 hours. From Tsuruoka Station, buses operated by Shonai Kotsu reach the base of Mount Haguro in about 50 minutes. Visitors who would rather not plan the logistics themselves can book guided Yamabushi experience tours, most departing from Tsuruoka or Toge village.

Mount Haguro
Open year-round. The 2,446-step stone stairway can be icy in winter.
Mount Gassan
Official hiking season July 1 – September 15 only.
Mount Yudono
Open June 1 – November 3. Photography, video, and description of the inner shrine are prohibited.
Getting there
Joetsu Shinkansen from Tokyo to Niigata, then the Uetsu Main Line to Tsuruoka Station (about 3.5 hours total). Shonai Kotsu buses reach the base of Mount Haguro in roughly 50 minutes.
Planning your visit
Mount Haguro alone makes a full day. The complete three-mountain circuit, including Gassan and Yudono, requires two days minimum and is only possible between July 1 and September 15.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What are the three mountains of Dewa Sanzan and what do they represent?
The three mountains are Mount Haguro (the present world), Mount Gassan (the past and afterlife), and Mount Yudono (the future and rebirth). Completing all three in sequence is understood as a symbolic journey through death and spiritual renewal.
Why is photography prohibited at Mount Yudono?
Mount Yudono is the most sacred of the three Dewa Sanzan mountains. Photography, video, and verbal description of what is seen at the inner shrine are prohibited out of respect for the sacred nature of the site — specifically the goshintai, a natural rock formation that spouts hot spring water and is the object of worship. The prohibition is considered an integral part of the spiritual experience.
What is a Yamabushi?
A Yamabushi is a practitioner of Shugendo, a tradition combining Shinto and Buddhist practices centered on mountain asceticism. The word means "those who retreat in the mountains." Yamabushi at Dewa Sanzan are still active today, conducting guided pilgrimage programs and leading seasonal rituals.
What are the sokushinbutsu at Dewa Sanzan?
Sokushinbutsu are monks who underwent a form of voluntary self-mummification over a period of years as a supreme act of religious devotion, aiming to become a "living Buddha." Several sokushinbutsu from the Dewa Sanzan area are preserved at Dainichibo and Churenji temples near Mount Haguro and can be viewed today.
When is the best time to visit Dewa Sanzan?
July through mid-September is the only period when all three mountains are open simultaneously. Mount Haguro is accessible year-round. Winter visits to Haguro are possible but the stone steps may be snow-covered and icy.
How do I get to Dewa Sanzan from Tokyo?
Take the Joetsu Shinkansen from Tokyo to Niigata, then the Uetsu Main Line limited express to Tsuruoka (approximately 3.5 hours total). From Tsuruoka Station, Shonai Kotsu buses reach the base of Mount Haguro in about 50 minutes.
