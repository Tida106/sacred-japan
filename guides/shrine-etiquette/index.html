How to Visit a Japanese Shrine: A Complete Etiquette Guide

Japan has roughly 80,000 Shinto shrines. Every one of them follows the same basic logic: a boundary between the ordinary world and sacred ground, a purification ritual before you enter the presence of a deity, and a prayer that follows a specific sequence. None of this requires you to be Shinto or religious in any particular way. The customs exist because they were designed to help people focus — to arrive at a shrine not distracted by whatever they were just doing, but present, clean-handed, and attentive. Knowing the sequence does not make you perform it better; it makes you less anxious about performing it, which is probably the point.

The Torii Gate

At a shrine entrance, you'll usually pass through a torii gate, which symbolically marks the boundary between the ordinary and the sacred. The gate is not decorative. It is a threshold, and crossing it is understood as a transition from the everyday world into the realm of the kami.

The center of the approach path (sandō) is considered the pathway of the gods (seichū), so walk along either side as a sign of respect. This is not a strict rule enforced by anyone, but it reflects a genuine belief that the central path is reserved for the deity. Bow once, briefly, before passing through the gate. When you leave, it is also polite to turn back and bow once more after passing through the torii.

The Temizuya: Purification

Temizu is the basic purification ritual you perform at a Japanese shrine before approaching the main hall, cleansing your hands and mouth to compose yourself for prayer. The temizuya (also called chōzuya) is the stone or wooden basin with a roof that you will find near the entrance of virtually every shrine.

The sequence, using a single scoop of water:

  1. Take the ladle (hishaku) in your right hand. Scoop water.
  2. Pour water over your left hand.
  3. Transfer the ladle to your left hand. Pour water over your right hand.
  4. Transfer the ladle back to your right hand. Cup a small amount of water in your left palm and rinse your mouth — do not drink directly from the ladle, and spit quietly to the side, not into the basin.
  5. Rinse your left hand once more.
  6. Hold the ladle upright so the remaining water runs down the handle, then replace it.

Note: Paper towels are rarely provided, so it is customary in Japan to carry a small hand towel or handkerchief to dry your hands. The essence of temizu is being considerate of those around you and acting quietly, rather than striving for perfection.

The Prayer Ritual

At the haiden, gently place your offering in the box, ring the bell if there is one, then bow twice, clap twice, and bow once to express gratitude or a prayer.

  • The offering box (saisen-bako): Toss a coin into the box before praying. A ¥5 coin is traditional. The word for five yen, "go-en," is a homophone for "good connection" or "good fortune."
  • The bell (suzu): If there is a large rope hanging from the ceiling with a bell attached, shake it gently to ring it.
  • Two bows (ni-hai): Bow deeply from the waist — roughly 90 degrees — twice.
  • Two claps (ni-hakushu): Press your palms together and clap twice. Your right hand should be positioned slightly lower than your left when your hands come together.
  • Prayer: With hands still pressed together, make your prayer silently.
  • One final bow (ippa): Bow once more deeply to close.

Exception: Izumo Taisha uses four claps rather than two. If you visit Izumo, adjust accordingly.

Shrines and Temples: The Key Difference

Shrines are Shinto; temples are Buddhist. Shrines are identified by the torii gate at the entrance and the practice of clapping hands. Praying at a temple usually involves quietly placing your offering, bowing once, and joining your hands together in front of your chest to pray, and then bowing again at the end. There is no clapping.

Omamori: Protective Charms

Omamori are the small fabric amulets sold at shrine and temple offices. Each is dedicated to a specific purpose. The charm should not be opened. Most omamori are intended to be replaced annually; after about a year, return the old one to a shrine or temple where it will be ceremonially burned with thanks.

For International Travelers: If you are a visitor and cannot return to Japan, do not worry. It is perfectly acceptable to keep the omamori as a lifelong, respectful souvenir in your home country rather than throwing it away.

Goshuin: The Red Seal Stamps

Goshuin are the red-stamped, hand-calligraphed records that shrine and temple offices write into a dedicated album (goshuincho) as evidence of your visit. They are considered sacred records of pilgrimage. To receive a goshuin, bring your goshuincho to the shrine or temple office, hand it over open to the next blank page, and make a small offering (typically ¥300 to ¥500).

Ema: Votive Plaques

Ema are small wooden plaques on which visitors write prayers or wishes, then hang them on a designated rack within the shrine precinct. Write your prayer or wish on the blank side of the plaque. There is no required formula for the wording.

What Not to Do

  • Do not walk down the center of the sandō approach path. The center is reserved for the kami.
  • Do not drink directly from the temizuya ladle, or spit into the basin.
  • Do not open an omamori.
  • Do not photograph inside worship halls unless you have confirmed it is permitted.
  • Do not be loud. Keeping voices low is not just courtesy — it is part of what makes the space work for everyone.
