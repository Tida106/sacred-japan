Byodoin Temple: The Building on Japan's Ten-Yen Coin

平等院鳳凰堂

A thousand-year-old hall built to be a physical representation of paradise, standing on its pond island in Uji — and reproduced on billions of Japanese coins since 1951.

Check your pocket. If you have a Japanese ten-yen coin, the building on the back is the Phoenix Hall of Byodoin Temple in Uji. It has been there since 1951, making it arguably the most reproduced image of any single building in Japanese history — billions of coins, each carrying a precise engraving of a hall that has stood on its pond island for nearly a thousand years and was built to be a physical representation of paradise.

That last part is not a metaphor. When Fujiwara no Yorimichi commissioned the Phoenix Hall in 1052, he was not building a monument to the Fujiwara family or a demonstration of aristocratic wealth, though it was certainly both of those things. He was building a three-dimensional version of the Pure Land — the western paradise of Amida Buddha — made visible on earth, on the banks of the Uji River, in the year that Buddhist cosmology had calculated to be the beginning of the final, degenerate age of the world.

The Age That Required a Paradise末法の時代

In 11th-century Japan, a doctrine called mappo — the concept of the "Latter Day of the Law" — had permeated court culture with a profound sense of historical anxiety. Buddhist teaching described three ages following the death of the historical Buddha: a first age of authentic teaching, a second of formal practice without genuine understanding, and a third age of complete degeneration in which neither the dharma nor its practitioners could function effectively. Different calculations produced different dates, but one widely circulated in Japan placed the beginning of mappo at 1052 CE — the year Yorimichi converted his father's Uji villa into a temple.

The Fujiwara response to living in the age of mappo was not despair but architecture. If the conditions for enlightenment through practice no longer existed, perhaps the Pure Land itself could be brought into the world through the power of art and devotion. The Pure Land of Amida Buddha was described in sutras as a realm of perfect symmetry, reflecting pools, pavilions that floated between water and sky, and bodhisattvas riding clouds playing heavenly music. Yorimichi built what he could of this vision: a hall positioned on an island in a pond, its wings extending symmetrically like a bird in flight, its interior housing the finest sculptural work of the Heian period.

The Fujiwara response to living at the end of history was not despair — it was architecture. If paradise could not be reached, it could be built.

What Jocho Made定朝の仏

At the center of the Phoenix Hall sits the Amida Nyorai statue, a National Treasure created by the master sculptor Jocho and completed in 1053. It stands 2.8 meters tall, made of Japanese cypress, lacquer, and gold leaf, using a technique called yosegi-zukuri — a joined-wood method in which multiple carved pieces are assembled into a single figure — that Jocho himself is credited with perfecting and that became the dominant mode of Buddhist sculpture in Japan thereafter. If you have ever visited Todai-ji and admired a large Japanese Buddhist image, the method by which it was carved is traceable to Jocho's work here.

The statue sits on a lotus throne beneath an elaborate wooden canopy, facing east — the direction from which worshippers would approach across the pond, making the pilgrim's approach a symbolic crossing from the ordinary world into the Pure Land. On the walls surrounding the statue, panels of Heian-period polychrome paintings depicted the nine grades of rebirth in Amida's paradise. Time and damage have left these partially readable, but traces of the original pigments survive.

Around the interior walls, 52 small bodhisattva figures — each different, each riding a cloud, each playing a different musical instrument — were installed in tiers above the main image. These are the Cloud-Riding Bodhisattvas, and 26 of them are now displayed at close range in the temple's underground museum; the others remain in the Phoenix Hall. Together they constitute one of the most intact ensembles of Heian Buddhist sculpture in existence.

The Only One That Survived唯一の遺構

Byodoin was built during the height of Fujiwara power, which did not last. As the clan's political dominance faded through the late Heian and Kamakura periods, the temple's resources diminished and its buildings were gradually lost to fire, decay, and neglect. Of the original complex, only the Phoenix Hall survived intact. It was never destroyed.

This survival was not entirely passive. In 1670, a full-scale renovation of the hall was undertaken. In 1698, a fire devastated Uji and damaged the hall's walls and doors, but did not destroy the structure itself; the damage galvanized public support for its preservation and accelerated subsequent repair campaigns. In 1951, when the hall was designated a National Treasure, its image was simultaneously adopted for the ten-yen coin — a moment that secured it in the consciousness of every Japanese person in a way that no heritage designation alone could accomplish.

The most recent major restoration was completed in 2014, when the exterior was repainted to approximate the original vivid red lacquer and gold that would have characterized it in the Heian period. The colors visible today — deeper and more saturated than most wooden temple structures in Japan — are a deliberate attempt to restore something of what Yorimichi's visitors would have seen.

The Museum Below the Ground鳳翔館

Byodoin's treasure house, the Hoshokan Museum, was designed to be largely invisible. Because the site's grounds are a nationally designated Historic Site and Place of Scenic Beauty, a conventional above-ground museum building would have intruded on the view of the Phoenix Hall. The architect Akira Kuryu solved the problem by sinking most of the building underground, using a series of skylights and reflecting pools to bring natural light into the galleries without breaking the surface of the landscape.

Inside, the 26 Cloud-Riding Bodhisattvas are displayed at eye level — the closest any visitor can get to sculptures that, inside the Phoenix Hall itself, are installed high on the walls and visible only at a distance. The temple bell (also a National Treasure, displayed here, with a replica bell installed in the tower outside), the Amida canopy fragments, and informative multilingual displays on the hall's construction complete the collection. Entry to the Hoshokan is included in the standard garden admission and deserves as much time as the Phoenix Hall exterior.

Uji: Matcha and the Tale of Genji宇治の町

Uji is not only Byodoin. The city is one of Japan's premier tea-producing regions, and the streets between the station and the temple are lined with tea shops serving matcha in every form — ice cream, soba noodles, sweets, and the ceremonial whipped preparation. The temple itself operates a tea salon, Sabo Toka, where visitors can drink Uji tea with traditional sweets while looking out toward the Phoenix Hall garden.

The Uji River bank is also the setting of the final chapters of The Tale of Genji — the 11th-century novel by Murasaki Shikibu, roughly contemporary with the Phoenix Hall's construction and arguably the world's first novel. The Uji chapters, known as the Uji Jujo, follow a heroine named Ukifune and contain some of the most psychologically dense writing in the entire work. A dedicated Tale of Genji Museum in Uji uses three-dimensional projections to bring the Uji chapters to life for visitors unfamiliar with the text.

Nearby, Ujigami Shrine — the oldest surviving shrine buildings in Japan, dating to the 10th and 11th centuries — provides an atmospheric counterpart to Byodoin and can be visited in the same half-day trip.

Visiting Today参拝案内

Byodoin is a 10-minute walk from JR Uji Station (JR Nara Line, approximately 17 minutes from Kyoto Station) or a 15-minute walk from Keihan Uji Station. From Nara, the JR Nara Line also connects directly to Uji in about 30 minutes, making Byodoin a natural stop between Kyoto and Nara — easily combined with a Nara day trip.

Getting there
Take the JR Nara Line from Kyoto Station to JR Uji Station (approximately 17 minutes on the Miyakoji Rapid service). The temple is a 10-minute walk from the station. From Osaka or Nara, the same JR Nara Line connects directly.
Admission
Entry to the garden and Hoshokan Museum costs ¥700 for adults. Entry to the Phoenix Hall interior requires an additional ¥300 and is conducted on guided tours of up to 50 people departing every 20 minutes; booking a slot promptly upon arrival is recommended on busy days.
Hours
The garden opens at 8:45 am, making it possible to arrive before the first major tour groups. The Hoshokan Museum opens at 9:00 am.
How long to allow
The garden and exterior of the Phoenix Hall can be seen in 30 minutes. Adding the Hoshokan Museum brings the total to about 1–1.5 hours. The Phoenix Hall interior tour adds another 30 minutes. Combined with the matcha shops on the approach and a visit to Ujigami Shrine, a half-day is comfortable.
What to book in advance
No advance booking is required for garden entry. On busy weekends and holidays, the Phoenix Hall interior tours fill quickly — arrive early and join the queue. Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Why is Byodoin on the ten-yen coin?
The Phoenix Hall was designated a National Treasure in 1951 and its image was adopted for the reverse of the ten-yen coin the same year, recognizing it as one of Japan's most significant surviving examples of Heian-period architecture. The phoenix statue on the hall's roof also appears on the reverse of the 10,000-yen note.
What is the Phoenix Hall built to represent?
The Phoenix Hall was built in 1053 by Fujiwara no Yorimichi as a physical representation of the Pure Land of Amida Buddha — the paradise described in Buddhist sutras — made visible on earth. Its position on a pond island, its symmetrical wing corridors, and its interior artwork were all designed to evoke the sutra descriptions of Amida's western paradise.
Is Byodoin a Shinto shrine or a Buddhist temple?
It is a Buddhist temple, jointly administered today by the Jodo (Pure Land) and Tendai sects. It was converted from an aristocratic villa to a Buddhist temple in 1052.
How long does a visit to Byodoin take?
The garden and exterior of the Phoenix Hall can be seen in 30 minutes. Adding the Hoshokan Museum brings the total to about 1–1.5 hours. Entry to the Phoenix Hall interior (guided tour, additional ¥300) adds another 30 minutes. Combined with time at the matcha shops on the approach and a visit to nearby Ujigami Shrine, a half-day is comfortable.
How do I get from Kyoto to Byodoin?
Take the JR Nara Line from Kyoto Station to JR Uji Station (approximately 17 minutes on the Miyakoji Rapid service). The temple is a 10-minute walk from the station.
Can Byodoin be combined with Nara on the same day?
Yes. Uji sits between Kyoto and Nara on the JR Nara Line. A logical sequence is Byodoin in the morning from Kyoto, then continuing to Nara by early afternoon — or vice versa.

The Hall That Outlasted Its Ageむすびに

Yorimichi built the Phoenix Hall in the year that his world believed history was ending — and the hall he built has outlasted the dynasty that built it, the palace culture that surrounded it, and a thousand years of fire, war, and change. The Amida that Jocho carved still sits at the center of the pond island, facing east across the water, and the coin in your pocket still carries its reflection. In Uji, what was meant to be the end of the world became something that has endured beyond nearly everything else the Heian period left behind.
