Enryaku-ji

延暦寺

The Temple That Trained Every Sect, Then Got Burned to the Ground

Every significant Japanese Buddhist school you have heard of — Pure Land, Zen, Nichiren — traces its founding to a single mountain outside Kyoto. The monks who created those traditions all spent formative years on Mount Hiei (比叡山) at a temple complex called Enryaku-ji (延暦寺), absorbing the comprehensive Buddhist curriculum that its founder, Saicho, had brought back from Tang Dynasty China. Then the mountain became a military power. Then it was burned to the ground by one of the most ruthless warlords in Japanese history, who killed everyone on it. Then it was rebuilt. A single lamp Saicho lit in 788 CE has never been extinguished — not even during the burning — and burns in the main hall today.

Saicho and the Mountain That Faced Kyoto最澄と比叡山

In 785 CE, a young monk named Saicho (最澄) made a decision that would eventually shape the entire course of Japanese Buddhism. Dissatisfied with what he saw as the increasing worldliness and corruption of the established Buddhist establishment in Nara, he climbed the wooded slopes of Mount Hiei — a 848-meter peak on the northeastern edge of what would become Kyoto — and established a small hermitage. Three years later, in 788 CE, he built a hall to house a small statue of Yakushi Nyorai, the Medicine Buddha, which he had carved himself. He lit an oil lamp before it and prayed that the flame would never be extinguished. It is still burning today.

The location was not accidental. In traditional Japanese cosmology, the northeast is the direction from which malevolent forces enter — the kimon (鬼門), the "demon gate." When Emperor Kanmu moved the imperial capital to Heian-kyo (平安京) (Kyoto) in 794 CE, Mount Hiei stood precisely in the capital's northeastern quarter. Saicho's temple complex was not merely a place of study; it was, quite literally, the spiritual shield protecting the new capital from the wrong direction.

Saicho traveled to Tang Dynasty China in 804 CE, studying the Tiantai school of Buddhism — known in Japanese as Tendai (天台) — which offered a systematic, comprehensive framework for understanding the entire Buddhist canon rather than specializing in a single sutra or practice. He returned with texts, teachings, and an imperial mandate.

In 807, Emperor Kanmu officially recognized Enryaku-ji as the headquarters of the new Tendai sect in Japan. The temple was named after the era of its imperial recognition: the Enryaku (延暦) era, 782–806 CE.

The Mountain That Made All the Others日本仏教の母山

What followed over the next four centuries was the most productive period in Japanese Buddhist history. Enryaku-ji became the institution that any serious monk needed to pass through. Its curriculum was comprehensive in a way no other temple offered, incorporating esoteric ritual, Pure Land devotion, Zen-style meditation, and rigorous textual scholarship simultaneously. The monks who studied there and then went on to found their own traditions form a roll call of Japanese Buddhism's defining figures.

Honen, who founded Jodo-shu (浄土宗) (Pure Land Buddhism) in Japan, studied at Enryaku-ji before concluding that the single practice of chanting Amida's name was sufficient for salvation. Shinran, his disciple and founder of Jodo Shinshu (浄土真宗) (True Pure Land Buddhism), spent twenty years on the mountain before leaving to follow Honen. Eisai, who introduced Rinzai Zen to Japan from China, also trained at Enryaku-ji first. Dogen, the founder of Soto Zen, began at Enryaku-ji before making his own journey to China. Nichiren, whose intensely nationalistic Buddhism would generate the Nichiren school and all its descendants, spent over twenty years at Enryaku-ji — he framed his later criticisms of other Buddhist schools in terms of what he had learned there.

All of these men spent years absorbing Tendai's comprehensive framework before diverging in their own directions. The irony is that Enryaku-ji's thoroughness was partly what made it possible for its students to leave and found competing traditions.

The Warrior Monks僧兵の時代

By the tenth century, a different kind of history was accumulating on the mountain. Succession disputes within the Tendai hierarchy between the factions of two of its early abbots — Ennin and Enchin — had escalated into open internal warfare, with the losing side eventually abandoning the mountain to found a rival temple at Mii-dera (三井寺). Both sides began recruiting and training armed monks, known as sohei (僧兵), to press their institutional claims. Enryaku-ji's sohei became particularly formidable — a standing military force housed inside a sacred complex that both the court and secular lords were reluctant to attack on religious grounds.

Throughout the Heian and Kamakura periods, the warrior monks of Enryaku-ji regularly descended from the mountain to force political demands on Kyoto's residents, settle disputes with rival temples by force, and intervene in succession controversies.

The eleventh-century Emperor Shirakawa is recorded as having said that the three things most beyond his control were "the waters of the Kamo River, the dice of the sugoroku game, and the monks on the mountain." By the Sengoku period, the complex had become effectively a fortified city — 3,000 sub-temples at its peak, its own army, its own revenue streams, and the moral authority that came from being the single most important Buddhist institution in Japan.

The Night Nobunaga Surrounded the Mountain信長の焼き討ち

On the night of September 29, 1571, Oda Nobunaga (織田信長) assembled an army of approximately 30,000 men and encircled Mount Hiei from every direction. He had sent repeated ultimatums demanding the monks' disarmament; they had ignored them, correctly calculating that no secular lord would dare attack a site of such sacred significance. Nobunaga's response was to demonstrate that this calculation was wrong.

His troops began by burning the town of Sakamoto at the mountain's base, driving its residents — along with monks who had come down from the mountain — up the slopes toward the temple complex for refuge. Then the army moved slowly upward, burning buildings, forests, and anyone who tried to escape. By nightfall, Enryaku-ji's main hall was burning. The following day, arquebus squads hunted survivors.

On the death toll: Contemporary accounts differ — estimates in historical sources range from 1,500 to 4,000, with some later accounts going much higher. Recent archaeological work on the mountain suggests that some buildings had already been vacated before the attack and the destruction may have been somewhat less than the most dramatic accounts indicate. What is not in dispute is that the complex was effectively destroyed, and that the killing included non-combatant monks, women, and children who had nowhere to go.

Nobunaga's own recorded words offered no apology. He described the monks as having abandoned the Buddhist law and framed the destruction as a purification rather than a sacrilege. The attack communicated its intended message to every other religious institution in Japan: military resistance to Nobunaga's authority would not be protected by sacred status. Other temples disbanded their armies.

One small hall survived the burning: the Ruri-do (瑠璃堂) (Lapis Lazuli Hall), a 13th-century structure located off an unmarked path from the Western Pagoda complex. It is the only building on the mountain that predates the destruction.

The Eternal Flame不滅の法灯

Here is the thing about the flame that Saicho lit in 788: it did not go out during the burning. This is not metaphor. The Fumetsu-no-Hoto (不滅の法灯) — the Inextinguishable Dharma Lamp — in the Konpon Chudo (根本中堂) (the main hall of the East Pagoda precinct) has been continuously replenished with oil by monks for over 1,200 years. When Nobunaga's forces approached, monks reportedly carried the flame off the mountain and then returned with it when rebuilding began. The lamp burns in the main hall today, in a building reconstructed in 1642 under the Tokugawa shogunate.

The Konpon Chudo is currently undergoing an extensive restoration project that began in 2016 and is expected to continue until around 2026. The hall remains open to visitors during construction, and the eternal flame is visible — scaffolding and all. There are few better ways to see a living Buddhist institution at work than watching its most sacred building being restored around a flame that has never gone out.

Visiting Today拝観のご案内

Enryaku-ji covers approximately 1,700 hectares across three distinct precincts — Todo (東塔) (East Pagoda), Saito (西塔) (West Pagoda), and Yokawa (横川) — connected by a shuttle bus that operates from spring through autumn. The Konpon Chudo (根本中堂) in Todo is the complex's symbolic and architectural center.

Saito contains the Shakado (釈迦堂), the oldest building currently standing on the mountain — it was moved from Mii-dera after the burning and predates the 1571 destruction. Yokawa, the farthest precinct, is the quietest and least visited. The complex was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994 as part of the "Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto." Admission covers all three precincts (¥1,000 for adults).

If you would prefer to explore Enryaku-ji's layered history with someone who can explain the significance of each precinct and place it within Kyoto's wider Buddhist landscape, guided tours of Mount Hiei and Enryaku-ji depart from central Kyoto and typically combine the temple with nearby sites.

BY BUS

The Hieizan Drive Bus runs directly from Kyoto Station to the Enryakuji Bus Center in the Todo precinct (approximately 60 minutes). This is the most straightforward option for most visitors.

BY RAIL

Take the Eizan Railway from Demachiyanagi Station to Yase-Hieizanguchi, then the Hieizan Cable Car to Hieizan Station, then the Hieizan Ropeway to the mountain top (about 40 minutes total from Demachiyanagi). A second cable car approach from the Shiga side departs from Sakamoto-Hieizanguchi Station on the Keihan Ishiyama-Sakamoto Line.

ON THE MOUNTAIN

A shuttle bus connects the three precincts (Todo, Saito, Yokawa). Note: the shuttle does not operate December through late March. Outside of operating season, visiting all three precincts requires significant walking or a taxi.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Why is Enryaku-ji called the "mother temple" of Japanese Buddhism?
Because the founders of Japan's major Buddhist schools — including Honen (Pure Land), Shinran (True Pure Land), Eisai (Rinzai Zen), Dogen (Soto Zen), and Nichiren (Nichiren Buddhism) — all trained at Enryaku-ji before founding their own traditions. The Tendai curriculum's comprehensiveness made it the training ground for the entire tradition.
What happened when Nobunaga burned Mount Hiei in 1571?
Nobunaga surrounded the mountain with roughly 30,000 troops, burned the town at its base, then destroyed the temple complex and killed its inhabitants. Death estimates in historical sources range from 1,500 to 4,000, including non-combatant monks, women, and children. One small hall — the Ruri-do — survived. The complex was rebuilt by Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu in subsequent decades.
Is the eternal flame at Enryaku-ji really 1,200 years old?
The Fumetsu-no-Hoto (不滅の法灯) (Inextinguishable Dharma Lamp) in the Konpon Chudo is maintained as a continuously burning flame first lit by Saicho in 788 CE. Monks replenish the oil daily and the flame is said never to have been extinguished — including during Nobunaga's attack in 1571, when monks reportedly carried it off the mountain.
What is the oldest building at Enryaku-ji?
The Shakado (釈迦堂) in the West Pagoda precinct. It was not originally built at Enryaku-ji — it was moved from Mii-dera temple by Toyotomi Hideyoshi during the post-burning reconstruction — and predates the 1571 destruction. The Ruri-do (Lapis Lazuli Hall), which survived the burning in place, also predates 1571.
How do you get to Enryaku-ji from Kyoto?
The most direct route is the Hieizan Drive Bus from Kyoto Station to the Enryakuji Bus Center (about 60 minutes). Alternatively, take the Eizan Railway from Demachiyanagi to Yase-Hieizanguchi, then the cable car and ropeway. A shuttle bus connects the three precincts on the mountain (seasonal — does not operate December through late March).
Is Enryaku-ji a UNESCO World Heritage Site?
Yes, since 1994, as part of the "Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto (Kyoto, Uji and Otsu Cities)." The designation covers 17 sites in and around Kyoto and Otsu, including Kinkaku-ji, Ryoan-ji, and Kiyomizu-dera.
