Edo Kiriko: How to Choose Authentic Japanese Cut Glass

江戸切子の選び方

Hand-cut colored glass from Tokyo is one of the best things to bring home, and one of the easiest to get wrong. Here is how I tell the real thing, care for it, and visit the workshops.

As a licensed guide, I watch the same thing happen in Tokyo gift shops. A guest picks up a small ruby-red cup, tilts it toward the light, and the cut pattern flashes like a jewel. Then comes the question: “Is this the real Edo Kiriko, and is it worth that price?”

This guide is the short version of what I tell them. It covers what Edo Kiriko is, how it differs from Satsuma Kiriko, how to check what you are buying, and how to keep it from chipping or cracking.

What Edo Kiriko Is江戸切子とは

Edo Kiriko is hand-cut glass from Tokyo, the old Edo. Craftsmen cut patterns into the surface with grinding wheels, so a clear glass with a thin colored layer shows crisp lines of color and sparkle. Kagaya Kyubei, who ran a glassware shop in Edo, is credited as the originator, and he is said to have learned the craft in Osaka. It was designated a national traditional craft in 2002, and about 80 percent of production is in Koto and Sumida wards.

The Tokyo craft association describes four main steps:

  • Sumitsuke: the design is outlined in ink with a bamboo stick.
  • Arazuri: rough grinding on metal wheels cuts the main pattern.
  • Ishikake: whetstones sharpen and smooth the cut lines.
  • Migaki: polishing on a wooden wheel brings out the shine.

The most famous pattern is nanako, rows of crossing fine lines that look like fish eggs in reflected light. Another is soko-giku, a chrysanthemum cut into the base of the glass. About twenty traditional patterns are recorded, and workshops also create new ones.

Edo or Satsuma?江戸切子と薩摩切子

Japan has two famous cut-glass traditions, and shops sometimes mix them up. Satsuma Kiriko began in Satsuma, now Kagoshima, around 1840. It stopped after the 1877 civil war and was revived in 1985. Many people connect the Edo colors to Satsuma artisans who came to Edo afterward.

  • Edo Kiriko: a thin colored layer, under about 1 mm, with sharp cuts and strong contrast.
  • Satsuma Kiriko: a thicker colored layer, about 2 to 3 mm, with a shading technique that makes the color fade softly.

Looks alone can be confusing, so hold the glass and compare the thickness and the depth of the cuts. If you love soft color gradients, Satsuma is the one to look for. If you want crisp, bright lines, choose Edo.

How to Spot the Real Thing本物の見分け方

There is no single stamp that settles it, so I check several things together.

  • The workshop. Ask who cut it. A real piece comes from a named workshop in Tokyo, not just a brand-like word.
  • The cuts. Hold it to the light. Lines should be sharp and even, and the pattern should meet cleanly at the crossings.
  • The color layer. The colored layer should look thin and even, with clear glass visible inside the cuts.
  • The origin. Marketplace listings that only say “Japanese style” or give no origin are not enough. Look for the maker and where it was made.
  • The price. According to one Japan article, small Edo Kiriko sake cups range from about 5,000 yen to nearly 100,000 yen depending on the pattern. A price far below that range deserves a second look.

🔍 The guide's tip: When in doubt, ask the shop for the workshop name and the pattern name. A seller who knows the maker will answer without hesitation. For verification, the Edo Kiriko Cooperative Association (edokiriko.or.jp) is a good place to start.

Care Without Cracksお手入れ

  • Wash by hand: and dry it carefully.
  • Store upright: not stacked, so the rims and cuts are not damaged.
  • Never: microwave, dishwasher or a drying rack.
  • Drinks: cold or room-temperature drinks are safest. Sudden temperature changes can crack the glass, so avoid hot drinks.

Visit the Workshops工房を訪ねる

The easiest place to see the craft is the Sumida Edo Kiriko Museum (Sumida Edo Kiriko-kan) in Tokyo. It is about a 6-minute walk from Kinshicho Station. Admission is free, and the shop sells everything from display pieces to everyday glasses. Artisans' work can be seen through windows, and there is a glass-cutting experience.

It is open from 10:00 to 17:00 and closed on Sundays, Mondays, public holidays, and during summer and New Year closures. The experience fee and booking details were not listed on the page I checked, so please confirm them on the official site (edokiriko.net, Japanese only) before you go.

Where to Buy買える場所

In Tokyo, the museum shop, the Edo Kiriko Showroom, the workshop shop Hanashyo, and the better souvenir shops are the safest places to buy. From abroad, specialist Japan-goods shops that name the workshop are my first choice. If you shop on Amazon, use the checks above. These search pages are a starting point, not a recommendation of any one listing: Edo Kiriko glass and Edo Kiriko sake cups.

A small sake cup or a short tumbler is the best first piece. It is easy to pack and easy to use. For the culture behind the cup, see Sake and Shrines.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is the difference between Edo Kiriko and Satsuma Kiriko?
Edo Kiriko is from Tokyo and is cut in a thin colored layer, giving sharp contrast between color and clear glass. Satsuma Kiriko is from Kagoshima, uses a thicker colored layer and a shading technique that makes the color fade softly. They can be hard to tell apart at a glance, so check the glass thickness and the depth of the cuts.
How can I tell if Edo Kiriko is genuine?
Ask for the workshop name and where the piece was cut. Look for crisp, even cuts and a thin, even colored layer. Be careful with very low prices or listings that only say Japanese style, since small sake cups from real workshops range from about 5,000 yen to nearly 100,000 yen depending on the pattern.
Can I put Edo Kiriko in the dishwasher or use it for hot drinks?
No. Wash and dry it by hand, store it upright, and avoid the microwave, stacking and drying racks. Hot drinks are best avoided because sudden temperature changes can crack the glass.
Why is Edo Kiriko so expensive?
Every cut is made by hand in several steps: outlining the pattern, rough grinding, whetstone grinding and polishing. A deeper or more complex pattern takes more time, which is why prices vary so widely.
Where can I see Edo Kiriko being made?
Sumida Edo Kiriko Museum, about a 6-minute walk from Kinshicho Station in Tokyo, has a shop and displays, and artisans' work can be seen through windows. It also offers a glass-cutting experience. Please check the official site for fees, booking and closing days before you go.

A Glass to Keepむすびに

A good piece of Edo Kiriko is small enough to carry home and special enough to use for years. Check the workshop, hold it to the light, and wash it by hand. If you are planning a Tokyo trip, add Sumida to the list. For more crafts, see our Japan crafts guide, or the iron kettle from Iwate in our Nambu Tekki guide.
