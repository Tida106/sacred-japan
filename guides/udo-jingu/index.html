Udo Shrine: The Cave Shrine in Miyazaki, Turtle Rock and the Lucky Undama Toss

鵜戸神宮

A Miyazaki shrine whose red main hall sits inside a sea cave, with the myth of the first emperor's father and a lucky stone toss.

As a licensed guide, I like shrines that make you work for the view, and Udo Shrine does it the other way round: you walk down to it, through red railings and a gate, to a hall tucked under a cliff at the edge of the sea.

Udo Shrine sits on the Nichinan Coast of Miyazaki, about 40 km south of Miyazaki City (japan-guide). Its main hall stands inside a sea cave, and the story told about that cave is one of the oldest in Japanese mythology: the birth of the father of the first emperor. The photographs here come from Wikimedia Commons, so you can see what each stage of the walk looks like. This guide covers the legend, the walk, the things people do in and below the cave, and how to get there.

The Birth in the Cave鵜戸の伝説

According to the MLIT multilingual guide, the shrine is dedicated to Ugayafukiaezu, who plays a key role in the myths as the father of Emperor Jimmu. The story goes like this. Hoori, a skilled hunter also called Yamasachihiko, married Toyotama, the daughter of the sea god. When her time came, she went ashore to give birth and began roofing a birth hut with cormorant feathers; labour started before it was finished. She asked her husband not to look. He looked, and saw her in her true form, which MLIT gives as a giant shark. She went back to the sea and left the baby behind. He was named for the unfinished roof, a name that roughly means “the one whose cormorant-feather thatch was never completed” (MLIT, Japan Today).

The local belief is that the birth took place in the cave where the shrine now stands (MLIT). Our Japanese mythology guide tells the story of Hoori and Toyotama in more detail,, and our Takachiho guide covers another Miyazaki site from the same early myths.

On the historical side, MLIT reports that a priest named Kaikyu founded a Buddhist temple here in 782 to look after the shrine, that the Ito family, lords of the Obi domain, supported it in the Edo period, and that they built the present main hall in 1711. The same source says the hall still has Buddhist elements, such as a window shaped like a temple bell, since mixing Shinto and Buddhist practice was normal until the end of the Edo period. Today the site is purely Shinto. Jeepe also gives 1711 for the rebuilding. Tabi-mag reports that the shrine’s own tradition places its founding in the reign of Emperor Sujin, which is legend and not a documented date.

The Walk Down to the Cave下り宮

Udo Shrine is called a kudari-miya, a “descending shrine”, because you walk down to the main hall instead of climbing up to it (Jeepe). From the car park the approach runs along the coast between sago palms, past the gate and the two-storey tower gate, then across a red bridge toward the cliff (Jeepe).

The tower gate is the first landmark. In the 2008 photograph a framed picture of a white rat hangs under the upper floor; in the 2009 picture below it shows an ox, so it looks like a yearly zodiac picture, which I have not confirmed.

Past the gate, the path turns to steps cut into the cliff. The staircase below is the one that takes you down to the hall. Jeepe says to expect many steps and uneven ground, and warns that the stone can be slippery in the rain. Japan Today describes the drop as about three storeys. GLTJP’s Japanese page says the descent involves about 800 metres of stone steps, which does not match the other accounts, so treat the length as approximate.

Inside the Cave: Breast Rocks and Turtle Rock洞窟・お乳岩・亀石

The cave measures about 38 m east to west, 29 m north to south and 8.5 m high (Tabi-mag, Jeepe), and the red hall fills a small part of it. In the photograph at the top the rock roof sits close above the tiles.

Ochichi Iwa, the breast rocks. The legend says Toyotama left her breasts on the wall so that her son could be fed. At the back of the cave there are two rocks of that shape, from which water drips (Japan Today, Nippon.com). MLIT says visitors touch them to pray for safe childbirth, healthy children and the health of mothers, and the shrine sells a milk candy, ochichi ame, associated with this water (Tabi-mag, Japan Travel). japan-guide adds that drinking the dripping water is thought to be good for pregnancy, birth and nursing, and that the shrine is also considered fortunate for couples and newlyweds.

Kameishi, the turtle rock. Below the terrace, a rock shaped like a turtle with a rope around a hollow on its back is said to be the turtle that carried Toyotama from the sea (Nippon.com). Another MLIT page gives a second version in which the turtle carried the baby’s aunt, who came to raise him after he was left behind. The two retellings differ, and I would take the turtle itself as the fixed part of the story.

Throwing an Undama運玉投げ

The best-known thing to do is the undama toss. You buy five small clay balls at the shrine, make a wish and throw them down from the terrace at the rope-ringed hollow on Kameishi. Men throw with the left hand and women with the right (japan-guide, MLIT). A ball that stays in the ring is said to grant the wish.

Sources disagree on the details. The price is 200 yen for five (Jeepe) or 100 yen for five (Japan Today, Lonely Planet, probably older). Jeepe puts the distance at roughly 10 m; Nippon.com gives a target about 60 cm wide and about 12 m from the cliff. MLIT says people used to throw coins until the 1950s, but children climbed down to collect them, so the shrine switched to clay. Nippon.com adds that local schoolchildren make the balls and the money goes toward school costs. I have not tried it myself and would keep expectations low: it is harder than it looks.

You will also see rabbit images around the shrine. Japan Today suggests this is because the Japanese word for rabbit shares its first sound with u, cormorant, and because rabbits symbolise fertility. I have not found a shrine source for that reading, so take it as one author’s guess.

Visiting Udo Shrine参拝案内

  • Address: 3232 Miyaura, Nichinan, Miyazaki 887-0101.
  • Fee: free (japan-guide, GLTJP).
  • Hours: 6:00 to 18:00 (japan-guide, GLTJP, Miyazaki tourism), 6:00 to 19:00 (Japan Travel) or seasonal (6:00 to 19:00 from April to September, 7:00 to 18:00 from October to March, per Japan Rail and Travel). Check before you go.
  • By car: the easiest way (japan-guide). About 50 minutes from central Miyazaki via Route 220 (Jeepe). Free parking, about 400 spaces (Jeepe); JORUDAN says 450.
  • By bus from Miyazaki: about 90 minutes and about 1,570 yen one way, then about 10 minutes on foot from the Udo Jingu stop (japan-guide). Japan Today gives about 70 minutes. The bus also stops at Aoshima.
  • By train and bus: JR Nichinan Line to Aburatsu or Ibii, then a bus of about 20 minutes (Japan Rail and Travel, JORUDAN). Buses are infrequent (GLTJP).
  • Time to allow: 30 to 40 minutes for the hall alone, about an hour with the undama, 1.5 to 2 hours with nearby sights (Jeepe).
  • Nearby: Namikiri Shrine, a small shrine in a sea cave, about 20 minutes’ walk beyond the Udo gate (Miyazaki tourism); Aoshima; Cape Toi (Jeepe).
  • Notes: wear shoes with grip. Early morning or late afternoon is quieter (Jeepe).

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

What is Udo Shrine?
Udo Shrine (Udo Jingu) in Nichinan, Miyazaki, is a Shinto shrine whose main hall stands inside a sea cave in a cliff facing the Hyuga Sea (Tokyo Weekender, GLTJP). It is dedicated to Ugayafukiaezu, the father of Emperor Jimmu, the legendary first emperor (MLIT, japan-guide). Local belief holds that he was born in this cave (MLIT).
What is the legend behind the cave?
Toyotama, daughter of the sea god, came ashore to give birth to Hoori’s child in a hut roofed with cormorant feathers. She asked her husband not to look; he did, and saw her in her true form, which the MLIT text gives as a giant shark. She returned to the sea and left the newborn, named Ugayafukiaezu after the unfinished roof (MLIT, Japan Today). More Than Tokyo glosses the name Udo as “cormorant dwelling”.
What is the undama toss?
You buy small clay balls (undama) at the shrine and throw them at a rope-ringed hollow on the back of Kameishi, the turtle-shaped rock below the cliff. Men throw with the left hand and women with the right; a ball that lands in the hollow is said to grant a wish (japan-guide, MLIT). The price is given as 200 yen for five (Jeepe) and as 100 yen for five in older sources (Japan Today, Lonely Planet), so check at the shrine. Nippon.com adds that local schoolchildren make the balls and the money goes to school costs.
How do I get to Udo Shrine without a car?
japan-guide says a bus from Miyazaki Station takes about 90 minutes and costs about 1,570 yen one way, with a 10-minute walk from the Udo Jingu stop; Japan Today gives about 70 minutes. From the JR Nichinan Line, buses run from Aburatsu or Ibii Station in about 20 minutes (Japan Rail and Travel, JORUDAN). Buses are infrequent, so check the timetable before you go (GLTJP).
How long should I allow?
Jeepe suggests 30 to 40 minutes for the main hall alone, about an hour with photos and the undama toss, and 1.5 to 2 hours with nearby sights. Tokyo Weekender suggests at least 30 to 60 minutes.
What are the hours and fees?
Entry is free (japan-guide, GLTJP). Hours differ by source: 6:00 to 18:00 (japan-guide, GLTJP, Miyazaki tourism), 6:00 to 19:00 (Japan Travel) and seasonal hours of 6:00 to 19:00 from April to September and 7:00 to 18:00 from October to March (Japan Rail and Travel). No closing days are listed (japan-guide, GLTJP); confirm the hours before you go.
Is it suitable for visitors with limited mobility?
Not easy. You reach the cave by descending steps cut into the cliff, and the stones can be slippery in the rain (Jeepe). Japan Today reports no special access for mobility-impaired visitors, and describes the stairs as about three storeys down.

Before You Goむすびに

Udo Shrine pairs well with Kirishima and Takachiho on a trip through the mythological south of Kyushu; our Kirishima Jingu guide covers the shrine of Ninigi, Hoori’s father. Go early, wear grip shoes, and give yourself time for the walk down.
