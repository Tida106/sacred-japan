Kumamoto Castle: The Fortress That Is Being Rebuilt

熊本城

Curved walls built to stop an army, a quake that broke them, and a restoration you can walk through.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I tell guests that Kumamoto is the castle where you can see two eras at once. The stone walls are curved like the inside of a bowl, so steeply that they were nicknamed "warrior repellers." Many of them were shaken apart in April 2016, and again in July 2026, and today you can walk past the numbered stones waiting to go back. It is one of Japan's Three Great Castles, and one of its most honest.

This guide covers Kato Kiyomasa's design, the fire of 1877, the earthquakes of 2016 and 2026, how the restoration works, and what to know before you go.

Kato Kiyomasa's Fortress歴史

Kato Kiyomasa, a retainer of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, became lord of Higo Province in 1588. Between 1601 and 1607 he expanded the castle on the Chausuyama hill into a huge complex with 49 turrets, 18 turret gates and 29 smaller gates, according to Wikipedia. After the Kato family was dispossessed by the shogunate, the Hosokawa clan took over in 1632 and held the castle until 1871.

The castle was also known as Ginkgo Castle, because many ginkgo trees were planted when it was built, according to GLTJP. For the wider picture of the twelve original keeps, see my castles overview, and for the stories behind four great castles, The Best Castles in Japan.

This is the view that most people remember from before 2016: a tall turret standing on a long stone base, with an ordinary plaza and a parked car in front of it. The Commons description does not name the turret. It shows how large the walls are compared with the people and cars. The photo is from 2014, so the scene has changed since the earthquakes.

The Walls Called Warrior Repellers武者返し

The famous feature is the stone wall. It starts with a gentle slope and curves upward until it is nearly vertical, which is why it is called musha-gaeshi, "warrior repeller." The idea is simple: a climber who starts easily runs out of footholds near the top. Wikipedia also lists wooden overhangs and rock falls among the defenses. The winding approach paths add to the effect.

The castle held out in 1877, during the Satsuma Rebellion, when Saigo Takamori's forces besieged it. A fire then destroyed the keep and other buildings. Saigo is quoted in Wikipedia as saying, "I did not lose to the Meiji government. I lost to Lord Kiyomasa." The present main keep is a concrete reconstruction from 1960, and the castle was designated a Special Historic Site in 1955.

This is the wall in action. The stones start with a gentle slope and then rise toward the vertical, and the keep sits well behind and above the visitors on the path. It was photographed in 2009, before the earthquakes, so some of these walls were later damaged and rebuilt.

The Commons description names two turrets, the Yonken-yagura on the left and the Gennoshin-yagura, both Important Cultural Properties, so I treat this as one of that pair. Look at the dark boards and the small square openings under the white plaster. The image is from 2012, before the earthquakes.

This is a more recent view, from May 2024. The tall stone base and the timber-clad turret above it show the castle's typical profile, and the green fencing and the bus-stop sign at the bottom show that this is a visitor route. The Commons description only says it is a castle tower, so I do not name the turret.

April 2016熊本地震

In April 2016, a series of earthquakes struck Kumamoto, with the strongest reaching seismic intensity 7. All 13 Important Cultural Property structures at the castle were damaged. Most of the roof tiles on the top floor of the keep fell, and roughly a third of the stone walls collapsed. Wikipedia notes that the keep itself survived with little structural damage.

The image most people remember is the Iidamaru five-story turret, whose lower walls collapsed but which kept standing, held up by its corner stones. Repair work began in June 2016, and new roof ornaments (shachihoko) were installed on the keep in 2018.

Here a turret still stands on a narrow corner of stone, with netting over the wall at the right and some plaster missing from its walls, which is consistent with earthquake damage. Commons calls it the Udo turret, and I take that to be the Uto turret. The photo is from December 2021, and the situation may have changed since.

The Commons description for this image mentions the damage from the 2016 earthquake. What you can see are the high stone walls, the keep and a long tiled building above them, and a bare garden of trees below. I cannot tell from the photo which sections were rebuilt. This is from January 2026, six months before the July earthquake.

July 2026: Shaken Again2026年7月

On July 28, 2026, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake (preliminary figure) struck Kumamoto Prefecture, with its epicenter near Uki and a maximum intensity of 7 in Uki and Hikawa, according to GIGAZINE's summary of the JMA announcement. The castle was closed for 16 days. According to the city's castle office, as reported by The Star, stone walls collapsed in 40 locations and structural damage was confirmed in 11 places, including cracked floors in the main keep.

The castle reopened on August 13, 2026, with about 200 people lined up before the gates opened. Officials told The Art Newspaper that the 2052 completion date could be delayed, but that they could not yet estimate by how much. I have not seen a detailed list of what is open, so please check current access before you plan your visit.

A Restoration You Can Walk Through復旧

Nobody can rebuild a stone wall in a few years. The stones are being numbered, stored and put back one by one, using older techniques so that the walls return to their earlier forms. In 2022, the mayor announced that the full restoration would be completed in 2052 instead of 2037, a delay of 15 years. The 2026 earthquake may push that date back again.

Before the 2026 quake, as of early 2025, GLTJP said the main keep, the Kenmotsu turret and a long wall had been restored, while the Honmaru Palace Great Hall, the Uto turret and the Iidamaru turret were still under restoration. The tour route has explanation boards that show photos from before the earthquake next to the present scene, so you see exactly what changed. It is the one castle where I tell guests not to rush: the work in progress is part of the visit.

Autumn colour and repair work in the same frame: the golden ginkgo stands beside the keep, while scaffolding and green fencing show that restoration was still going on in 2020. Commons describes the photo only as a ginkgo and Kumamoto Castle. I include it because it is exactly the kind of view the tour route gives you.

This is the keep as it looked in January 2026. The two fish-shaped roof ornaments in the foreground are shachihoko, the same kind of ornament that was installed on the keep in 2018, and the plaque at the bottom reads “Yomigaeru Kumamoto-jo”, or roughly “Kumamoto Castle Revives”. All of this is before the July 2026 earthquake, so check the current state before you plan around it.

A gold-leaf room with pine painted on the screens, lit from inside and seen through dark pillars. The Commons description names only the castle, so I cannot say which building this is. It is a good reminder that the restoration covers painted interiors as well as stone walls.

Visiting Kumamoto Castleアクセス

  • Getting there: From Kumamoto Station, the streetcar, the Shiromegurin sightseeing loop bus or a local bus all reach the castle area in roughly 20 to 30 minutes, followed by a walk of about 10 minutes. From Aso Kumamoto Airport, an airport limousine bus takes about an hour.
  • Hours: One recent guide lists 9:00 to 17:00, closed December 29.
  • Admission: The same guide lists 800 yen for adults and high school students, and 300 yen for elementary and junior high students. Preschool children are free.
  • What is open: The main keep houses a history museum, with a view over the city from the top floor. Some structures are closed or open only on set days while work continues, for example the Uto turret on the second Sunday of each month, according to GLTJP, and the July 2026 earthquake may have changed this. Please check current access before you go.
  • Seasons: About 800 cherry trees, best from late March to early April, lit at night. Autumn color usually peaks from mid-November to early December.
  • Nearby: The Hosokawa Gyobu-tei residence, a traditional wooden mansion with a Japanese garden, is in nearby Sannomaru Park.

Prices, hours and the open areas change with the work, so please confirm on the official castle site before you go.

Late March to early April is the time for cherry blossoms, and this photo shows them with the turret and the curved wall behind. It was taken in 2011 and is a reminder of the season, not of the current layout, since the walls here have been repaired in the years since.

Making It a Day Trip滞在

Kumamoto works well as a half-day stop on a Kyushu trip, and it is close to the Shinkansen. Give yourself time for the castle grounds and the nearby garden.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How much does Kumamoto Castle cost?
One recent guide lists 800 yen for adults and high school students, and 300 yen for elementary and junior high students, with preschoolers free. Please confirm on the official site, because prices and open areas can change.
Is Kumamoto Castle open after the July 2026 earthquake?
The castle reopened on August 13, 2026, 16 days after the July 28 earthquake. The earthquake collapsed stone walls in 40 places and cracked floors in the main keep, so some areas may be closed or restricted. Please check the official site before your visit.
Is the keep original?
No. The main keep is a concrete reconstruction from 1960. The original burned in 1877. Some other structures are original and were damaged in 2016, and some are rebuilt. Both the 2016 and 2026 earthquakes damaged the site.
When will the restoration be finished?
The target set in 2022 is 2052, delayed from 2037. After the July 2026 earthquake, officials said it could be delayed again but could not yet say by how much.
How long should I spend at Kumamoto Castle?
Plan on about two hours for the keep, the walking route and the grounds. Add time for the nearby Hosokawa Gyobu-tei residence if you want to see it.

A Castle You Can Watch Healむすびに

Kumamoto is not a castle that has been returned to its old state. It is a castle in the middle of its recovery, and that is what makes it worth the walk. Go once now, and go again when the walls are finished.
