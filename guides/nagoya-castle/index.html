Nagoya Castle: Golden Shachi, a Rebuilt Palace and a Keep That Is Closed

名古屋城

Ieyasu's showpiece castle, with its golden shachi, a rebuilt palace and a keep that has been closed since 2018.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I have to start a Nagoya Castle visit with a warning: you cannot go up the main keep. It has been shut since May 2018, and the golden shachi on its roof are for looking at from the ground. That sounds like a reason to skip it. I think it is the opposite, because the castle's best rooms are now at ground level, in a palace that was rebuilt over ten years and opened in full in 2018.

This guide covers how the castle began, the golden shachi, what the 1945 air raid took and what came back, the Honmaru Palace, the walls and gardens, and the practical details, including the new ticket price. Where my sources disagree, I say so.

Ieyasu's Showpiece築城

Wikipedia says Tokugawa Ieyasu decided to build the castle in 1609 and 1610, and that work began in January 1610. It was a national project: about twenty daimyo were ordered to supply labour and stone, including Kato Kiyomasa, Fukushima Masanori and Maeda Toshimitsu. Wikipedia names Nakai Masakiyo as the works commissioner. Many of the stones still carry the marks of the families that supplied them, and the largest block is known as the Kiyomasa Stone.

The keep was finished in December 1612 and the Honmaru Palace in February 1615, with the Ninomaru Palace following in 1617, according to Wikipedia's account of the construction. The castle later belonged to the Owari branch of the Tokugawa family, the line that ruled this region.

After the Meiji Restoration the castle changed hands. Wikipedia says the Imperial Household Ministry took it over in 1893 and renamed it the Nagoya Detached Palace, and that it was given to the city in December 1930. Wikipedia adds that twenty-four structures were designated national treasures that same year, which matters for what happened next.

The Golden Shachi金の鯱

The pair of golden shachi, mythical fish with a tiger's head, is what the whole city associates with the castle. The official site and Wikipedia give the male, on the north end of the roof, as 2.621 meters tall and 1,272 kilograms, using 44.69 kilograms of gold. The female, on the south end, is 2.579 meters and 1,215 kilograms, with 43.39 kilograms of gold.

The earlier pairs did not all survive. Wikipedia says the government took the shachi in 1871, and the pair that stood on the roof was lost when the keep burned in 1945. The current pair went up on the rebuilt keep in 1959. Wikipedia says they were shown at the 2005 Expo and then returned to the roof. For years I have told guests that they can read the castle's whole history in those two animals, since they have been melted down, taken by the state, burned and replaced.

From the foot of the walls the keep rises in stacked tiers of green roofs, with one gold shachi just visible on the top ridge. Visitors gather on the gravel in front, but this was taken while the keep was already closed, so the crowd is here for the outside view and the grounds. The cherry branches on the right are still bare, which fits a late-March date. The stone base flares outward as it climbs. A plain white structure at the right side of the keep looks like a later addition, in keeping with the concrete rebuild, though Commons does not label it.

1945 and the Concrete Keep戦災と再建

Wikipedia says the air raid of 14 May 1945 burned the keep, the Honmaru Palace and other buildings that had been designated national treasures only fifteen years earlier. The site was made a Special Historic Site in 1952. The keep you see now is a steel-reinforced concrete reconstruction that reopened on 3 October 1959, and it is the building that has been closed since 2018.

Here you can count the tiers and see why the keep photographs so well. Green copper roofs step upward, each tier with dark gables, and the barred windows sit in rows on the white walls. The two gold shachi stand at the ends of the top ridge. The lowest roof is covered in grey tiles rather than green copper. A plain pale structure at the lower right is attached to the keep and looks like a later addition, in keeping with the concrete rebuild, and bare branches at the bottom suggest early spring.

GLTJP and Japan-guide say the closure is for seismic safety and age, and that the city plans a wooden reconstruction. GLTJP gives fiscal 2032 as the target, while Japan-guide says it will come after 2032 with no fixed schedule. The official site, as far as I could see, only says that the keep is closed. I would not plan a trip around a reopening date.

The Honmaru Palace本丸御殿

This is the reason to come now. According to GLTJP and Wikipedia, the Honmaru Palace was reconstructed from 2009 to 2018, over about ten years and roughly 15 billion yen, and opened to visitors in full in June 2018. It has more than thirty rooms in the shoin style, the formal residential style of the warrior class. Photography is allowed inside, with no flash or tripod.

This looks like the entrance porch of the Honmaru Palace, though Commons labels it only as Nagoya Castle. The roof sweeps in a deep curve over red-brown timber and white plaster panels, and black beams carry gold fittings with a three-leaf crest that resembles the Tokugawa hollyhock, which Commons does not confirm. Low bamboo-style barriers keep visitors off the stone step, and a stripe of pale pavement leads in. It is a good sign of how fresh the reconstruction is, because the wood has not yet weathered to grey.

I tell visitors to go slowly through the rooms and look at the thresholds between them, because the rank of the people a room was built for shows in how much gold and detail is on its walls. It is not a museum in the usual sense. It feels closer to walking through a building that has just been finished, which in fact it has.

Commons titles this only as an interior of Nagoya Castle, and the colours point to the Honmaru Palace. The view is taken from the threshold, which is how most visitors see the rooms. Black lacquer frames hold gold-ground paintings of crowds, riders and groves of pine, with gold fittings at each joint, and the black ceiling carries more gold ornaments overhead. A second room opens at the right with a blue shoreline painted on its sliding doors. The wall paintings in the reconstructed palace are reproductions, and I am describing only what the photo shows.

This is the plainest and most dramatic room in the set. Three wall panels of gold leaf face you with nothing painted on them, and the light turns the whole room amber. The painted doors do the talking: a snow-covered rock on the left, red maple leaves and slender pines on the right, so two seasons meet in one room. Above, the pale wooden ceiling is divided into square coffers and the bamboo transoms let in a little light.

Here the walls are a seascape. Blue water fills the panels on the right, boats float on it, and a small red torii stands on the pale shore at the far right. To the left, an open sliding door shows the next room, with black lacquer, gold fittings and more painted panels. It gives a good idea of how the rooms connect, and why I tell people to look through each doorway before they step through it. Commons gives the room title only in Japanese; as far as I can tell, the two Katsuragi photos come from different rooms.

This one is different, and I include it because it shows what the palace looked like in the display cases before the reconstruction. It is a model room: tatami, a brazier under a bird-cage cover, two tall candle stands, a bench covered in green silk, and lattice sliding screens with small painted panels below. The photo was taken in 2011, years before the rebuilt palace opened in full.

The signs identify the room as the Taimenjo, with a floor plan on one side and an English text on the other. As I read it, the English sign explains that in the original Honmaru Palace this room lay innermost among the public rooms and served as a reception room for daily and political affairs and informal meetings, and that the panels show seasonal scenes of ordinary people's lives in Kyoto and elsewhere. Please treat it as an exhibit label from 2011, not as the current wording in the palace.

Walls, Turrets and the Ninomaru Garden石垣と庭園

Wikipedia says three of the original corner turrets survive, in the southwest, southeast and northwest, while the northeast turret was lost in the war. The guides I checked describe the surviving ones as normally closed to visitors, so plan to see them from outside, from the moat side.

From the air you can see how the castle sits in the city. The green-roofed keep stands at the center, the moat curves down the right side of the picture, and the towers of central Nagoya fill the far left. The long, low dark roofs at the foot of the keep appear to be the Honmaru Palace, though Commons gives no labels, and a small white turret stands on the stone wall to their left. The trees along the moat show December autumn color, so this is a good picture of the grounds in the last weeks of the season.

The Ninomaru Garden, which Wikipedia says was designated a place of scenic beauty in 1953 and enlarged in 2018, is a quiet counterweight to the palace. GLTJP counts about 1,000 cherry trees of ten varieties across the grounds, and the cherry blossom festival runs from late March to late April.

Pale pink blossoms hang over the path, and the keep rises behind them with its green roofs and the gold shachi at the top against a clear sky. A long stone wall runs along the right, and a few people near the bottom show how large the building is. The photo is from 2016, two years before the keep closed, so the view from the path is what you will still see today, while the inside is not open. GLTJP counts about 1,000 cherry trees on the grounds, so there are plenty of places to frame a shot like this.

The Commons description says this is a gold-plated tea kettle (pure silver body with 24-karat gold plating) shown at the Ninomaru Teahouse, and that in 2017 tea was served with it on Fridays from 10:00 to 16:00. That is a note from 2017, so ask at the castle whether it still runs. The hammered body catches the light in small facets, and the fish-head handles echo the shachi on the keep.

Visiting Nagoya Castleアクセス

  • Getting there: Take the Meijo subway line to Nagoya-jo station and use exit 7, about five minutes on foot (GLTJP). The station was renamed in January 2023 and older maps may still show Shiyakusho. The Meguru tourist loop bus stops here, costs 210 yen per ride or 500 yen for a day, and does not run on Mondays.
  • Tickets: 1,000 yen for adults from 1 October 2026, previously 500 yen, with junior high students and younger free (GLTJP, Japan-guide and Watabi agree). I could not confirm this on the official fee page, so check before you go.
  • Hours: 9:00 to 16:30, with the Honmaru Palace closing at 16:00, and closed from 29 December to 1 January (GLTJP).
  • The keep: Closed to visitors since May 2018. Check the official Nagoya Castle site for the latest.
  • Time to allow: Most of a morning if you take the palace slowly and walk the garden.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

Can I go inside Nagoya Castle's main keep?
No, not at the moment. GLTJP and Japan-guide say the keep has been closed since May 2018 because of earthquake-resistance and ageing concerns. The plan is to rebuild it in wood. GLTJP gives fiscal 2032 as the target for completion, while Japan-guide says only that it will not be before 2032 and that no firm schedule exists, so treat any date as uncertain and check the official site before you travel. You can still walk the grounds and enter the Honmaru Palace.
How much does Nagoya Castle cost and when is it open?
GLTJP, Japan-guide and Watabi agree that admission rose to 1,000 yen for adults from 1 October 2026, up from 500 yen, with junior high students and younger free. GLTJP gives 9:00 to 16:30, with the Honmaru Palace closing a little earlier at 16:00, and closure from 29 December to 1 January. I could not confirm the fee on the official fee page, so check it before you go.
What is there to see if the keep is closed?
The main draw is the Honmaru Palace, reconstructed between 2009 and 2018 in shoin style with more than thirty rooms, and open in full since June 2018. The walls, the moats, the surviving corner turrets seen from outside and the Ninomaru Garden are all on the grounds. Watabi's advice is blunt: if climbing a keep is the point of your trip, Osaka Castle is the better choice for now.
How do I get to Nagoya Castle?
GLTJP says to take the Meijo subway line to Nagoya-jo station and leave by exit 7, about a five-minute walk. The station was renamed in January 2023; older maps may still say Shiyakusho (City Hall). The Meguru tourist loop bus also stops here, costs 210 yen per ride or 500 yen for a day pass, and does not run on Mondays.
When is the best time to visit?
GLTJP counts about 1,000 cherry trees of ten varieties on the grounds and gives late March to late April for the cherry blossom festival, which is when I would go. The palace interior is a good rainy-day plan, since much of it is indoors.

The Keep Is Closed. The Castle Is Not.むすびに

Nagoya Castle is in an in-between moment: the keep waits for its wooden rebuild, while the palace beside it is as complete as it has been in eighty years. Go for the palace, look up at the gold, and come back when the tower reopens.
