Kochi Castle: The Keep and the Palace That Both Survived

高知城

Shikoku's original keep, and the only castle where the lord's palace still stands beside it.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I tell guests that most castles in Japan give you the tower and ask you to imagine the lord's home. Kochi Castle does not. GLTJP says it is the only castle in Japan where both the original keep and the Honmaru Palace survive, and the two stand within a short walk of each other on a hill in the middle of Kochi city, on Shikoku.

This guide covers Yamauchi Kazutoyo and the castle's start, the 1727 fire and the rebuild, the keep, the Kaitokukan palace, the gates and statues, and the practical details of a visit.

Kazutoyo, the Fire and the Rebuild築城と再建

Japan Visitor says Yamauchi Kazutoyo was moved to Tosa from Kakegawa after serving Tokugawa Ieyasu at Sekigahara in 1600. GLTJP says he was the first lord of the Tosa domain and that construction ran from 1601 to 1611. Wikipedia adds that he rebuilt the earlier Otakayama Castle on a larger scale and named it Kawanakayama Castle, later renamed Kochi Castle. I found that naming detail in one source.

The castle did not survive intact in its first form. Wikipedia and Japan Visitor say a fire in 1727 destroyed much of the castle. Japan Visitor says it was rebuilt between 1729 and 1753, that the tower was rebuilt in 1749 to its original design, and that the Otemon gate survived the fire. Wikipedia says the Kaitokukan palace also burned and that its repair was finished in 1749.

After that, Wikipedia says the domain ended in 1871 and the castle escaped the Meiji orders to destroy feudal fortifications, and that it came through the Pacific War intact. It became a National Historic Site in 1959 according to Wikipedia, with the protected area expanded in 2014.

This photo, taken through glass, shows a model of the castle on its hill. The keep stands at the back, a large roofed compound sits below it, and tiers of white stone walls step down the slope. It shows many more roofs than the keep and palace that stand today, but I do not know which period the model shows.

The Keep: The Hawk Castle天守

Japan Experience and Japan Visitor say the curving tiled eaves of the keep earned it the nickname Hawk Castle. Descriptions of its stories differ: GLTJP describes a four-tier exterior with six stories inside, Japan Experience gives six floors at about 18.5 meters, and Wikipedia calls it a five-story keep. I would rely on the six-story count inside, which most of these sources support, and treat the others as different ways of counting.

From below you can count the layers: a large gable on the second roof, a smaller one above it, and the top floor ringed by a dark railed balcony. Small figures stand on that balcony, which gives a sense of scale, and fish-shaped ornaments sit at the roof corners. The curving eaves are what earn the nickname Hawk Castle.

Commons says this was taken from the Sannomaru, the lower enclosure. The keep stands on its stone base behind the pines, and a white-walled building with a tiled gable sits to the right. A low bamboo fence runs along the lawn. I cannot name the building from the photo.

This low angle shows how much of the castle's height is stone. The walls under the keep are made of small, fitted stones, while the wall at the right is rougher and darker. Clipped hedges and cycad palms fill the garden below, and white flowers show on the hedge at the lower left.

GLTJP says the shachihoko roof ornaments were believed to help prevent fire, which fits a castle that had already burned once. Japan Experience mentions spikes set into the north-east wall of the tower, which it calls ninja repellents, and a hidden room for soldiers inside. GLTJP instead places the surviving shinobi-gaeshi in the Kaitokukan, so sources place these spikes differently and I would not repeat either claim more strongly than that.

Japan Experience says the top of the keep gives views of Kochi town on a clear day.

This is the view Japan Experience means by the town laid out below on a clear day. Low-rise city fills the flat ground, a red-and-white tower rises at the right, and hills close the horizon. The trees in the foreground are bare, so it is winter light.

From the top the castle layout becomes clear. A tiled gate roof fills the left, a broad stone stair climbs between walls in the middle, and a flagpole stands on an open gravel court beyond. Pink lanterns hang among the trees at the right, though I cannot tell what they are for. The city and green hills fill the background.

Kaitokukan: The Lord's Palace懐徳館

The Kaitokukan is the Honmaru Palace, and it is the reason Kochi is unusual. GLTJP says it was where the lord received guests, and Wikipedia describes it as a shoin-style palace with eight traditional rooms. GLTJP calls its shinobi-gaeshi, an intruder-prevention feature, the only one of its kind still standing in Japan. The same source says admission to the palace is included with the keep ticket.

Gates, Walls and Statues城門と石垣

Sources give the Otemon different ages. GLTJP says it was built in 1801 and has an ishi-otoshi on its second floor for dropping stones or boiling water, Japan Visitor says it survived the 1727 fire, and Japan Experience calls it the oldest surviving part of the castle. I cannot reconcile those, so please treat the date as uncertain. Japan Visitor says a square enclosure in front of the gate, a masugata, lets defenders fire on intruders from three sides.

The Otemon is the main gate. The upper floor is boarded in black with shuttered windows, a white gable rises at one end, and the base is built from large pale stones. The keep shows small in the distance above the trees, which reminds you that you still have a climb ahead. The wide open ground in front fits Japan Visitor's description of a square enclosure, but I cannot confirm that from the photo.

Inside, the gate is massive. The pillars are heavy timbers studded with iron, and the doors are thick, with a black-boarded upper floor above. Two visitors walk through, which shows the scale. It looks like the same gate as the photo above, but Commons titles it only as Kochi Castle.

GLTJP says the black Tsume Gate, also called the Karakuri Gate, connects the Honmaru Palace and the Ninomaru, and that its corridor served as offices for senior retainers. Japan Experience calls it a two-storey black-painted gate linking the inner and outer citadels.

The Tsume-mon is a dark two-storey building, black boards below and white plaster with barred windows above, set between two stone walls. A sign in front says it is a nationally designated important cultural property. GLTJP says this gate links the Honmaru Palace and the Ninomaru, and that its corridor served as offices for senior retainers.

Broad, worn stone steps run between mossy walls, with visitors climbing in single file. Commons does not say which flight this is. It is what the approach feels like: steady steps up through shade and stone before you reach the keep.

There are statues too. GLTJP says a bronze statue of Yamauchi Kazutoyo stands 4.32 meters tall on a 5.08-meter pedestal, unveiled on 20 September 1996, the anniversary of his death. Japan Visitor says a statue of his wife Chiyo stands in the grounds. Japan Experience says a statue of Itagaki Taisuke, a founder of the Meiji-era People's Rights Movement and a native of the area, stands just inside the main entrance.

Kazutoyo rides in armor with a spear held out in front, on a granite pedestal with a bronze plate that I read as his name. GLTJP gives the current bronze statue as 4.32 meters tall, unveiled on 20 September 1996. Commons notes a sculptor, Motoyama Hakun, for an earlier statue dated 1912, so I cannot say which version this photo shows.

Visiting Kochi Castleアクセス

  • Getting there: GLTJP says about 15 minutes by Tosaden Kotsu bus to Kochijo-mae, or about 15 minutes by tram with a transfer at Harimayabashi, and about 30 minutes on foot from JR Kochi Station.
  • Tickets: 420 yen for visitors 18 and over, under-18s free (GLTJP). Japan Experience and Japan Visitor give 400 yen, so please confirm at the gate. The ticket covers the keep, the Kaitokukan, and neighbouring gates (GLTJP).
  • Hours: 9:00 to 17:00, last admission 16:30, closed 26 December to 1 January, with possible extensions during events (GLTJP).
  • Sunday market: Japan Experience says the main entrance is at the end of Otesuji, the street that holds the Sunday market.
  • Cherry blossoms: about 225 trees reach full bloom in late March and are illuminated at night (GLTJP).

This is the market Japan Experience mentions on the street leading to the main entrance. Stalls under green tarpaulins run down both sides of a street lined with palms, and shoppers fill the middle. It is a crowded, ordinary market morning, so go early if you want to move easily.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How much does Kochi Castle cost and when is it open?
GLTJP lists 420 yen for visitors 18 and over, with under-18s free, for the keep, the Kaitokukan palace and the neighbouring gates. Japan Experience and Japan Visitor give 400 yen, so I would confirm at the gate. GLTJP gives 9:00 to 17:00 with last admission at 16:30, closed 26 December to 1 January, and says hours may be extended during events.
Why is Kochi Castle special?
GLTJP says it is the only castle in Japan where both the original keep and the Honmaru Palace survive, and that it is one of 12 castles with an original Edo-period keep. It also says 15 buildings on the grounds are Important Cultural Properties.
Is the keep original?
Yes. Japan Visitor says the castle burned down in 1727 and was rebuilt between 1729 and 1753, with the tower rebuilt in 1749 to its original design. So the keep you climb is the 18th-century rebuild, not the 1601 to 1611 original, but it is still an original wooden keep.
How do I get there from Kochi Station?
GLTJP gives about 15 minutes by Tosaden Kotsu bus to Kochijo-mae, or about 15 minutes by tram with a transfer at Harimayabashi, and about 30 minutes on foot from JR Kochi Station. Japan Experience says the main entrance is at the end of Otesuji, the street that holds the Sunday market.
When are the cherry blossoms?
GLTJP says about 225 cherry trees in the park reach full bloom in late March and are illuminated at night. Bloom timing varies by year, so check a local report before planning around it.

One Castle, Both Halvesむすびに

Kochi is the castle I send people to when they ask what a lord's life looked like, because the answer is still standing a short walk from the tower. Climb the keep for the view, then spend the same time in the palace.
