Maruoka Castle: A Stone Roof, a Steep Stair and a Keep Rebuilt After 1948

丸岡城

Fukui's small original keep, its 120-ton stone roof, and how it was put back together.

WRITTEN BY SACRED JAPAN ／ LICENSED NATIONAL TOUR GUIDE (ENGLISH)

As a licensed guide, I send guests to Maruoka Castle for one detail they cannot see anywhere else: the roof. It is covered in stone tiles, about 6,000 of them, and Nippon.com puts the total at about 120 tons. Under it sits a small three-story keep in Sakai, Fukui, one of the 12 original keeps, and one that fell down in 1948 and was put back together.

This guide covers who built it and when, the dating debate, the family behind the famous short letter, the stone roof and steep stairs, the earthquake rebuild, two local legends, and the practical details for a visit.

1576, or Closer to 1630?築城年の謎

The traditional story starts in 1576. Wikipedia and GLTJP say Shibata Katsutoyo, the nephew and adopted son of Shibata Katsuie, built the castle in that year on the orders of Oda Nobunaga. Wikipedia says Katsutoyo held it until 1583, when he died during the Battle of Shizugatake.

The keep itself is a different question. japan-guide says the keep was once thought to be the oldest in Japan, but that recent studies date it to the early 1600s. Nippon.com says recent research shows it was built in the Kan'ei era (1624 to 1644) when Honda Narishige became lord. Wikipedia says a 2019 survey dated most of the through-pillars to after 1626, and that the style may point to heavy reconstruction around 1628 under the Honda. Fun! Japan adds that some argue Inuyama's keep, completed in 1617, is the oldest, which is why we have a guide to Inuyama Castle.

Wikipedia traces the later owners. The Aoyama clan lost the domain after Sekigahara, Honda Narishige received it in 1613, and the Arima family ruled for eight generations until the domain system ended in 1871. It also says most buildings were demolished after the Meiji Restoration and the keep and grounds were bought by the town in 1901.

The Lord Whose Father Wrote a Short Letter一筆啓上

Honda Narishige's father, Honda Shigetsugu (1529 to 1596), is remembered for a letter. Markus Sesko writes that he sent it to his wife from camp at the Battle of Nagashino in 1575, and gives it as: be careful about fire, do not let O-Sen lose weight, and feed the horses well. A page on the 2006 Maruoka Castle stamp gives a different reading of the middle line, "Don't make Sen cry". Translations of such short lines vary.

Sesko says Sen was their son Senchiyo, later Narishige. That is the link to Maruoka: Wikipedia says Narishige received the Maruoka domain in 1613. GLTJP says the castle ticket also covers a museum about the short letter, so the story is part of the visit.

The Keep: Stone Roof, Steep Stairs天守

Nippon.com describes a free-standing, watchtower-style keep of two layers and three floors with a large gabled roof, and says it is a national important cultural property. GLTJP gives its height as about 12 meters. Wikipedia says the keep stands on a stone platform of 6.2 meters, and that the stone base is the old rough-laid kind called nozura-zumi, which drains well.

Seen from below, the keep looks even smaller than its 12 meters (GLTJP). The top floor has a railed lookout all the way round, which is what "watchtower style" means in practice. Below it the first floor is a white plaster band over dark wooden boards. At the lower left is a sign reading 丸岡城, and the trees are in fresh early-May leaf.

The roof is the main attraction. Nippon.com says it is made of stone tiles, each 20 to 60 kg, about 6,000 in all, and about 120 tons for the whole roof, built to resist heavy snow. Wikipedia says its sources give 60 or 120 tons, and that the tiles come from local stone, so I say "about 120 tons" and note the disagreement. Fun! Japan calls the stone roof unique among the surviving keeps.

From the foot of the keep you can read its parts. A broad gabled roof sits over the entrance, the ground story is boarded in dark wood with small square openings, and the whole building stands on rough, unshaped stones, the nozura-zumi style Wikipedia describes. The small hooded window at the lower right looks like the kind of stone-drop window Wikipedia mentions, but I have not confirmed which window it is. The roof looks gray-green here; I cannot tell stone from clay tile in a photo, so I rely on the sources for the stone tiles.

Inside, japan-guide says the keep is mostly unfurnished, with a few information displays and a diorama on the lowest floor. The stairs are the real event. GLTJP says they are very steep, at a maximum angle of 67 degrees, and japan-guide says ropes hang from the ceilings to help you climb. GLTJP says the top floor looks west over the Sakai Plain and the Mikuni hills.

Inside, japan-guide says the keep is mostly unfurnished, and this photo agrees: a bare plank floor, thick squared posts and beams, and small windows with wooden shutters and a railing outside. Two small signs on stands ask visitors to take care. Commons does not say which floor this is, so I do not name one.

This is the stair I warn guests about. It is almost a ladder: narrow treads, a very steep rise, and a knotted white rope hung from above for you to hold. The signs on it ask visitors to take great care going up and down, and a stand beside it asks people to keep to the left. GLTJP gives the maximum angle as 67 degrees.

1948: The Keep Falls and Is Put Back福井地震

Wikipedia says the 1948 Fukui earthquake destroyed the stone base and the keep collapsed. Nippon.com says it was reassembled at the strong request of citizens, reusing about 70% of the parts to restore its 17th-century form. Wikipedia says that was completed in 1955 with a reinforced-concrete frame, reusing about 70% of the pillars and 60% of the beams, and that the top-floor windows were changed from sliding doors to push-up windows. It also says the keep was a National Treasure from 1934 and was re-designated an Important Cultural Property in 1950.

So I describe it honestly: an original keep, rebuilt from its own timbers, with modern parts in the base.

Mist and a Sacrifice: Two Legends伝説

The castle is also called Kasumigajo, the Mist Castle. Fun! Japan says the legend is that a giant snake breathed out a cloud of mist to hide the castle from attackers, and japan-guide says the mist hid the castle from approaching enemies.

Wikipedia tells a darker tale. During construction the stone base kept collapsing, a vassal suggested a human sacrifice, and a poor one-eyed mother of two named O-shizu volunteered if one of her children would be made a samurai. The promise was not kept, and her spirit is said to make the moat overflow with rain each April, which is why a small tomb was built to soothe her. I could only find this in one source, so I tell it as a legend and not as history.

The tall stone at the left is carved お静慰霊碑, a memorial to O-shizu. Beside it a smaller stone stands under a cap of snow. Commons does not say what that smaller stone is, so I do not call it her tomb, though Wikipedia says a small tomb was built to soothe her. The pine and shrub are wrapped in the rope supports used in snowy regions, and the town and mountains stretch out behind. It is a reminder that Fukui gets deep winter snow, the same snow the stone roof was built to bear.

Visiting Maruoka Castleアクセス

  • Getting there: japan-guide gives a bus from Fukui Station to Maruoka-jo of about 50 to 60 minutes and 670 yen, roughly hourly, then a short walk. About 30 minutes by car, with free parking next to the castle.
  • Tickets: 450 yen for adults and 150 yen for elementary and junior high students (GLTJP). Credit cards are not accepted. The ticket covers the keep and the small museum.
  • Hours: 8:30 to 17:00, last entry 16:30, no closing days (GLTJP and japan-guide).
  • Cherry blossoms: about 400 cherry trees. The festival runs about the first three weeks of April, with over 300 paper lanterns lit in the evenings (japan-guide). Fun! Japan gives April 1 to 20.
  • Old Castle Festival: Fun! Japan says it is held in October, on the second Sunday and the day before, with a historical procession and a parade of karakuri floats.
  • Phone: 0776-66-0303 (GLTJP).

The photo below shows the cherry-blossom season that the festival is built around.

In mid-April the keep rises out of the blossoms, with red-and-white paper lanterns hanging among the trees. This matches japan-guide's description of the evening lantern lights during the festival. The photo is in soft evening light, which is why it looks so pale and quiet.

Making It a Trip滞在

japan-guide lists Eiheiji Temple, Tojinbo, Echizen Ono Castle and the Ichijodani ruins among the sights around Fukui, so Maruoka fits a day or two in the prefecture.

Frequently Asked Questionsよくあるご質問

How much does Maruoka Castle cost and when is it open?
GLTJP and japan-guide list 450 yen for adults and GLTJP lists 150 yen for elementary and junior high students. Both give 8:30 to 17:00 with last entry at 16:30, and japan-guide says there are no closing days. GLTJP says credit cards are not accepted. Please check the official site for current prices and hours.
Is Maruoka Castle really the oldest keep in Japan?
It was long called that, and 1576 is the traditional date. japan-guide says recent studies date the keep to the early 1600s, Nippon.com says it was built in the Kan'ei era (1624 to 1644) under Honda Narishige, and Wikipedia says a 2019 survey dated most of the through-pillars to after 1626. Fun! Japan notes some argue Inuyama is older. I say it is one of the 12 original keeps and leave the title aside.
Why is the roof made of stone?
Nippon.com says the stone tiles were used to withstand heavy snow, with about 6,000 tiles of 20 to 60 kg each and about 120 tons in total. Wikipedia gives the roof weight as 60 or 120 tons depending on the source, so I say about 120 tons per Nippon.com and GLTJP.
What happened in the 1948 earthquake?
Wikipedia says the Fukui earthquake of 1948 destroyed the stone base and the keep collapsed. Nippon.com says it was reassembled at the strong request of citizens using about 70% of the original parts, and Wikipedia says the work was finished in 1955 on a reinforced-concrete base.
How do I get to Maruoka Castle?
japan-guide says a bus from Fukui Station bound for Maruoka-jo takes about 50 to 60 minutes and costs 670 yen, with a short walk from the stop, and that driving takes about 30 minutes with free parking. Fun! Japan gives about 50 minutes by Keifuku bus and a one-minute walk. GLTJP also mentions a local bus from JR Ashihara Onsen Station. Please check current timetables.

A Small Keep Worth the Detourむすびに

Maruoka is not the biggest or the most famous castle. It is the one where you stand under a roof of stone and climb stairs steep enough to need a rope, in a keep that was reassembled from its own timbers.
