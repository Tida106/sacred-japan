# Heian Shrine: The Ancient Capital That Built Itself a Memory

**Meta description:** Heian Shrine looks like it has stood in Kyoto for a thousand years. It was built in 1895. That gap between appearance and reality is exactly the point — and understanding it tells you more about Kyoto than almost any older building can.

---

Heian Shrine opened on March 15, 1895. It was inaugurated as the centerpiece of an industrial exhibition fair marking the 1,100th anniversary of Kyoto's founding as Japan's imperial capital. The buildings are a replica — built at five-eighths scale because the city could not acquire enough land to reconstruct the original at full size — of the Chōdōin, the great ceremonial hall of the Heian-period imperial palace, a building that no longer exists. The shrine enshrines two emperors: the first emperor to rule from Kyoto, and the last. It was designed by an architectural historian. It was built to look ancient.

All of this is true, and none of it diminishes the shrine. Understanding why Kyoto built this particular building, at this particular moment, for these particular emperors, tells you something about the city that older and more genuinely historical sites cannot.

## What Kyoto Had Just Lost

In 1869, the Emperor Meiji moved the imperial court from Kyoto to Tokyo. For more than a thousand years, Kyoto had been the capital of Japan — the seat of the emperor, the center of Japanese culture, the city whose name meant, quite literally, "the capital." Overnight, it was no longer any of those things. The population declined. Businesses followed the court to Tokyo. The city that had spent eleven centuries as the cultural and political center of Japan found itself, in the space of a few years, a provincial city with a great deal of history and diminishing relevance.

The founding of Heian Shrine was Kyoto's civic response. <cite index="9-1">The background to its founding is related to the reconstruction of Kyoto, which was devastated by war at the end of the Edo period and moved the capital to Tokyo during the Meiji Restoration. The people of Kyoto united their enthusiasm and the passion of people across the nation to work towards reviving Kyoto.</cite> The shrine was not a spontaneous act of religious devotion. It was a deliberate act of civic identity — a statement that Kyoto's significance was not contingent on housing the emperor, because Kyoto's significance predated and would outlast any particular political arrangement. The city commissioned a replica of its own lost imperial palace and turned it into a shrine for the emperors who had defined its history. It was, in effect, building a monument to itself.

## The First Emperor and the Last

<cite index="6-1">The shrine was built on the occasion of the 1100th anniversary of the capital's foundation in Kyoto and is dedicated to the spirits of the first and last emperors who reigned from the city, Emperor Kammu (737–806) and Emperor Komei (1831–1867).</cite>

Emperor Kammu moved the capital to Heian-kyō in 794, choosing the site for reasons both practical and cosmological. The location was surrounded on three sides by mountains and open to the south — the classical Chinese geomantic ideal for a capital city. It was also, importantly, away from Nara, whose powerful Buddhist clergy had become entangled in imperial politics to a degree that Kammu found threatening. The new capital was to be a fresh start: a planned city on a Chinese grid, with a clear separation between imperial governance and Buddhist institutional power. The Heian period that followed his reign — nearly four centuries of relative peace and extraordinary cultural flowering, during which The Tale of Genji was written, the distinctive Japanese aesthetic of mono no aware was first articulated, and the courtly culture that still inflects Japanese aesthetics today was formed — bears his name.

Emperor Komei, the last emperor to actually reign from Kyoto, was added to the shrine's deities in 1940. He died in 1867, one year before his son, the Meiji Emperor, moved the court to Tokyo. <cite index="9-1">Emperor Komei is said to have laid the foundation for the Meiji Restoration, and the enshrinement of his spirit at Heian Shrine further enhanced its status as a cultural symbol of Kyoto.</cite> Together the two emperors bracket the entire span of Kyoto's imperial history: Kammu who created it, Komei who closed it.

## The Architecture

<cite index="8-1">The architecture design was a reproduction of the Chōdōin (Emperor's palace in the former eras) in 5/8th scale. The large red entrance gate is a reproduction of the Outenmon of the Chōdōin. The architecture of the main palace mirrors the style and features of the Kyoto Imperial Palace, the style from the 11th–12th century (late Heian Period).</cite>

The five-eighths scale is not visible to the eye — you would need to have seen the original Heian palace and remember its dimensions to notice. What is immediately apparent is the color: the entire complex is vermilion and white, a palette that was characteristic of Heian-period court architecture and that distinguishes the shrine visually from the greens and browns of most older Kyoto temples and shrines. <cite index="12-1">The torii gate leading up to the Heian Shrine is actually one of the largest in the country, towering overhead at a height of about 24 meters. Drawing visitors in through Okazaki Park to the shrine's impressively vermilion Ōtenmon Gate, this torii was the largest in the country when it was built, and remains the tallest in Kyoto.</cite>

<cite index="12-1">Owing in part to its Chinese style, the influence of the god-beasts of the four cardinal directions — Suzaku the red phoenix (South), Byakko the white tiger (West), Seiryū the blue dragon (East), and Genbu the black snake-turtle (North) — abound throughout the grounds of the shrine.</cite> These four guardian spirits are carved into bronze lanterns, built into the corridor decorations, and encoded in the names of the shrine's two towers: Sōryūrō (Blue Dragon Tower) and Byakkorō (White Tiger Tower). Walking the inner precinct with this in mind turns the architecture into a cosmological map.

In 1976, <cite index="8-1">part of the shrine caught on fire, and nine of the buildings, including the honden, or main sanctuary, burned down.</cite> The current buildings are reconstructions dating from shortly after the fire, rebuilt through public fundraising. The shrine that visitors see today is, strictly speaking, a reconstruction of a replica of a building that no longer exists — three removes from the Heian-period original. This does not seem to reduce its power as a place.

## The Garden

Behind the main shrine buildings, <cite index="8-1">the Japanese-style garden takes up about half the land area (approximately 33,060 square meters). Renowned gardener Ogawa Jihei VII, known as Ueji, created the garden over more than twenty years. The water used in the ponds comes from the Lake Biwa Canal.</cite>

The garden is divided into four sections — South, West, Central, and East — each designed to be at its best in a different season. The South Garden's weeping cherry trees (shidare-zakura) make it one of the most celebrated hanami spots in Kyoto during late March and early April, when the trees' drooping branches brush the surface of the ponds. The Central and East gardens shift to irises in May and early June. Autumn brings maple color throughout. <cite index="8-1">Species otherwise rare in Japan, such as the striped bitterling, the yellow pond turtle, and the Japanese pond turtle live in and around the ponds.</cite>

The garden admission (¥600 for adults) is separate from the shrine grounds, which are free. The garden is worth the fee in any season and is particularly worth it during cherry blossom season, when the combination of weeping cherries, still water, and the vermilion bridge visible across the Central pond produces the kind of image that requires no context to understand.

## The Jidai Matsuri

<cite index="8-1">On October 22, Heian-jingū hosts the Jidai Matsuri, which is one of the most important festivals of Kyoto. The procession of this festival begins at the old Imperial Palace, and includes carrying the mikoshi (portable shrines) of Emperors Kanmu and Kōmei to the Heian Shrine.</cite>

The Festival of Ages — the name refers to the historical periods represented in the procession's costumes — is one of Kyoto's three major festivals, alongside the Aoi Matsuri in May and the Gion Matsuri in July. Roughly 2,000 participants march in costumes representing every major period of Japanese history from the Meiji era back to the Heian period, in reverse chronological order, so that the procession moves from the near past toward the more distant past as it progresses. The route ends at Heian Shrine with the arrival of the two emperors' portable shrines, completing the journey that the shrine itself was built to symbolize: the return of Kyoto's imperial history to Kyoto.

## Visiting Today

Heian Shrine is located in the Okazaki area of Kyoto, a ten-minute walk from Higashiyama Station on the Kyoto Municipal Subway or about 30 minutes by city bus from Kyoto Station. The shrine grounds are free and open from 6:00am to 6:00pm (hours vary seasonally). The garden (¥600) opens at 8:30am. The Okazaki area immediately surrounding the shrine includes the Kyoto Municipal Museum of Art, the National Museum of Modern Art Kyoto, the Rohm Theatre Kyoto, and the Kyoto City Zoo — making it a natural full-day destination when combined with the shrine and garden.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How old is Heian Shrine?**
Heian Shrine was founded in 1895, making it one of the newer major shrines in Kyoto. It was built for the 1,100th anniversary of Kyoto's founding as Japan's imperial capital and is a five-eighths scale replica of the Chōdōin, the ceremonial hall of the original Heian-period imperial palace.

**Who is enshrined at Heian Shrine?**
Two emperors: Emperor Kammu (737–806), who moved the imperial capital to Kyoto in 794, and Emperor Komei (1831–1867), the last emperor to reign from Kyoto before the capital was moved to Tokyo in 1869. Komei was added to the shrine in 1940.

**Why was Heian Shrine built?**
As a civic monument to Kyoto's imperial heritage, built after the Meiji Restoration moved the imperial court to Tokyo. The shrine was the centerpiece of an 1895 industrial exhibition marking the 1,100th anniversary of Kyoto's founding, designed to reassert the city's cultural significance after the loss of its status as capital.

**Is Heian Shrine's garden worth visiting?**
Yes. The Shin'en garden (¥600 admission, separate from the free shrine grounds) is divided into four sections designed for different seasons: weeping cherry blossoms in spring, irises in early summer, maple color in autumn, and snow-covered pines in winter. It is one of Kyoto's most celebrated cherry blossom viewing spots.

**What is the Jidai Matsuri?**
The Festival of Ages, held annually on October 22 at Heian Shrine. Roughly 2,000 participants march in period costumes representing every era of Japanese history from Meiji back to Heian, beginning at the Kyoto Imperial Palace and ending at Heian Shrine. It is one of Kyoto's three major annual festivals.

**How do I get to Heian Shrine from Kyoto Station?**
Take the Kyoto Municipal Subway Karasuma Line to Karasuma Oike, transfer to the Tozai Line, and exit at Higashiyama Station (approximately 20 minutes total). The shrine is a ten-minute walk northeast of the station. Alternatively, city bus number 5 from Kyoto Station reaches the area in about 30 minutes.
